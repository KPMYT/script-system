One cup of plain white vinegar, poured into your furnace's drain line about once a month, keeps slime from choking it all winter. And it's not bleach. Does your furnace have two white plastic pipes going out through the wall or the roof? Then it makes water every time it runs, and that water needs a way out. When your line clogs, the furnace shuts down, often on the coldest nights. Clearing it can run you $100 to $250, and nothing in your furnace is broken. The cup costs pocket change. It won't open a line that's packed solid, but it keeps yours from getting there. Eleven steps, start to finish.

Step one. Make sure your furnace has this drain at all. Go stand in front of it with a flashlight and follow the pipes that carry the exhaust away. Two white plastic pipes, the kind called PVC, heading out through a wall or the roof, mean you've got a high-efficiency furnace. Its efficiency rating will read 90 percent or higher. These are called condensing furnaces. Yours pulls so much heat out of the exhaust that it cools down and turns to water, a bit like a cold glass sweating in July. On a cold day your furnace can make several gallons of that water. Running hard, it can reach about eight to ten gallons, and it's slightly acidic. Every drop of it has to leave your house somehow. But plenty of houses have a standard 80 percent furnace instead, with a metal flue pipe running up into a chimney. That furnace doesn't make this water, and it has no condensate drain of its own. If you spot a drain hose near one, it usually belongs to the air conditioner's coil. That's a summer job, and it's not this one. So which kind is in your house? Two white pipes, keep going. A metal flue, you can let this one go and sleep easy. I'd settle that before you buy a single jug, because there's no sense treating a drain you don't have.

Step two. Follow the water and find the right opening. Low on your furnace there's a plastic trap, a fitting built to always hold a little water. From the trap, a hose or a pipe runs off to a floor drain, or to a condensate pump when there's no drain close by. That whole run is your condensate line, the furnace's way of getting its water out of your house. Trace it with your flashlight. You're hunting for one of two openings along it. The first is a cleanout, a short stub of pipe with a cap on it. The second is a vent tee, a T-shaped fitting with one end open to the air. Either one is where the cup goes. You'd think any opening down there would do, but there's a pair of small rubber hoses near the trap that you never touch. They run to the pressure switch. That's a safety switch that only lets the burners light once it senses the inducer drawing the exhaust out. The inducer is the small fan that pushes that exhaust out the white pipes. You never pour vinegar, water or anything else into those hoses or into the inducer, and you never bypass that switch. Can't tell which opening is which? Don't guess. Ask the tech at your next visit to show you, and I'd make a note of it once you know. And if your line has neither a cleanout nor a vent tee, the trap itself comes off, the way your furnace manual shows. Usually that's a couple of hose clamps.

Step three. Know what that slime does once it gets going. Your trap and your line sit full of still, warm water all season, and dust finds its way in. Over a winter that turns into slime, a slick film of growth called biofilm that coats the inside of the trap and the hose. It builds a little at a time. Then one day the water can't get past it, and it backs up toward your furnace. When that happens, the pressure switch can't close, so the burners won't light. Your furnace shuts down. Or it short cycles, which means it starts, runs a minute, quits, and tries again, over and over. Some furnaces just flash an error code at you. And sometimes the trap simply overflows onto the floor. Ever had your furnace click on, hum, and give up before the warm air came? This can be exactly that. It sounds like the end of the furnace, and the first fear for a lot of folks is a quote for a whole new one. But nothing's broken, you've got a plugged drain. The visit to clear it is still a full service call. The cup of vinegar that keeps it clear is never on the invoice. I'd much rather you hear about it from me now than find out on a cold night with the house cooling down.

Step four. Leave the bleach on the shelf. To be fair to bleach, it does kill slime. That's why it's the jug a lot of folks reach for first. But Carrier and Lennox both recommend white vinegar over bleach for condensate lines. Carrier says harsh chemicals can damage system components. And furnace manuals usually call for plain water in the trap, nothing stronger. Think about what that water runs through. A plastic trap, the hoses, and sometimes a pump. Vinegar is a mild acid. It loosens that biofilm, and it's gentle on all of those parts. I've been in this trade 45 years, and vinegar is what I'd reach for every time. Here's the trade-off, though. Vinegar acts slower than bleach. One cup won't strip a dirty line in a single go. It works as a habit, a cup at a time, and that's how it keeps the line clear all winter. And this next part is about you, not the furnace. Never mix bleach and vinegar. Together they give off chlorine gas, and that's toxic. If bleach has gone down that line before, flush it well with plain water first, before the first cup of vinegar ever goes in. But nothing goes in at all until the power's off.

Step five. Shut it down before you open anything. Turn your thermostat down so the furnace stops calling for heat, and wait until the blower stops. Then find your furnace's power switch. It looks like an ordinary light switch, on the furnace or on the wall close by, and you flip it off. If your cleanout or vent tee sits outside the cabinet, that's all you need. But if the cabinet door has to come off to reach the trap, prove the power is really off first. A non-contact voltage tester does that. It's a pen-shaped tool that beeps or lights up near a wire with power in it, and they run about $15 to $25. Try it first on an outlet or a lamp you know is working, so you know the tester works. Then hold it to the cable feeding the furnace. If that cable's wrapped in metal and the tester can't read through it, turn the thermostat up for a minute. No hum, no click, and the little light on the control board stays dark. Then you can take the door off. Is that a lot of fuss for a cup of vinegar? Maybe. But a furnace cabinet has wiring inside that can hurt you, and I'd never open one on a guess.

Step six. Pour the cup and let it sit. Measure out about one cup of plain white vinegar. Open the cleanout cap, or find the open end of the vent tee, and pour it in slowly. Slow matters. You want it running down through the trap and the line, not splashing back out at you. Then you leave it alone for 30 minutes. That's the vinegar's time to soften the slime. You might be tempted to dump in half the jug, but the dose is one cup, and the habit does the rest. While you're waiting, keep an eye on the opening you poured into. The vinegar should sink away and drain. If it just sits there, filling the cleanout and going nowhere, that line is already blocked, and step ten is your next move. That's not a failure on your part. It means the slime got a head start on you. I'd set a kitchen timer for the half hour, so the flush doesn't slip your mind.

Step seven. Flush it and watch where it comes out. After the 30 minutes, you pour a cup or two of warm tap water into the same opening. Warm, not boiling. The trap and the hoses are plastic, and boiling water is hard on plastic. Then go to the other end of your line, at the floor drain or wherever the hose ends. What you want to see is a steady flow. It may come out cloudy, with little bits of slime in it, and that's a good sign. That's the stuff that was coating your line, and it's on its way out. A trickle that stops and starts, or nothing at all, means it's still partly blocked. What you don't do is put a garden hose on it, or blow it out with compressed air. You'd think more push means a cleaner line, but that line is plastic and held together with clamps, and it isn't built for that kind of force. I'd give it the full cup or two, so the whole line gets a rinse. Can you hear the water running free into that drain? That's what clear sounds like, and it cost you a cup out of the pantry. But if your hose ends at a pump, that pump wants a look too.

Step eight. If the water goes to a pump, check the pump. No floor drain close to the furnace? Then your water probably runs into a condensate pump. It's a small tank with a motor on top, sitting near the furnace. As the tank fills, a float rises and starts the motor, and a thin tube carries the water away. But the same slime that coats your line rides along into that tank. It can gum up the float so it sticks. Many of these pumps have a safety float switch that shuts the furnace off before the tank overflows. So you can end up with no heat, and the furnace isn't at fault at all. The good news is your vinegar flush runs through the pump too. While the water goes in, watch it. The pump should kick on and empty its tank. If the motor runs but the tank won't empty, or the tank's full and the motor never starts, the pump needs replacing. That's a call. Put in by a tech, a new one runs about $150 to $450. I think that's a stiff price for a little plastic box, but it beats a wet floor. Never bypass the float switch. It's there to keep that water where it belongs.

Step nine. Fill the trap with water before the power goes back on. This is the step I won't let you rush. You might think an empty trap is a clean trap, but the water sitting in it is a seal. If your trap runs dry, exhaust gas can push out through the drain and into your house, and the furnace may not light at all. Furnace makers say to fill the trap before start-up. If you went in through the cleanout or the vent tee, finish with plain water, so the trap is full of water when you're done. If you took the trap off and emptied it, refill it with plain water before you put it back on. Then put the cabinet door back, flip the power switch on, and set the thermostat back where you like it. Now you listen to it start. First the inducer fan hums. Then the ignition, and then the burners light. A little after that, warm air comes out of your vents. Go back and look at the floor around your furnace. Dry floor, warm air, and the inducer humming along. That's a furnace with a clear drain, and you didn't pay anybody to tell you so.

Step ten. When one cup isn't enough. The vinegar is prevention. It won't clear a line that's already packed, and you'll know, because the cup just sits there and won't drain away. So here's the next rung, and you can handle this one too. Power off at the switch, and proved off with the tester, the same as before. Take the trap off the way your manual shows, usually by loosening the hose clamps. Rinse it out with warm water and a little vinegar. Then fill it with plain water and put it back. But a few things mean you stop and call a tech you trust. A cracked trap is one. A hose that sags so the water can't run downhill is another. If the line runs clear and the error code is still showing, that's a call too. So is water on the floor when you can't find where it came from. There's no shame in any of those, and I'd rather you make that call early than mop up after it. Leave a leak like that under a basement furnace, and it soaks into the flooring, the drywall and whatever you've got stored down there. A typical water damage job runs about $1,300 to $6,000. That's a gut punch over a little plastic hose.

Step eleven. Make it a habit, because the habit is what keeps it clear all winter. One cup won't last the whole season, and I won't pretend it does. Biofilm takes weeks to build back up in your line. So your cup goes in once a month, or every other month, right through the heating season. Miss a month and you're fine. The slime doesn't come back overnight. On the first of the month I pull my furnace filter and look at it, with my coffee in the other hand. If you've got one of these furnaces, that's a good morning for the cup of vinegar too. Now the money. A gallon of white vinegar holds 16 cups, so each cup works out to about 25 to 30 cents. That's pennies and a few minutes a month, against a no-heat call in the middle of January. Nobody mails you a reminder card for a cup of vinegar. But now you don't need one. For tonight, just one thing. Go find that drain line and its cleanout or vent tee, so your first cup has somewhere to go.

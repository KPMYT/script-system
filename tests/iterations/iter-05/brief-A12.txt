TITLE: The REAL Reason Your Plug Won't Stay In The Outlet

REFERENCE (judge only): "Plug Won't Stay In Outlet" · 2,539,124 (Everyday Home Repairs, 2020-12-30) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0h_r2bD5bMs

BRIEF:
A plug that won't stay in usually means the receptacle's contacts are worn out. Each slot holds a spring-formed brass contact that grips the plug blade; that grip is what keeps the plug in and what makes a tight, low-resistance electrical connection. Thousands of insertions, cords tugged at an angle and heat from high-draw loads such as vacuums, space heaters and hair dryers fatigue the metal until the contacts spread apart and no longer grip. Two-prong plugs fall out first because they have no ground pin to brace them, which is why worn outlets in hotels, kitchens and bathrooms are the familiar ones. Bent or thin plug blades, common on cheap chargers, cause the same symptom, so try the plug in another outlet first. The real problem is that the loose fit is a warning. A loose contact touches the blade over a smaller area, so resistance rises and the contact heats under load; CPSC states that loose-fitting plugs can cause overheating and fires, and that a loose connection cannot carry much current without getting hot. Heat further softens the spring metal, so the grip gets worse over time, and arcing can scorch the receptacle body. A half-pulled plug also leaves energized metal exposed to fingers. Signs: plugs that droop or drop out, a faceplate warm to the touch, brown or melted marks, crackling or buzzing, a burning smell, or power that cuts in and out when the cord moves. Spreading the plug blades is not a fix. CPSC's answer is to have the outlet replaced: breaker off, verified dead with a tester, and a new receptacle of the same rating, GFCI protected where the location now requires it and tamper-resistant where required (NEC 406.4(D)(3), 406.4(D)(5), 406.12). Scorched wires or a melted box call for a licensed electrician.

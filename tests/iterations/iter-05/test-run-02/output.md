If a plug keeps sliding out of your outlet, the real reason is usually the outlet itself. The metal grips inside its slots have worn loose.

So try this. Does every plug you own fall out of that one outlet, or does one plug fall out of every outlet you try? Every plug in one outlet points at the outlet. One plug everywhere points at the plug.

Most folks shrug it off as an old outlet being fussy. But that loose fit is a warning, and it's a warning about heat.

You can swap a worn outlet yourself with a screwdriver and a couple of testers. The part that isn't yours is anything scorched or melted. By the end of this video you'll know which new outlet belongs in that spot, and the test that proves you wired it right.

Step one. The two-plug test. Do it before you pick up a screwdriver.

Take the plug that keeps dropping out and push it into an outlet in another room, one that holds your plugs well. Then take a plug you trust, say from a lamp that never gives you trouble, and push it into the suspect outlet.

If your loose plug holds tight somewhere else, and your good plug sags in the suspect outlet, you've found it. It's the outlet. That's the usual answer.

If your loose plug falls out everywhere you try it, look hard at the plug instead. Its two flat blades may be bent, or thin and flimsy. You'll see that a lot on cheap phone chargers.

Now, about the old trick. You'd figure the answer is to pry those blades apart a little so they bite harder. Plenty of people do it. But spreading the blades isn't a fix. The Consumer Product Safety Commission, the CPSC, says bending plug blades usually makes the overheating worse.

After thirty-five years in the trade, I'd still leave those blades alone. If your charger has bent or flimsy blades, I'd replace it, not reshape it.

You'll know you've got it sorted when a good plug goes into a good outlet with a firm push and stays put with the cord hanging down. I'd do this one first, every time. That's two minutes, no tools, and now you know which one you're fixing.

If it's your outlet, the next question is why a part that held for years suddenly lets go.

Step two. Watch which plugs give up first.

In most houses it's the two-prong ones. Your phone charger slides out and your lamp cord drops, while the three-prong plugs hang on longer. Is that what happens at your place?

It usually is, and the reason sits inside. Behind each slot in your outlet sits a strip of brass, bent into a spring, so it pinches the plug's blade from both sides.

That pinch has two jobs. It holds your plug in the wall. And it makes a tight, solid connection, so the power crosses into the plug easily instead of fighting its way across.

A three-prong plug gets help. Its round pin is the ground, the path that gives stray current a safe way back, and that pin braces the plug in the outlet. A two-prong plug has nothing but its two blades. So when the grip weakens, yours are the first to slide out.

That's also why the worn outlets you remember are in hotel rooms, kitchens and bathrooms. Those outlets get plugged into all day long.

So what wears a spring out? Every time you push a plug in, the brass flexes. Do that thousands of times and the metal gets tired.

Then there's the yank. When you pull a plug out by its cord at an angle, you bend those contacts sideways, a little more every time.

And then there's heat. Your vacuum or your hair dryer pulls a lot of power through those two small contacts, and the heat softens the spring.

Put all that together and the contacts spread apart. They stop gripping.

You might look at your outlet and think it's fine, because the face isn't cracked and nothing looks broken. But the wear is all behind the slots, where you can't see it. And a worn contact doesn't spring back. You can't adjust it from the front. The fix for a worn outlet is a new outlet.

I'd rather you swap a tired outlet on a quiet afternoon than wait for it to get worse.

So that's why your phone charger is always the first to go. You didn't do anything wrong. That outlet just wore out doing its job.

But a worn grip isn't only a nuisance, and the next step is the one I most want you to hear.

Step three. Pay attention to how that outlet feels, sounds and smells when you use it, because the loose fit is a warning.

Here's what's going on at a worn contact. When the brass doesn't grip, it only touches your plug's blade on a small spot. A small spot has a harder time carrying the power, so it heats up when you've got something running.

I'm not being dramatic. The CPSC says loose-fitting plugs can cause overheating and fires. They also say a loose connection can't carry much current without getting hot.

And it builds on itself. Heat softens the spring metal even more, so the grip gets weaker, so the contact runs hotter. Over time, electricity can jump the tiny gap, which is called arcing, and that can scorch the plastic body of your outlet.

There's a second risk, and it's a simpler one. A plug hanging halfway out leaves part of its metal blades showing, and those blades still carry power. They sit right where a small finger can find them. Until you've replaced that outlet, I'd leave it empty and use a sound one nearby.

That's the real problem behind the droop. So here's what I want you to notice. A plug that droops or drops out. A cover plate that feels warm when you touch it in everyday use. Brown marks or melted spots around the slots. A crackle or a buzz. A smell like hot plastic. Or a lamp that flickers off and on when you move its cord.

Is any of that going on at yours? A droop is easy to put up with, but it's the first sign on that list. If all you've got is the droop, you've caught it early, and I'd much rather you catch it there. That's a swap, and the next steps walk you through it.

But some of those signs mean you stop now.

If the cover plate is warm, or the plastic around the slots has turned brown, stop using that outlet now. Unplug whatever's in it. Then get a licensed electrician to look at it soon.

If you smell burning or hot plastic, see sparks, or hear crackling or buzzing, unplug what's in it or stop using it. If you can do it safely, switch that circuit off at the breaker. Your breaker is a switch in your panel, the breaker box where the power comes into the house, and it shuts off one circuit. A circuit is that breaker plus everything it feeds. Then call a licensed electrician. If the smell or the buzz is coming from the panel itself, don't touch the panel.

Heat or scorching means the trouble may be in the wiring behind your outlet too, and that's an electrician's job. The good news is you'll notice every one of those signs in everyday life, with your eyes, your ears and your nose, long before you'd ever need a tool.

If yours only droops, you're ahead of it. Now, before you buy anything, there's one job to do first, and it decides what you buy.

Step four. Look inside the box before you shop.

What's behind that cover plate decides which outlet you need, and whether this is your job at all. So you look first, with the power off and proven off.

While the power's still on, push a plug-in outlet tester into your worn outlet. That's the little tester with three lights on its face. A worn outlet won't hold it, but you can keep a hand on its body to hold it in. Watch the lights come on. I like this tester for the job, because in a minute you'll watch those same lights go dark.

If your outlet only has two slots and no round hole, skip the plug-in tester. You'll use a non-contact tester at each slot instead, the pen-shaped one that beeps near a wire with power. Then you'll use a two-probe tester across the two slots. That's a tester with two metal probes that reads whether there's really power between them.

Then go to your panel and switch off the breaker for that outlet. Back at the outlet, hold the plug-in tester in again. Its lights should stay dark. Check the top half and the bottom half, because the two halves can be fed from two different breakers.

It's tempting to go by the labels on your panel door. But labels can be wrong. The tester is what you trust.

Put a strip of tape over that breaker and leave a note on it, so nobody flips it back on while you work.

Now take your non-contact pen tester and check that it works on an outlet you know has power. Take the cover plate off your worn outlet. Hold the tester to the screws on its sides. Take out the two screws that hold the outlet to the box, and ease it forward by its metal strap, without touching the wires. Then hold your tester to every wire and every cable you can see in that box, not just the ones on the outlet. Last, check the tester on that outlet with power one more time. If the battery died halfway through, that last check is what catches it.

Your pen tester is handy, but its silence never proves a wire is dead on its own. In a metal box, or on cable wrapped in metal armor, it can stay quiet on a wire that still has power. There, put the two-probe tester on the wires themselves.

Say your tester still lights up at one of those wires. Then you stop, and your hands stay out of the box. Go back to the panel, find the breaker that's still feeding that wire, switch it off, and test again. If you can't find it, call an electrician.

I know that's a lot of testing for one outlet. Do every bit of it anyway. I mean that.

Now look. If you see brown or blackened plastic, scorched insulation on the wires, or a melted box, you stop there. The breaker stays off, and that outlet stays out of service until a licensed electrician has seen it.

If the insulation on your wires is brittle, cracked or crumbling, that's a stop too. Have an electrician look at it.

And if your house went up between about 1965 and the mid-1970s, look at the color of the wire ends. Dull silver instead of copper, or a cable printed with the letters A L or the word aluminum, means you've got aluminum wiring. That's a stop. Repairs on aluminum are a licensed electrician's job.

If none of that's there, you're in good shape. Take a picture with your phone and make a few notes. Is there a bare copper or green wire in the box? That's your ground. Count the cables coming in. Check whether the little brass tab between the two screws on one side has been snapped off. Notice whether any wires are pushed straight into the back of the outlet. And look closely at the slots on the old outlet, because one detail there matters in the next step.

Then ease the outlet back in, put the plate back on, and leave the breaker off and taped while you shop.

Now you'll walk into the store knowing exactly what your box needs, instead of guessing in the aisle.

Step five. Pick the right outlet.

Start with those slots. If one slot has a little sideways T, you've got the twenty-amp kind, and your new one has to match. Amps measure how much current is flowing, and that rating says how much the outlet's built for. If there's no T, buy the ordinary kind, same as the old one.

Next, your new outlet should be tamper-resistant. Inside its slots are little shutters that open only when both blades push in at the same time, so a hairpin or a key can't go into one side. A replacement has to be tamper-resistant wherever the code calls for it, and in a home that takes in most outlets.

It's easy to assume any outlet will do from there. But where it sits decides what kind it has to be. So where's yours? A bathroom, the kitchen, the garage, the basement, a crawl space, the laundry, outside, or within six feet of a sink, a tub or a shower?

If it's in one of those spots, your new one has to be GFCI protected. A GFCI is an outlet with test and reset buttons that cuts the power in a split second if current starts leaking where it shouldn't, like through a person. That protection can come from a GFCI outlet right there, or from one already protecting it from somewhere else on the circuit. An outlet protected that way often carries a small sticker that says GFCI protected, and I'd look for that sticker before you buy. Outside, your new one also has to be the weather-resistant type. Towns adopt the code on their own schedule, so check with your local inspector if you're not sure what yours requires.

If you found no ground wire in the box, don't buy a plain three-prong outlet. Your choices are a two-prong outlet, or a GFCI outlet marked no equipment ground. That GFCI protects you from shock, but it doesn't add a ground.

Whatever you buy, make sure it's listed. Listed means a testing lab such as UL has tested it and certified it.

One more thing to check on the package. Many outlets have small holes in the back where you just push a wire in and a little spring holds it. That's what people call a backstab. It's quick. But that spring can loosen as the outlet heats up and cools down. The screws on the side grip better, and so do clamp-style holes on the back, where a screw tightens a plate down on the wire.

I'd use the side screws or the clamps every time, and I'd leave the push-in holes empty.

Now the outlet in your bag fits your box, your room and the code. You've done the hard thinking, and the next part is for your hands.

Step six. Make the swap.

Back home, prove it's dead again before you touch anything. Somebody may have peeled that tape off while you were out. Check your pen tester on an outlet with power, take the plate off, ease the outlet out, test every wire, and check the tester on the good outlet again. It takes you a minute.

Now unhook the old outlet. Loosen each screw and slide each wire off. Where a wire goes straight into a hole in the back, cut it off close to the old outlet. Then strip a fresh end with wire strippers, the tool with sized notches that takes the insulation off without nicking the copper. If that leaves you a wire too short to reach a screw comfortably, the usual fix is a pigtail. That's a short piece of wire joined on inside the box. If you've never made one, that's a fair place to call an electrician.

Each wire has its own home. The hot wire, usually black, carries the power from the breaker, and it goes on a brass screw. The neutral, the white one, carries the current back to the panel, and it goes on a silver screw. The bare copper or green wire, your ground, goes on the green screw. If you swap the black and the white, the outlet will still work. But the metal inside a lamp's socket can then carry power with the lamp switched off. That's why the colors matter.

Strip each wire to the gauge molded on the back of your new outlet. The length changes from one outlet to the next, so go by the one in your hand.

On a side screw, bend the bare end into a hook and set it on clockwise, about three-quarters of the way around. The direction looks like it doesn't matter. But tightening the screw pulls a clockwise hook in, and it pushes the other kind out. With the clamp-style kind, the straight end goes in and the screw clamps down on it. Either way, you put one wire under each screw, unless the outlet's marked for two.

If that brass tab was snapped off on the old one, snap off the same tab on the new one, so the two halves stay fed the way they were.

Tighten every screw as firm as the instruction sheet says. The maker prints that figure for each outlet.

If you're putting in a GFCI, follow its sheet closely. The terminals marked line take the power coming from the panel. The ones marked load feed any outlets beyond it. If the sheet loses you at any point, the maker's own advice is to call a qualified electrician.

I'd take my time on the hooks. A rushed one is how a brand-new outlet ends up with a loose connection of its own.

Fold the wires back in neatly, screw the outlet to the box, and put the cover plate on. Every outlet gets its plate, so nothing with power is left out in the open.

You've got a new outlet, tight on every screw, with every wire where it belongs. Now for the step that tells you it's right.

Step seven. Power back on, and the test that proves it.

Peel the tape off the breaker and switch it back on.

Push the plug-in tester into your new outlet. Its lights should show the pattern the chart on the tester calls correct, usually two lights on. Try the top half and the bottom half.

If your box had no ground wire, the tester will show an open ground. That looks like a fault, but it's expected there. If you put in a GFCI marked no equipment ground, its test button is how you check it.

If you put in a GFCI, plug in a lamp and turn it on. Press the test button, and the lamp should go out. Press reset, and it should come back on. If it doesn't, I'd replace that GFCI before anybody uses that outlet.

The plug-in tester is a good check, but it's never permission to go back in the box. If you ever need to open it again, the breaker goes off and you prove it dead again.

Those lights tell you the wiring's right. But the real test is the plug that used to fall out. Push it in. It should take a firm push to seat, and it should stay put with the cord hanging down. Does yours hold?

That's the grip your old outlet lost. And the connection behind it is tight again, which was the part that mattered all along. I like that kind of fix. You can feel that it's right.

This week, give the outlets that work the hardest the same two-plug test from step one. Try the one your vacuum always goes into, and the one by the bathroom sink. A plug that sags tells you which outlet is next, while it's still an easy swap.

[warm] Is any of that going on at yours? A droop is easy to put up with, but it's the first sign on that list. If all you've got is the droop, you've caught it early, and I'd much rather you catch it there. That's a swap, and the next steps walk you through it.

[emphatic] But some of those signs mean you stop now.

If the cover plate is warm, or the plastic around the slots has turned brown, stop using that outlet now. Unplug whatever's in it. Then get a licensed electrician to look at it soon.

If you smell burning or hot plastic, see sparks, or hear crackling or buzzing, unplug what's in it or stop using it. If you can do it safely, switch that circuit off at the breaker. Your breaker is a switch in your panel, the breaker box where the power comes into the house, and it shuts off one circuit. A circuit is that breaker plus everything it feeds. Then call a licensed electrician. If the smell or the buzz is coming from the panel itself, don't touch the panel.

Heat or scorching means the trouble may be in the wiring behind your outlet too, and that's an electrician's job. [warm] The good news is you'll notice every one of those signs in everyday life, with your eyes, your ears and your nose, long before you'd ever need a tool.

If yours only droops, you're ahead of it. Now, before you buy anything, there's one job to do first, and it decides what you buy.

[matter-of-fact] Step four. Look inside the box before you shop.

What's behind that cover plate decides which outlet you need, and whether this is your job at all. So you look first, with the power off and proven off.

While the power's still on, push a plug-in outlet tester into your worn outlet. That's the little tester with three lights on its face. A worn outlet won't hold it, but you can keep a hand on its body to hold it in. Watch the lights come on. I like this tester for the job, because in a minute you'll watch those same lights go dark.

If your outlet only has two slots and no round hole, skip the plug-in tester. You'll use a non-contact tester at each slot instead, the pen-shaped one that beeps near a wire with power. Then you'll use a two-probe tester across the two slots. That's a tester with two metal probes that reads whether there's really power between them.

Then go to your panel and switch off the breaker for that outlet. Back at the outlet, hold the plug-in tester in again. Its lights should stay dark. Check the top half and the bottom half, because the two halves can be fed from two different breakers.

[matter-of-fact] It's tempting to go by the labels on your panel door. But labels can be wrong. The tester is what you trust.

Put a strip of tape over that breaker and leave a note on it, so nobody flips it back on while you work.

Now take your non-contact pen tester and check that it works on an outlet you know has power. Take the cover plate off your worn outlet. Hold the tester to the screws on its sides. Take out the two screws that hold the outlet to the box, and ease it forward by its metal strap, without touching the wires. Then hold your tester to every wire and every cable you can see in that box, not just the ones on the outlet. Last, check the tester on that outlet with power one more time. If the battery died halfway through, that last check is what catches it.

[thoughtful] Your pen tester is handy, but its silence never proves a wire is dead on its own. In a metal box, or on cable wrapped in metal armor, it can stay quiet on a wire that still has power. There, put the two-probe tester on the wires themselves.

Say your tester still lights up at one of those wires. Then you stop, and your hands stay out of the box. Go back to the panel, find the breaker that's still feeding that wire, switch it off, and test again. If you can't find it, call an electrician.

[warm] I know that's a lot of testing for one outlet. Do every bit of it anyway. I mean that.

[slower] Now look. If you see brown or blackened plastic, scorched insulation on the wires, or a melted box, you stop there. The breaker stays off, and that outlet stays out of service until a licensed electrician has seen it.

If the insulation on your wires is brittle, cracked or crumbling, that's a stop too. Have an electrician look at it.

And if your house went up between about 1965 and the mid-1970s, look at the color of the wire ends. Dull silver instead of copper, or a cable printed with the letters A L or the word aluminum, means you've got aluminum wiring. That's a stop. Repairs on aluminum are a licensed electrician's job.

[warm] If none of that's there, you're in good shape. Take a picture with your phone and make a few notes. Is there a bare copper or green wire in the box? That's your ground. Count the cables coming in. Check whether the little brass tab between the two screws on one side has been snapped off. Notice whether any wires are pushed straight into the back of the outlet. And look closely at the slots on the old outlet, because one detail there matters in the next step.

Then ease the outlet back in, put the plate back on, and leave the breaker off and taped while you shop.

Now you'll walk into the store knowing exactly what your box needs, instead of guessing in the aisle.

[matter-of-fact] Step five. Pick the right outlet.

Start with those slots. If one slot has a little sideways T, you've got the twenty-amp kind, and your new one has to match. Amps measure how much current is flowing, and that rating says how much the outlet's built for. If there's no T, buy the ordinary kind, same as the old one.

Next, your new outlet should be tamper-resistant. Inside its slots are little shutters that open only when both blades push in at the same time, so a hairpin or a key can't go into one side. A replacement has to be tamper-resistant wherever the code calls for it, and in a home that takes in most outlets.

[thoughtful] It's easy to assume any outlet will do from there. But where it sits decides what kind it has to be. So where's yours? A bathroom, the kitchen, the garage, the basement, a crawl space, the laundry, outside, or within six feet of a sink, a tub or a shower?

If it's in one of those spots, your new one has to be GFCI protected. A GFCI is an outlet with test and reset buttons that cuts the power in a split second if current starts leaking where it shouldn't, like through a person. That protection can come from a GFCI outlet right there, or from one already protecting it from somewhere else on the circuit. An outlet protected that way often carries a small sticker that says GFCI protected, and I'd look for that sticker before you buy. Outside, your new one also has to be the weather-resistant type. Towns adopt the code on their own schedule, so check with your local inspector if you're not sure what yours requires.

If you found no ground wire in the box, don't buy a plain three-prong outlet. Your choices are a two-prong outlet, or a GFCI outlet marked no equipment ground. That GFCI protects you from shock, but it doesn't add a ground.

Whatever you buy, make sure it's listed. Listed means a testing lab such as UL has tested it and certified it.

One more thing to check on the package. Many outlets have small holes in the back where you just push a wire in and a little spring holds it. That's what people call a backstab. It's quick. But that spring can loosen as the outlet heats up and cools down. The screws on the side grip better, and so do clamp-style holes on the back, where a screw tightens a plate down on the wire.

I'd use the side screws or the clamps every time, and I'd leave the push-in holes empty.

[warm] Now the outlet in your bag fits your box, your room and the code. You've done the hard thinking, and the next part is for your hands.

[matter-of-fact] Step six. Make the swap.

Back home, prove it's dead again before you touch anything. Somebody may have peeled that tape off while you were out. Check your pen tester on an outlet with power, take the plate off, ease the outlet out, test every wire, and check the tester on the good outlet again. It takes you a minute.

Now unhook the old outlet. Loosen each screw and slide each wire off. Where a wire goes straight into a hole in the back, cut it off close to the old outlet. Then strip a fresh end with wire strippers, the tool with sized notches that takes the insulation off without nicking the copper. If that leaves you a wire too short to reach a screw comfortably, the usual fix is a pigtail. That's a short piece of wire joined on inside the box. If you've never made one, that's a fair place to call an electrician.

[thoughtful] Each wire has its own home. The hot wire, usually black, carries the power from the breaker, and it goes on a brass screw. The neutral, the white one, carries the current back to the panel, and it goes on a silver screw. The bare copper or green wire, your ground, goes on the green screw. If you swap the black and the white, the outlet will still work. But the metal inside a lamp's socket can then carry power with the lamp switched off. That's why the colors matter.

Strip each wire to the gauge molded on the back of your new outlet. The length changes from one outlet to the next, so go by the one in your hand.

On a side screw, bend the bare end into a hook and set it on clockwise, about three-quarters of the way around. [slightly amused] The direction looks like it doesn't matter. But tightening the screw pulls a clockwise hook in, and it pushes the other kind out. With the clamp-style kind, the straight end goes in and the screw clamps down on it. Either way, you put one wire under each screw, unless the outlet's marked for two.

If that brass tab was snapped off on the old one, snap off the same tab on the new one, so the two halves stay fed the way they were.

Tighten every screw as firm as the instruction sheet says. The maker prints that figure for each outlet.

If you're putting in a GFCI, follow its sheet closely. The terminals marked line take the power coming from the panel. The ones marked load feed any outlets beyond it. If the sheet loses you at any point, the maker's own advice is to call a qualified electrician.

[quietly] I'd take my time on the hooks. A rushed one is how a brand-new outlet ends up with a loose connection of its own.

Fold the wires back in neatly, screw the outlet to the box, and put the cover plate on. Every outlet gets its plate, so nothing with power is left out in the open.

[warm] You've got a new outlet, tight on every screw, with every wire where it belongs. Now for the step that tells you it's right.

[matter-of-fact] Step seven. Power back on, and the test that proves it.

Peel the tape off the breaker and switch it back on.

Push the plug-in tester into your new outlet. Its lights should show the pattern the chart on the tester calls correct, usually two lights on. Try the top half and the bottom half.

If your box had no ground wire, the tester will show an open ground. That looks like a fault, but it's expected there. If you put in a GFCI marked no equipment ground, its test button is how you check it.

If you put in a GFCI, plug in a lamp and turn it on. Press the test button, and the lamp should go out. Press reset, and it should come back on. If it doesn't, I'd replace that GFCI before anybody uses that outlet.

The plug-in tester is a good check, but it's never permission to go back in the box. If you ever need to open it again, the breaker goes off and you prove it dead again.

[thoughtful] Those lights tell you the wiring's right. But the real test is the plug that used to fall out. Push it in. It should take a firm push to seat, and it should stay put with the cord hanging down. Does yours hold?

[short pause] That's the grip your old outlet lost. And the connection behind it is tight again, which was the part that mattered all along. I like that kind of fix. You can feel that it's right.

[warm] This week, give the outlets that work the hardest the same two-plug test from step one. Try the one your vacuum always goes into, and the one by the bathroom sink. A plug that sags tells you which outlet is next, while it's still an easy swap.

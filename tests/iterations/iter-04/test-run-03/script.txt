Number one is ignoring the early signs of roots in your sewer line.
The water climbs high in the bowl before it goes down, the tub gurgles when the washer drains, and the same clog comes back a month after you plunged it.
Plenty of folks figure that’s just an old toilet acting old.
But roots go looking for water, and an old sewer line with a cracked joint is a free drink.
One thin root gets in, catches paper and grease, and grows into a wet, hairy mat that chokes the line.
When the same clog comes back twice, stop plunging and ask for a camera inspection, a small waterproof camera fed down the line so you know what’s in there.
It runs about $150 to $300, depending on where you live.
Catch the roots early and they can usually be cut out.
Wait, and you’re paying for a dig-up or for relining, which means sliding a new liner inside the old pipe, and either one is thousands.
A couple hundred to look, against an excavator in your yard.
I’d make that call every time.

But roots take years.

Number two is a toilet that runs.
You’re lying in bed, you hear the tank hiss and refill with nobody flushing, and you roll over.
But a running toilet can waste up to about 200 gallons a day, and you pay for every one of them while you sleep, tens of dollars a month on the bill.
Here’s a check before you buy a thing.
Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, don’t flush, and come back in half an hour.
Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking, that’s the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank.
A new one costs less than a fast-food lunch, and it’s about a ten-minute swap with the water shut off at the little valve behind the toilet.
Call somebody out, and you’re paying a trip fee for a rubber flap.
Do it yourself, and the hissing stops tonight.

Number three is never testing your main water shutoff until the day you need it.
That’s the day water’s coming through the ceiling, you’re in the basement in your socks, and the valve won’t turn.
You’d think a valve just works when you need it.
But one that’s sat open for decades crusts up with minerals and seizes, and the first time anybody touches it is in a panic, which is when old handles snap off.
So test it this weekend, while nothing’s wrong.
Find the main, usually where the water line comes through the foundation, or out by the meter.
Turn it off, check that a faucet upstairs goes dry, and turn it back on.
Once or twice a year keeps it free.
If it won’t budge, don’t force it.
A stuck main is a call, because a snapped valve is a far bigger problem than a stiff one.
Ten minutes, and when a pipe bursts at two in the morning, you’re the one who stops the water, not somebody on the emergency rate.

Number four is running your washer on the same rubber hoses for years.
When did you last pull the machine out and look?
If they’re plain black rubber and you can’t remember putting them on, they’re on borrowed time.
Rubber dries out, cracks at the fittings, and bulges like a tire with a bubble in it.
But from the front, everything looks fine, and those hoses hold full house pressure all day, even with the machine off.
When one bursts, it doesn’t drip.
It pours, and if you’re out running errands, it pours for hours.
Replace them every five years at the latest, sooner if they’re rubber.
A braided stainless pair runs about $15, and all it takes is the two valves behind the washer shut and a pair of pliers.
Fifteen bucks and ten minutes, against a flooded laundry room.
There’s no service call for a hose that never burst.

That one’s about water getting out.
The next one’s about what you pour in.

Number five is grabbing the jug of chemical drain cleaner every time a drain slows down.
It fizzes, the sink drains, and it feels like the job’s done.
But it only burned a hole through the middle of the clog, and the rest of that slimy gunk is still stuck to the pipe, waiting for the next wad of hair.
In an old house, that chemical can sit in the drain and work on cast iron and galvanized pipe that’s already thin from decades of water.
So the clog comes back, and so does the jug, until you’re paying to replace pipe instead of clearing a clog.
Use a plunger first, with a wet rag in the overflow hole on a bathroom sink.
Then a hand snake, which is a coil of wire you crank down the drain to pull the hair out.
And if there’s already chemical in a drain that won’t go down, don’t plunge it or pour anything on top, because it can splash back in your face.
A hand snake costs about what three jugs do.
I’d leave the jug on the shelf.

But the jug isn’t the only thing on that shelf making a promise it can’t keep.

Number six is believing the word “flushable” on a package of wipes.
Is there a box of them next to your toilet right now?
Then you already know they go down when you flush.
But going down isn’t the question.
Toilet paper breaks up in water in seconds.
A wipe stays in one piece, snags on a rough joint or a root in an old line, and catches the next one, until there’s a gray, stinking rope of wipes and grease in your sewer line.
Then it backs up out of the lowest drain in the house, and clearing a main line runs into the hundreds.
Put a small trash can with a lid next to every toilet.
Wipes go in the can, and only toilet paper goes down.
The package will never tell you that, but a cheap trash can does the job the label won’t.

Number seven is ignoring a jump in your water bill.
The bill comes in higher, nothing’s changed, and you blame the water company.
But a bill that climbs for no reason is very often a leak you can’t see, inside a wall, under the slab, or a toilet running between flushes.
The EPA puts the average home’s leaks at close to 10,000 gallons a year, every gallon of it quietly costing you.
Turn off every faucet, and make sure nothing’s running, no washer, no dishwasher, no ice maker.
Read the water meter and write the number down.
Wait at least half an hour, two hours is better, and read it again.
If it moved, water’s going somewhere it shouldn’t.
Start with the cheap suspects, like the toilet check from number two.
A leak-detection visit costs real money, and your meter does the first part for free.

But hidden leaks aren’t the only thing building up where you can’t see.
The next one’s in your kitchen drain, and it’s been piling up since breakfast.

Number eight is grease down the kitchen sink.
Where does your bacon grease go when the pan’s done?
If it’s down the sink with hot water and a squirt of dish soap, you probably figure that carries it clean through.
But it only carries it a few feet, to where the pipe runs cold.
Then it sets on the inside of the pipe the way wax sets on a candle holder, thicker every time, until the drain is a slow, smelly, slimy tube.
Clearing a grease clog in the kitchen line can run $150 to $400.
Wipe the pan with a paper towel before it ever sees the sink.
Pour the cooled grease into an old can, let it set, and throw it in the trash.
A paper towel and an empty can, against a plumber with a snake on a Saturday.

And while you’re standing at that sink, open the cabinet under it.

Number nine is a slow drip under a sink that nobody notices.
You reach under for the dish soap, the cabinet floor feels soft or warped, and there’s a musty smell.
You’d figure a leak makes a puddle.
But a slow one soaks into the cabinet, then the floor under it, and grows black mold back there in the dark for months, until you’re replacing a sink cabinet and maybe the floor it sits on.
On the first Saturday of every month, with my coffee, I look under every sink in the house with a flashlight.
Do the same, and with the water running, wipe a dry paper towel along every joint and along the trap, that’s the U-shaped bend in the drain.
Wet towel, you’ve found it.
A big plastic nut on the drain often just needs snugging by hand.
Where the water line comes out of the wall, a quarter turn with a wrench and no more, because old fittings crack when you lean on them.
Thirty seconds a sink, and that cabinet stays dry.

The next one is a number your house has never told you, and it’s behind more leaks than you’d think.

Number ten is never checking your water pressure.
Do you know what yours runs at?
If you don’t, you’ve got plenty of company, because strong pressure feels great in the shower and nobody complains.
But it pushes on every hose, toilet valve and faucet in the house, and on the water heater, all day long, and wears them out early.
Pressure is measured in psi, pounds per square inch, and a house is happiest around 50 to 60.
Over about 80, everything’s straining, and most plumbing codes call for a pressure-reducing valve, a bell-shaped valve where the water comes in that holds the pressure down.
A pressure gauge runs about $10 to $25.
Screw it onto an outside faucet, turn the water on, and read the needle.
Over 80, have that valve put in, or have the one you’ve got checked.
Nobody’s going to knock on your door to tell you your pressure’s too high, but the gauge tells you in a minute.

And if your pressure’s been high for years, your water heater’s been paying for it.

Number eleven is never checking the anode rod in your water heater.
The hot water comes out rusty or smells like rotten eggs, and you figure the tank is done.
But a lot of the time, that’s the anode rod telling you it’s used up.
It’s a metal rod hanging inside the tank, and its whole job is to corrode away so the steel tank doesn’t.
Once it’s gone, the water goes to work on the tank, and a tank that rusts through can’t be fixed, so that’s a new water heater.
Check the rod once a year, because depending on your water, it can last a couple of years or five.
First shut off the power at the breaker, or turn the gas control to off, then close the cold water going in and open a hot faucet to let the pressure off.
If the rod under the hex head on top is down to bare wire or crusted thick, replace it, and a new one runs about $30 to $40.
If that hex head won’t break loose, pay somebody, because it’s still far less than a tank.

While you’re standing there, look at the temperature dial.
That’s number twelve, and it can cost you twice.

Number twelve is setting the water heater too hot.
Folks crank it up so the shower lasts longer, or never touch it after it goes in.
But hotter water costs you on the bill every day, and it’s dangerous.
At 140 degrees, water can give an adult a third-degree burn in about six seconds.
At 120, it takes about five minutes, and older people and small children burn faster.
The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends 120, and the Department of Energy figures turning it down to 120 can save you around $35 to $60 a year.
On a gas heater, it’s the dial on the gas control.
On an electric one, shut the breaker off first, because the thermostats sit behind panels with live wiring.
Not sure where yours is set?
Run the hot tap a couple of minutes and put a cooking thermometer in a cup of it.
One dial, and the bill comes down and nobody gets scalded at the sink.

Number thirteen is doing your own gas-line work.
You’re swapping a gas dryer or a stove, the line’s right there, and it looks like fifteen minutes.
But gas work is a licensed job, it usually needs a permit, and a fitting that’s a hair loose doesn’t drip like water.
It leaks gas into your house, and some people can’t smell gas at all, and the smell can fade.
A licensed hookup where the line’s already there usually runs a few hundred dollars, and that’s the best money you’ll spend on the house.
And if you ever smell gas, here’s every step.
Get everybody out right now, and move away, upwind.
On the way out, don’t flip a light switch or touch an appliance, a doorbell, a phone or a flashlight.
Don’t light anything, and don’t start the car.
Once you’re well away, call 911 and the gas company, and don’t assume somebody else already called.
Don’t go back in until they say it’s safe, and let the gas company turn the gas back on and relight the pilots.
Leave the gas to the people with the license.

Now, about who you hire for a job like that.

Number fourteen is hiring a plumber on price alone, without checking the license.
One quote’s way under the other two, and that fellow can come tomorrow, so it feels like a win.
But if he isn’t licensed, nobody’s checking his work, there’s no permit, and when it fails you can pay twice, once for the bad job and again to tear it out and redo it to code.
And your insurance may not cover the damage.
Checking takes two minutes, and it’s free.
Look him up on your state’s licensing board website and make sure the license is active.
Then ask whether the permit’s included, get the price in writing, and ask what the warranty is on the work.
I’m not telling you to take the highest quote, and I’d ask what’s behind a big number too.
But the cheapest bid only saves you money if the job gets done once.

Number fifteen is leaving a garden hose hooked to the outside faucet into the first freeze.
Is your hose still connected from the summer?
With the hose on, water stays trapped in the faucet and the pipe behind it, and when it freezes, it splits the pipe, a lot of the time inside the wall.
You won’t know until spring, when you turn that faucet on and the water runs into the wall instead of out the hose.
Once a frozen pipe bursts, you’re looking at $500 to $2,000 to repair, plus the cleanup.
That kind of money for a hose you could’ve unscrewed in a minute turns my stomach.
So unscrew it, drain it, and hang it up for the winter.
If that faucet has its own shutoff inside the house, close it, then open the outside faucet so it drains.
You might have a frost-free sillcock, that’s an outdoor faucet with its valve set deep inside the warm wall.
But even that one can split if the hose stays on, because the hose holds the water in.
So go out tonight before bed and take it off.
One hose, one minute, and one less thing for winter to find in your house.

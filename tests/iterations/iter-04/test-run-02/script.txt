That toilet pulled up off the floor, its drain packed solid with tree roots, didn’t get that way overnight.
A camera down the sewer line would’ve found those roots years earlier for a couple hundred bucks, and once they’ve taken over the line, you’re into the thousands.
Nobody gets paid for the flood that never happens, so nobody calls to remind you to look.

Number one.
Ignoring the early signs of roots in the sewer line.
Older houses often have clay or cast-iron sewer pipe with a joint every few feet, and roots go looking for water at those joints.
A root as thin as a hair gets in, catches some paper, gets fed, and grows.
What you’ll notice is a toilet that flushes slow, a gurgle from the tub or the floor drain when somebody flushes, and the same clog coming back every few months no matter how many times it gets cleared.
That last one’s the giveaway.
A clog that keeps coming back has something holding it there.
So instead of paying to clear it over and over, get a camera inspection.
A plumber feeds a small camera down the line, and you see exactly where the roots are and what shape the pipe is in.
Lay the prices next to each other.
$150 to $300 for the camera, $300 to $1,000 to cut the roots out, and $5,000 to $20,000 if the line has to be dug up or relined.
And if water comes up in the tub when you flush, or two drains back up at once, skip the appointment and call today, because that’s the main line.

Number two.
Ignoring a running toilet.
A toilet that hisses, trickles, or refills itself in the middle of the night can waste up to about 200 gallons a day.
That can add tens of dollars a month to the bill.
Nine times out of 10 it’s the flapper, the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush.
Here’s a check that costs nothing.
Take the lid off the tank, put a few drops of food coloring in the water, and don’t flush for about 20 minutes.
If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper’s leaking.
A new flapper runs about $4 to $10, and it’s a ten-minute swap.
Turn off the little valve on the wall and hold the handle down until the tank’s empty.
Unhook the old flapper, hook on the new one, and leave just a little slack in the chain.
If the color stays put but the tank keeps refilling, the water’s going down the overflow tube in the middle, and that’s the fill valve set too high or wearing out.
That’s still a homeowner job, and still a cheap part.

Number three.
Never testing the main shutoff until the day you need it.
If you do only one thing from this video, do this one.
When a pipe lets go, get the water off in the first minute and you’re reaching for a mop.
Let it run while you hunt for the valve, and a flooded basement commonly runs anywhere from $2,000 to $7,000 to clean up, and that’s if it’s clean water.
The main shutoff is the valve where the water line comes into the house.
In older homes it’s usually in the basement or crawl space on the street side, near the meter, or in the garage in warm parts of the country.
Find yours this weekend.
Then turn it off and back on, and do that once or twice a year so it doesn’t seize.
The catch is the valve itself.
In older houses, plenty of them are gate valves with a round handle, and after decades of sitting still they can be stuck, or break inside when you force them.
If it won’t budge, leave the cheater bar in the toolbox and have it replaced on a weekday, on your schedule, instead of at two in the morning with water coming through the ceiling.
And show everybody in the house where it is.

The next one’s about the weather, and this is the time of year for it.
Number four.
Leaving the hose hooked to the outside faucet into a freeze.
A frost-free outside faucet shuts the water off well inside the wall, where it’s warm, and it’s built to drain the rest out.
With a hose screwed on, that water can’t drain.
It freezes and splits the pipe inside the wall.
You find out in spring, the first time you turn it on and the water goes into the wall instead of the hose.
Once a frozen pipe bursts, you’re usually looking at $500 to $2,000 to repair it, and the water cleanup comes on top of that.
Before the first hard freeze, take the hoses off, drain them, and put them away.
If your house has an indoor shutoff for that outside faucet, and a lot of older houses do, close it, then open the outside faucet so the line drains.
A foam cover over the faucet costs a few dollars and buys you some insurance.

That one protects a wall.
The next one protects your water heater.
Number five.
Never checking the water heater’s anode rod.
Inside every tank water heater, a long metal rod hangs down from the top.
Its whole job is to corrode, so the steel tank doesn’t.
It’s like the brake pads on your car.
They wear out so the expensive part doesn’t have to.
When the rod’s used up, the water goes to work on the tank itself, and that ends in a tank that leaks from the bottom.
A leaking tank can’t be fixed.
It gets replaced.
A new anode rod is about $30 to $40.
Depending on your water, one lasts anywhere from a couple of years to five, so look at it once a year.
Before you touch anything, turn the power off at the breaker for an electric heater, or turn the gas control to off on a gas one.
Shut the cold water to the tank, open a hot faucet to let the pressure off, and drain a few gallons.
The rod has a hex head on top of the tank, and it takes a big socket and some muscle.
If it’s down to bare wire, or eaten to about the thickness of a pencil, replace it.
If the heater’s already past ten or twelve years and that rod’s gone, I’d put the money toward the next heater instead.

Number six.
Setting the water heater too hot.
Plenty of tanks are set hotter than anybody needs.
At 140 degrees, it takes about six seconds for hot water to give an adult a third-degree burn.
At 120, it’s about five minutes.
Older folks and little kids burn faster than that.
Those are the Consumer Product Safety Commission’s numbers, and 120 is the setting they recommend.
It costs you money, too, because a tank loses heat all day long.
Every 10 degrees you turn it down saves a few percent on heating your water.
On a gas heater, the dial’s on the front of the gas valve.
On an electric one, the thermostats sit behind access panels with live wires back there, so the breaker goes off before a panel comes off.
And if you need hotter water for a dishwasher or a health reason, a plumber can fit a mixing valve so the tank runs hot and the faucets stay safe.

Now the one I said almost everybody gets backwards.
Number seven.
Pouring chemical drain cleaner into old pipes.
I get why people reach for the jug.
It’s right there on the shelf, it’s cheaper than a service call, and it’s worked before.
That’s being careful with money, and there’s nothing wrong with that.
The trouble is what it does in an old house.
Those cleaners work with heat and harsh chemistry, and old galvanized and cast-iron drain pipe has been thinning from the inside for decades.
And when it doesn’t clear the clog, which happens plenty, it sits in the trap waiting for whoever works on that drain next.
Never plunge, snake, or open a trap on a drain that’s got drain cleaner sitting in it, because it can splash back in your face.
Next time, start with a plunger, then hot tap water, not boiling, and if that won’t do it, a hand snake.
A 25-foot snake runs about $25 to $35, and it pulls the clog out instead of trying to melt it.
If you smell sewage, or every drain in the house is slow, that’s past the sink.
Go back to number one.

Number eight.
Flushing the wipes that say flushable on the package.
That word means it’ll go down the toilet, and that’s all it means.
Toilet paper starts breaking up almost as soon as it’s wet.
A wipe holds together, and it rides down the line in one piece until it finds something to snag on.
Old pipe gives it plenty to catch on.
A rough joint in cast iron, or a few root hairs at a clay joint, the same roots from number one.
One wipe catches, the next one catches on it, and the paper piles up behind them.
Snaking the main sewer line usually runs $150 to $500, and a stubborn one costs more.
The fix costs nothing.
Put a small trash can with a lid next to every toilet, and make it the rule for the whole house.
Wipes, paper towels, cotton swabs and floss all go in the can.
The toilet gets toilet paper and nothing else.

Number nine.
Pouring grease down the kitchen sink.
It goes down as a liquid, so it feels harmless.
But the pipe under your floor is cool, and grease firms up as it cools, the same way bacon fat turns white in the pan overnight.
It coats the pipe, catches food scraps, and the opening shrinks every month.
Running hot water while you pour doesn’t fix it.
It just carries the grease a little farther before it sets.
The garbage disposal won’t save you either.
It chops food, and it does nothing to fat.
A grease clog in the kitchen line can run $150 to $400 to clear, more if it needs jetting.
Here’s what to do instead.
Let the grease cool in the pan, scrape it into an old can or a jar, and throw it out with the trash.
Wipe the pan with a paper towel before it goes in the sink, so the drain doesn’t get so much as a spoonful.

Number ten.
Ignoring a slow drip under the sink.
The drip under the sink, behind the cleaning bottles, never gets fixed, because nobody sees it.
Meanwhile the floor of that cabinet soaks it up.
Those cabinet floors are usually particleboard, and particleboard swells, crumbles and grows mold, and then the water finds the subfloor.
A ruined sink cabinet swapped for a stock one runs $250 to $600, and that’s if the floor under it is still good.
My own drains get a look the first Saturday of every month, with my coffee, and a flashlight under each sink is part of it.
To catch the slow ones, wipe every pipe and fitting under the sink dry.
Lay a dry paper towel flat underneath, then fill the sink, let it drain, and run the faucet for a minute.
Look for the wet spot, and run a dry finger around each joint.
A drip at a slip nut, the big plastic nut on the drain, often stops with a snug quarter turn by hand.
A drip at the shutoff valve or the supply line is a cheap part.
A crack in the pipe itself means a new piece of pipe.

Number eleven.
Never checking your water pressure.
Pressure that’s too high feels great in the shower.
But it’s pushing on every faucet washer, every toilet fill valve, every hose and every joint in the house, all day and all night.
Most homes do best around 50 to 60 psi.
Plumbing codes generally call for a pressure regulator once it’s over 80, because above that, everything wears out faster.
The signs are pipes that bang when a faucet shuts off, toilets that run, and faucets that start dripping again a few months after you fixed them.
A screw-on pressure gauge costs about $10 to $25.
Thread it onto an outside faucet or the washing machine connection, make sure nothing else in the house is running, and open the faucet.
If it reads over 80, the fix is a pressure-reducing valve, and that’s a plumber’s job.
If you already have one, it’s usually a bell-shaped valve near the main shutoff, and they wear out too.

That one makes everything else in the house last longer.
The next one is a cheap part standing between your washer and a flooded laundry room.
Number twelve.
Leaving rubber washing machine hoses on past five years.
Those black rubber hoses behind the washer hold full water pressure all day, whether the machine’s running or not.
Rubber hardens and cracks with age, and when one lets go, it pours for as long as nobody’s home to hear it.
Pull the washer out and look.
Bulges, cracks near the metal ends, rust on the fittings, or past five years old, and they’re done.
A braided stainless pair is about $15.
Shut off both valves behind the machine, put a towel down, unscrew the old hoses, and put the new ones on hand tight, then a little more with pliers.
Turn the valves back on and check for drips.
And if you’re leaving for more than a day or two, turn those two valves off.
It takes ten seconds.

Number thirteen.
Ignoring a jump in the water bill.
If the bill goes up and you can’t think of a reason, the water’s going somewhere.
It might be a toilet, back to number two.
It might be a pipe under the slab or out in the yard.
The EPA puts leaks in the average home at close to 10,000 gallons a year.
Your water meter will tell you for free.
Turn off every faucet and anything that uses water.
Read the meter, or snap a picture of it.
Wait at least half an hour, two hours if you can, and read it again.
If the number moved, something’s leaking.
A lot of meters also have a little leak dial, a small triangle or star, and if that’s turning with everything off, it’s the same answer.
Now shut the valves behind the toilets and do it again.
If the meter stops, it’s a toilet.
If it keeps moving, that’s the time to call for leak detection.

Number fourteen.
Hiring the cheapest bid without checking the license.
Sometimes the low price is the smart move.
But plumbing is one of the trades where bad work hides.
It’s inside the wall or under the floor, and it can look fine for a year.
When it fails, you can pay twice, once for the bad job and again to tear it out and redo it to code.
And insurance may not cover it.
Checking a license is free.
Your state licensing board has a lookup on its website, and in some places it’s the city or the county.
Type in the plumber’s name, make sure the license is active, and look for complaints.
Then ask a few plain questions before you sign anything.
Will you pull a permit?
Is the price in writing?
What’s the warranty on the work?
A good plumber answers those without blinking.

Number fifteen.
Doing your own gas line work.
This is the one I said I’d never let you touch.
Everything else in this video, you can check, and a lot of it you can fix yourself.
Gas is different.
Moving a line, hooking up a stove or a dryer, swapping the connector behind one, any of it.
A fitting that’s a hair loose doesn’t leak like water.
There’s no puddle and no drip to warn you.
It’s a licensed job, it usually needs a permit, and a short gas line to one appliance is a few hundred dollars well spent.
If you ever smell gas, get everybody out of the house now and move away from it.
Don’t flip a light switch, don’t use the phone inside, and don’t start the car in the garage.
Once you’re well away, call 911 and the gas company, and don’t assume somebody else already called.
Don’t go back in until they tell you it’s safe.
Some people can’t smell gas at all, and the smell can fade, so if anybody in the house says they smell it, believe them.

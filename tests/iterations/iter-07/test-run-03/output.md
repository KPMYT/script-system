SCRIPT TYPE: Talking Head

The way I was raised in Paris, a woman didn't wait to lose weight before she dressed well, and she didn't buy a size up to hide.

Most French women over 60 skip both habits, and they still don't look frumpy, because frumpy comes from fit, proportion, fabric and finish, and all four work at any size.

So here are the five rules they follow, and how to borrow each one from your own closet.

Rule number one is fit before size.

When your body changes, the first instinct is to hide it, and a bigger size feels like a safe place to do that. I understand the instinct. But buying a size up to cover your middle or your arms does the opposite of what you want. The extra fabric hangs from the widest point of your body and falls straight down, so the whole outfit takes on that width. You end up looking bigger than you are, and older too, because shapeless clothes are exactly what frumpy looks like.

So why does the same kind of jacket look so different on a French woman of the same size? It's the alterations. In Paris, taking new clothes to a tailor is an ordinary habit for a lot of women, and it isn't treated as a luxury. They buy the piece that fits the widest part of them, but they don't stop there. They have the rest taken in or taken up until it sits right.

Here's where to start. Fit the shoulders first, because a tailor can shorten a sleeve or take in a waist easily, but shoulders are hard to change. On a jacket or a blouse, the shoulder seam should sit right at the edge of your shoulder bone. Do the seams on your jackets sit there, or halfway down your arm? If they drop, the jacket is too big, whatever the label says.

Then the hems. Pants should just touch the top of your shoe, or stop at the ankle bone if they're cropped. Sleeves should end at the wrist bone, so a little of your wrist shows. Those two small changes make a piece off the rack look like it was made for you.

And a word about fitted, because that word makes women nervous. You don't have to wear anything tight. Fitted means the fabric follows your shape instead of floating around it. If buttons pull or a zipper fights you, go up a size and have the tailor take in whatever is loose. Clothes that close easily and still follow your shape are the goal.

Here's the check. Put on your favorite jacket, stand side-on to a full-length mirror, and take a photo with your phone. If there's fabric sliding off the shoulder or bunching at the ankle, that piece goes to the tailor before it goes back in the closet.

I'd take one tailored jacket over three new ones any day. The fit is what makes clothes look expensive, and nobody can see what size is on the label.

Rule number two is a small wardrobe of neutrals with one accent.

In Paris, many women build their closet on a short list of colors: navy, black, gray, camel, cream and white. Then they add one color they love, usually in a scarf or a lip. That's the whole palette.

It sounds limiting, but when every piece is a neutral, almost everything you own goes with everything else. You stop buying the bright top that matches nothing, and you stop standing in front of a full closet with nothing to wear.

How many of your clothes actually go together? Be honest with yourself. A closet full of loud, unmatched pieces makes a woman look like she got dressed in a hurry, and that's a frumpy look before the fit even comes into it.

So go through your closet and pull out everything in those neutrals. That's your foundation. If black feels harsh next to your face, I'd lean on navy instead, because it's softer and just as smart. Then choose your one accent. I'd pick it at a window in daylight. Hold the color up to your face, and if your skin looks brighter and your eyes look clearer, that's your color. If you look tired, put it back.

And repeat your outfits. In Paris, it's usual to wear the same good coat or the same navy sweater again and again, and plenty of women there have something close to a uniform. I think it's one of the most freeing habits you can borrow, but it only works once the colors agree with each other.

Repeating also means fewer pieces, so each one has to be better made, and better made mostly means the fabric. A thick cotton, a fine wool or a silk holds its shape and hangs smoothly, but a thin, stretchy knit clings to every line and goes shapeless after a few washes. In the shop, scrunch the fabric in your hand for a moment. If it springs back smooth, it's worth a place in your closet.

Now nearly everything in your closet goes with everything else, and you look polished even on the mornings you didn't try.

That's the color sorted. The next rule is the one that gives you your shape back.

Rule number three is the third piece and a visible line.

The outfit that adds the most width is often the one you reach for to feel comfortable. It's a long, loose tunic over wide pants. I get the appeal. It's easy, and it covers everything. But it covers your shape too. There's no waist and no line, just one big block from your shoulders to your knees, and the eye takes that whole block as your width.

What many French women wear instead is a column with a third piece over it.

The column is one color from top to bottom. A navy top with navy pants, or black with black. One color carries the eye up and down instead of across, so you look longer and narrower without changing a thing about your body.

Then you add a third piece on top, like a trench coat, a well-cut blazer or an open cardigan, worn open. I love a trench for this, because it goes over almost anything in spring and fall. The open edges make two long lines down the front of you, and the column shows between them. That frame works at any size, and it's the reason a simple outfit looks finished.

What do you grab when you're cold at home? If it's a fleece or a baggy sweatshirt, that's the first piece I'd swap for a cardigan you can wear open.

Next, let the eye find your waist. A front tuck means tucking just the front of your top into the waistband and leaving the sides and back loose. It shows where your waist is without pulling anything tight across your middle. If you'd rather not tuck at all, that's fine. Choose a top that ends at the hip, keep the third piece open, and you get the same long line.

For the bottom half, dark straight jeans are a French staple. Dark denim looks dressed, and a straight leg falls in one line from the hip, so it balances a jacket without clinging.

And then the striped Breton top, the navy and white stripe most people picture when they think of Paris. It started as part of the French navy's uniform in the nineteenth century, but you'll still find it in French closets today, worn with dark jeans and a blazer.

The check is simple. Stand at the mirror in your column and your third piece, and look for one long line from shoulder to hem. If there's one line, you're done. If there's a block, open the jacket or tuck the front.

You come out looking longer, leaner and more pulled together, and you didn't have to lose a pound to get there.

The outfit's built now, so the next job is the finish, starting at your feet.

Rule number four is polished shoes and quiet accessories.

Shoes are where a good outfit often falls apart. Athletic sneakers as everyday shoes are a very American habit, and it makes sense, because they're comfortable. But a gym shoe under a blazer and dark jeans pulls the whole outfit back to the gym.

Are your sneakers for walking, or for everything?

In France, most women wear ballet flats, loafers or a low heel through the day. They can take a little hunting, but plenty of them are easy to walk in once you find the right pair, and they finish an outfit instead of fighting it.

Comfort matters more every year, and wanting shoes that don't hurt is just good sense. So look for a loafer with a cushioned insole, a flat with a grippy sole, or a low, wide heel. If your balance isn't what it was, skip backless shoes and anything slick on the bottom, because steady footing comes first. And if your feet need a sneaker, keep one, but make it plain leather in white or navy with a slim sole, and save the running shoes for your walks.

Then the accessories. A silk scarf is the one piece a great many French women wear almost every day. It brings your accent color right up to your face, and it covers the neck if you'd like it covered. I have one knot I can tie in the doorway without looking in a mirror, and I think every woman should have a knot like that. Pick one you can do with your eyes closed, and the scarf stops being a fuss.

Your bag should be structured, meaning it holds its own shape when you set it down, and it should have no visible logo. A soft bag stuffed to the top slumps and bulges, and a big logo does the talking for you. A plain leather bag in one of your neutrals goes with everything you own.

And wear one statement at a time. If the scarf is the star, the earrings stay small. If the necklace is bold, the scarf stays home.

Before you leave the house, look at your feet and your bag in the full-length mirror. If either one belongs at the gym or the beach, swap it.

Now the outfit looks finished from head to toe, and the first compliment you hear might be about your shoes.

That covers everything from the neck down. The last rule frames your face.

Rule number five is low-maintenance hair and a light hand on the face.

In France, plenty of women over 60 wear a cut that air-dries into shape and grows out well, so it looks good the day after the salon and weeks later too. A stiff, sprayed set looks dated, and it's work every single morning. I'd always choose a cut that moves over one that holds still.

So ask your stylist for a cut that works with your natural texture and falls into place when you let it dry on its own. Tell them you want it to grow out well between appointments. If you do use a dryer or a flat iron, put on a heat protectant first and use a lower setting, especially on gray or fine hair.

Then the gray. Letting your gray come in can feel like giving something up, and that feeling is real. But gray worn on purpose can look striking. Dull, frizzy gray can look like an accident, but glossy gray with a sharp cut looks like a choice. Ask about a gloss at your next appointment, and smooth a drop of shine serum through the ends at home. If you love your color and want to keep it, keep it. Shine and a good cut work on any color.

The face follows the same idea. French women tend to put their effort into skincare first, and plenty buy theirs right off the pharmacy shelf. Then the makeup stays light: groomed brows, a little mascara, and a red or berry lip. That lip can be the one accent color from rule number two.

When did you last take a close look at your brows? They frame your eyes, so a shaping, or just tidying the strays and filling the gaps lightly, makes your whole face look more awake.

To check it, stand at a mirror in daylight with no scarf and no jewelry on. If your hair moves and your face looks fresh with almost nothing on it, you've got it.

The result is hair and a face that look cared for, with nothing stiff or heavy, and that's about as far from frumpy as it gets.

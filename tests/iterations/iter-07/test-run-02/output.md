SCRIPT TYPE: Talking Head

If your bob never looks right at home, you may be styling it wrong after 60. Five small secrets decide whether it turns heads or drags your face down.

A flat crown with all the width at the ends makes a triangle that pulls everything lower. Ends curled under and sprayed stiff turn into a helmet. And dull, flat color makes even a good cut look tired.

None of this needs a new haircut. These five fixes work on the bob you already have, fine or thick. And most of them take a few minutes at your own mirror, so the change shows instantly.

Secret number one. Lift the crown, and let the ends stay calm.

Turn sideways to your mirror and look at the shape of your bob. If the top lies flat against your head and all the fullness sits down around your jaw, you've got the triangle. It's narrow at the top and wide at the bottom, and that shape pulls the eye straight down to the jawline. The face looks heavier and lower than it really is.

Width at the ends can't fix that, but lift at the crown can. The crown is the top of your head toward the back, where the hair swirls. When there's height there, the outline turns into a soft oval, and the eye travels up to your eyes and cheekbones instead.

It's discouraging to walk out of the salon with a bob you love and watch it go wrong by the first wash at home. But the cut didn't go anywhere. The blow-dry in the chair put lift in at the roots, and nobody showed you that step.

So how do you get that lift back yourself?

Start on damp hair. Work a little mousse or a root-lift spray into the roots at the crown, and only there. Root lift means a product that helps the hair stand up from the scalp instead of lying flat. Keep heavy creams away from the top, because they weigh fine hair down and flatten it.

Spray on a heat protectant, and turn the dryer to a lower setting if your hair is fine or color-treated. Then take a round brush, lift each section at the crown straight up from your head, and aim the air at the roots. Let each section cool on the brush for a moment before you let go. That cooling is what sets the lift.

If holding a brush over your head tires your arms, there's an easier way. Roll two or three Velcro rollers into the crown while the hair is still warm, and leave them in for ten minutes while you do something else. Take them out and loosen the hair with your fingertips. I like the rollers best on the days you want the height to last.

Now check it. Take a photo of your profile with your phone, or stand sideways to the mirror and follow the outline with your eyes. The highest point should be at the crown, never out at your ears. I'd always choose height at the top and calm at the bottom.

When the height moves up, the whole face looks lifted and rested. There's one more way to get lift at the top without picking up a brush at all, and it comes down to where you part your hair.

Secret number two. Move your part.

Where has your part been sitting for years? If it's in the same spot every day, the hair on both sides has been trained to lie flat there. A center part also cuts the face into two equal halves, and that perfect symmetry can look severe on a softer face.

Moving it feels strange at first, but a deep side part, or one just off center, gives the hair on the fuller side somewhere to rise. You get instant height at the root and a little asymmetry, and asymmetry is softening.

On fine or thinning hair, the part does one more job. A center part that has widened shows more scalp under a bright light. Move it over, even by an inch, and the line falls on hair that was never trained to lie flat, so it looks fuller. That's a styling effect and nothing more. It does nothing to the hair itself, and if your hair is suddenly shedding or thinning in patches, that's something to show your doctor or dermatologist.

On damp hair, take the tail of a comb and draw the new part back from your hairline, lined up with the arch of one eyebrow for a deep side part. Blow-dry the roots against the new direction first, then brush them back over. Drying against the part is what makes the roots stand up.

Will it want to fall back into the old groove? For the first few days, yes. But keep drying against it, and it settles.

I take one last look in the mirror by my front door before I go out, and a part that's gone flat is exactly what that mirror catches. Make that your check too.

And if you love your center part, keep it. Dry the roots from side to side so they don't lie down, and nudge it a little off center. You'll still get the lift.

With height at the root and a little asymmetry, your bob already looks softer. The ends are next, and they're where the old habits run deepest.

Secret number three. Stop curling the ends under.

You know the look. The ends are rolled under with a big round brush or a curling iron, then sprayed until nothing moves. Somewhere along the way, someone taught you that a finished bob curls under and stays put. For years, that was what polished meant.

But a rounded-under bob with no movement looks like a helmet. The curve closes around the face like a bowl, and because the hair never moves, it looks set rather than grown. That stiffness is what makes a bob look dated.

After a lifetime of dressing well, I'd tell you this is the change that freshens a bob the fastest.

So what do you do instead? Finish the ends straight, or bent very slightly out, with a flat iron. Take a small section, glide down slowly, and at the very end turn your wrist a little outward. The flat iron sits right on the hair, so the heat protectant matters even more here, and fine or color-treated hair wants the lower setting.

Then break up the line. Rub a little texture cream or texture spray between your fingertips and pinch the ends, so they sit in soft pieces instead of one solid curve. A texture product gives the hair a little grip and separation, without the wet shine of a gel.

Finish with a flexible-hold spray instead of a firm one. It keeps the shape but lets the hair move when you turn your head.

Do you love a little curve at the ends? Keep it. A soft bend is lovely. The only thing to give up is the hard, set roll.

Here's your check. Stand at the mirror and shake your head gently. The hair should swing and fall back into place. If it moves as one piece, there's too much curl or too much spray.

When the ends move, the whole bob looks lighter and more current, and someone may ask whether you've had it cut.

Once the ends swing, the front of your bob is where you turn heads.

Secret number four. Frame your face.

Look at how your bob falls in front. If it hangs evenly on both sides like a pair of curtains, it's covering your jaw, your ears and the earrings you picked out this morning.

When the jawline softens after 60, it's natural to want your hair to cover it. I understand that instinct. But a curtain of hair at the jaw draws the eye to the very place you're trying to hide, and it closes the face in.

It can feel bare for a day or two, but try tucking one side behind your ear. The side opposite your part, the one with less hair, is usually the right one. It shows the line of your jaw and lets an earring catch the light, and the bob instantly looks more deliberate. I love a tucked side with a small earring.

Then look at the front pieces. You want them to fall toward your cheekbones, because wherever the hair points, the eye follows, and the cheekbones are the most lifted part of the face. If your forehead feels exposed, a soft side-swept fringe softens it. A fringe is what many people call bangs, and side-swept means longer pieces that fall to one side instead of a blunt line across.

Take the words to your stylist. Ask for front pieces that land at your cheekbones, and a soft side-swept fringe, nothing blunt. If you've ever left the chair with something you didn't ask for, a clear sentence and a phone photo of the shape you want make you much easier to hear.

Not sure about a fringe? Ask for it a little longer than you think. It can always come shorter at the next visit, but it can't go back the other way.

Here's the check. Take two photos straight on in daylight, one with both sides down and one with a side tucked. You'll see which one looks more open.

With one side tucked, your face looks brighter and more open. The last secret has nothing to do with shape at all.

Secret number five. Make it shine.

Stand at a window and look at your hair in daylight. Does it catch the light, or does it look flat and all one shade? Dull, flat color adds more years than the cut ever will.

Shine is light bouncing off a smooth surface. Dull hair soaks the light up, and when that flatness sits right next to your face, your skin looks tired along with it.

What brings the light back? Start with a shine serum, a light, silky liquid that smooths the surface of the hair. Use a few drops on the ends only. Keep it off the roots, or you'll flatten the crown you just lifted.

Next, ask your colorist about a salon gloss. A gloss is a sheer finish your colorist puts over the hair to give it shine, and it's one of the easiest things to ask for at your next visit.

If you color your hair, look at the shade itself. Dark color can feel like the safe choice, but very dark, flat color, and flat black most of all, can look harsh against softer skin. Ask for one to two shades lighter than your natural shade before the gray, with warmth in it. Warmth means a hint of gold or honey, rather than an ashy or inky tone. If you color at home, do a patch test before every application, as the box says. If anything itches, burns or swells, stop and call your doctor.

And if your bob is gray, I think it's one of the most striking looks a woman can wear, as long as it stays bright. But gray can turn brassy, which means it picks up a yellow cast. A purple shampoo about once a week cancels that yellow. Once a week is enough.

Your check is the same window. Look again in daylight after a week or two. The crown and the ends should catch the light.

Hair that shines looks cared for, and the color right next to your face looks fresh instead of faded.

You might be wondering whether these five secrets work on very fine hair or very thick hair. They do, with small changes. Fine hair wants less product and the lower heat setting. Thick hair can take more lift at the crown and a little more texture at the ends.

But none of it holds if the shape has grown out. A bob keeps its line with a trim every six to eight weeks, so book the next one before you leave the chair.

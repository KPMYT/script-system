TEST (iter-20)

There are ten things in your winter closet right now that make an elegant woman look dated, and you'll put at least one of them on this week. Under all those layers, they make you look wider and older than you are.

The best-dressed women over fifty, the ones who look polished in a frozen parking lot in January, have stopped wearing every one. It isn't their age or their budget. They simply know what each piece does to a woman's line. Most of these swaps come from your own closet, and your mirror shows it that same evening.

When a woman looks bulky in winter, the fault is never hers. It's the shape, the length and the fabric. You've stood by the front door, buttoned up, wondering why you looked so much wider than in summer.

So let's start with the one we reach for first when the frost comes, and get wrong most often.

Number one is the big puffer with wide quilting, those thick padded bands that run straight across from the collar to the hem. You've caught yourself in a shop window on a cold afternoon, and the coat looked older and wider than the woman inside it. That isn't your imagination. Each of those bands carries the eye across your body instead of up it, and a whole row of them makes you look wider and shorter at once.

We buy them because we believe the puffier the coat, the warmer we'll be, and warm is all a winter coat has to do. But the width of those bands adds bulk to your outline, not elegance. You can be every bit as warm in something slimmer. Wide quilting also belongs on a ski slope, and it was everywhere a few winters ago, so on a town coat it dates fast.

Instead, look for a puffer that reaches the knee, with a fine diagonal quilt, the kind that runs in slim chevrons, and a belt at the waist. The padding runs in narrow slants, and the belt gives you back your waist. It's just as warm in a January parking lot. Or, if you'd rather, step into a proper wool coat in a dark neutral. Either way, the next shop window you pass shows you, not the coat.

Once the outline is right, the eye travels down, and that's exactly where most of us stop paying attention.

Let me ask you something. When you pull on jeans in winter, where do the hems go? For a lot of us, the answer is number two: skinny jeans tucked into chunky shearling boots, the big soft sheepskin ones with the denim pushed down inside. It feels safe and sensible. But a heavy boot stops the eye at the calf, and the tucked jean points straight at it, so your leg seems to end there, shorter than it really is. The look also belongs to one particular moment, and that moment has passed.

You'd think those soft boots are at least the safe choice for ice. Yet many of them have smooth, flat soles that slide. Grip comes from the sole, not the bulk.

Most of us already have a pair of straight or wide-leg dark jeans at the back of the closet. Let the hem fall over a sleek leather ankle boot, and a plain Chelsea boot is the easiest of all. It has a lovely history, too. Joseph Sparkes Hall, bootmaker to Queen Victoria, is credited with the elastic-sided ankle boot during her reign, so she could pull hers on without fuss. The name Chelsea only came a century later, from London in the sixties.

When you walk, the hem should move over the boot, never vanish inside it. On any winter boot, choose a non-slip lugged sole and a low block heel, and a boot you can pull on sitting down without a struggle. On ice, grip comes before style every time. Get those right, and your leg runs all the way to the floor, and you walk out the door steady.

The next one matters more than anything else in this video, and it comes down to a single line.

Number three is the coat that stops at the hip: the car coat, and its cousin, the fleece zipped right up to the chin. A short coat feels brisk and practical, the coat of a woman with places to be. I understand why we choose it. But the eye takes the edge of a coat for the edge of you. So a hem that lands at the hip draws a line straight across the widest point, and you look wider and shorter in one stroke. On a coat, the hem is the decision that matters most.

We tell ourselves a short coat is younger, and easier for driving. Except it's the hem that decides what the whole coat does to you, and a longer coat sits perfectly comfortably in a car seat.

What you want is a wool coat that stops at mid-thigh or at the knee, in camel, navy or charcoal. Those quiet colors go with everything else in this video. In the shop, make sure the shoulder seam sits on your shoulder bone, never slipping down your arm.

And don't trust the front mirror alone. Turn sideways and look back over your shoulder at your seat. The hem should clear it, never land on it, and never stop at the widest point of your hips. That one look over the shoulder tells you more than ten minutes facing the glass.

The first time you wear it, someone will say, "What a beautiful coat," and they'll mean you.

Stand at your mirror in your coat for a moment and look at what sits just under your chin. For many of us, that's number four, the chunky infinity scarf, the thick knitted loop wound twice around the neck. It parks a solid block right under your face, exactly where you want light and the least bulk. Long lines lengthen and lines that run across widen, and a loop is one big line across.

You'd think the thicker the scarf, the warmer you'll be. But fine cashmere is warm for its weight, and the extra thickness mostly adds bulk where your face wants light. In the coldest cities, the chic women wear thin scarves and let them hang.

Reach instead for a long, fine scarf in cashmere or silk, in burgundy, soft gray or ivory. Drape it open down the front of your coat, so it makes two long lines from your shoulders to your hips. Your whole frame looks taller and narrower at once. Just keep the ends clear of car doors and escalators.

And you may keep the thick loop. It's perfect for the dog walk, the garden or a snowy country road. It's only the town coat and the good wool that it fights.

Number five isn't a piece of clothing at all, and yet it sits above every coat you own. It's the heavy winter face: a full matte foundation under a set of lash extensions. A full matte base sits flat. It takes the light off instead of letting it move across, and in winter, when daylight is already thin, flat looks tired. Lash extensions do the same from the other side, too dark and too heavy for daylight.

We reach for more coverage because winter seems to ask for it. But more coverage means less light, and less base with more glow looks rested.

Swap the matte base for a tinted moisturizer and the black mascara for brown. Then trade the dark lip for a soft rose or a berry. Let the extensions grow out, or have them removed at the salon, and never pull them off yourself. Then look in a window in daylight, not under the bathroom bulb, because the window tells the truth.

Nobody will ask what you've done. They'll simply say you look rested.

Somebody told most of us, years ago, that matching was the mark of a lady. And once, it was. Number six is the whole look bought together. It might be the matching fleece set in one soft color, or the bag, the belt and the lipstick chosen in one shade. Or it's the new boot, the new bag and the new color, all worn at once, because the shop window showed them that way.

But the eye needs somewhere to stop. When everything matches, it slides straight off you, and you vanish into one color. Contrast is what stops the eye. And a trend worn head to toe dates before the winter is out, taking the whole outfit with it.

So let one piece argue with the rest. Try a soft cashmere sweater over a faux leather skirt, matte against shine, or a dark wool coat over plain dark jeans. If there's a new trend piece you love this winter, the bag or the boot, let it be that one different piece. Wear it with the classics already in your closet, in navy, gray or chocolate brown. A second trend piece goes back on the hanger. One new thing among quiet classics looks chosen, never copied.

"But shouldn't my bag match my shoes?" Two things may match. It's five matching things where it goes wrong.

That's when you'll hear the compliment every woman wants in winter: "You look so put together."

The next one starts on a small screen, late in the evening.

You saw a look on your phone and loved it. You found every piece, put it all on, and somehow, on you, it looked borrowed. That's number seven, the copy-paste outfit, every piece bought and worn exactly the way the woman on the screen wore it.

We think, if it looks good on her, it'll look good on me. But nothing in it was chosen for your coloring, your proportions or your life, and people feel that, even when they can't name it. The picture was put together for somebody else. The best-dressed women never copy a look whole.

Take the look, then change one thing so it's yours. You might swap the accessories for warm cognac leather, a cognac shoe, belt and bag against navy or gray, and the whole outfit lifts. Or keep it simpler still and change one accessory from the picture. Just one. That's all it takes for the outfit to stop being hers and start being yours.

Elegant in winter was never about owning more. It's about giving the eye one place to land, and the next one is a place the eye lands all day long.

Number eight is about your hands. In winter, they're the one part of you on show from morning to night. They hold the bag, pull on the gloves and pass the cup across the counter. So busy nail art, the long nails with glitter and little stones and a different design on every finger, fights with your coat all day. When I was designing, the models' hands were kept plain on the runway, so nothing competed with the clothes.

Long, decorated nails feel elegant and fun, I know. But short nails in one color look far more polished, and they go with every outfit you own. Choose a true red, a deep burgundy, or a nude close to your own skin.

And you keep your color, even a dark one. A near-black for the evening is beautiful, and it's still one color. It's the busyness that goes, never the color. Tonight, take the old polish off, paint one shade, and you'll have elegant hands before bed.

Where do you think the parka was born? Number nine is the fur-hood parka worn in town. It comes from the Arctic. The word reached English through Russian, from the Nenets people of Arctic Russia. And that fur-ruffed hood was made by Arctic peoples to keep the wind off the face in extreme cold. It was built for the tundra and the mountain, not the high street.

In town, it looks dated the moment you step out of the car. And the bulk of the hood and the body hides the line of everything underneath it. That's why elegant women are not wearing it on the high street, however cold the morning.

We tell ourselves it's the warmest, most sensible coat for a cold town winter. But for errands and the walk from the car, a lined wool coat and a hat keep you warm, and look elegant doing it. A tailored wool coat in charcoal and a cashmere hat will do it. The hat does the hood's job, and it frames your face instead of hiding it.

Save the parka for the day you're truly on the mountain.

Here's the last one, and you can try it before you leave the house. Put on your coat, turn the collar up and stand at arm's length from the mirror. Now, how much of your jewelry can you see?

For most of us, the answer is none, and that's number ten: the jewelry nobody can see. The tiny studs and the fine chain vanish the moment the collar goes up or the scarf goes on. In winter, the coat is the outfit, and fine everyday jewelry disappears beneath it. So from a distance, the whole look seems unfinished.

Small, delicate jewelry always feels like the tasteful, safe choice. But under a winter collar, delicate simply means invisible. One piece scaled for the coat is what shows.

Dress the coat itself. Pin a brooch on the lapel, or wear earrings in a jewel tone, something that catches the light at a distance. A coat needs a finish the way a room needs a lamp. Old brooches turn up on trays at flea markets, and in many a mother's jewelry box.

Then stand at arm's length again. One shine should show. If nothing does, add one, and if three do, take two away.

A charcoal coat, a hat and one jewel, and heads turn in the street.

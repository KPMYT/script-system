Here's the whole shopping list, and most of it's free.

Start with the sawdust, from untreated solid wood only. Pine, oak, poplar, whatever you've been cutting lately.

Never pressure-treated lumber, plywood, MDF, or anything painted or finished. Those carry glues and chemicals that don't belong in anybody's wood stove.

If treated boards went through your vac lately, empty it and start fresh.

Next, paraffin wax, the plain household kind that comes in blocks in the canning aisle. Figure about $4 for a one-pound block at the big box store.

A full batch takes six blocks, so call it $25 of wax.

Old unscented candle stubs work too, and they're free, as long as you fish the wicks out while they melt. I'd still buy the blocks. No wicks to chase around the can, and no smell.

Egg cartons cost you nothing. Save your own, ask the neighbors, ask the church kitchen. Paper cartons only, never foam.

Then the gift wrapping, such as it is. Small brown kraft gift bags with paper handles run about $24 for a pack of 50.

Paper tags on string are about $6 for a hundred.

Put the bag and the tag together and you're well under a dollar a pack. Prices subject to change.

Last, and optional, a handful of small, dry pine cones from the yard. They're free, and they're what makes the bag look like a gift. Cinnamon sticks do the same job with a smell, but they cost money.

For tools, there's no saw and no drill anywhere in this one. You need an old pot with a clean coffee can or a big soup can standing in it. That's a homemade double boiler.

That pot never cooks food again, so grab one from the thrift store, or retire the one with the wobbly handle. Nobody's going to miss it.

An electric burner does the heating. If your stove's gas, plug in a cheap hot plate instead.

Add a pair of pliers to grip the hot can, an old spoon, a serrated bread knife, gloves and safety glasses. A kitchen thermometer is nice to have, but you can skip it.

Cut the lid and the front flap off every carton, so you're left with a tray of 12 cups.

Set the trays on foil or a flattened cardboard box. Melted wax runs, and once it's on your bench, it isn't coming off.

Now fill each cup with sawdust and press it down with your thumb. Add more. Press again.

Leave a little room at the top. A cup packed to the brim leaves the wax sitting on top instead of soaking through.

All 15 trays take about half an hour.

Once the trays are full, it's time to melt the wax. Put a few inches of water in the pot, stand the can in the water, and set it on the electric burner turned low.

When the water's steaming, break a block of wax into the can and let it melt slowly. Stir it now and then with the old spoon.

Paraffin melts well below boiling, so if that water's at a rolling boil, it's too hot. Turn it down.

This is the one part of the job that can actually hurt you, so take it seriously. Melt wax only in the double boiler, on low heat. Never in a pan straight on a burner, and never over a gas flame or any open flame.

Wax that gets too hot can catch fire. Keep the gloves and glasses on, keep a pot lid within reach the whole time, and don't walk away from melting wax.

If it ever does catch, put the lid on it and turn the burner off. Never water. Water on burning wax makes it spatter, and that spreads the fire.

Pouring is next. Grip the can with the pliers, gloves on, and pour about a tablespoon slowly over each cup.

Let it soak down before you move on. If it's sitting on top, wait a minute and add a little more.

Done right, the wax reaches the bottom of the cup, with the sawdust just showing on top.

The first tray is slow. After that, figure about 6 minutes a tray.

Now the dressing up, and it has to happen while the wax is still soft. Press one small pine cone into the top of every cup. It burns right along with the rest.

Then leave the trays flat overnight to harden.

Next morning, take the bread knife and cut each tray apart into single cups.

Before a single one goes in a bag, test one. Take it outdoors to a fire pit, set it under a little kindling, and light the paper edge, not the top.

It should burn about 5 to 10 minutes on its own. That's long enough to catch the kindling or get a wood stove going.

If it won't hold a flame, it didn't get enough wax. Add another tablespoon to each cup left on that tray.

Packing is where a pile of pucks becomes a Christmas gift. Six to a bag, fold the top over twice, and tie the tag through the fold.

Handwrite the tag. Something like, "Fire starters. Light the paper edge. One per fire."

You'll need a pencil for that, and mine's never behind my ear when I need it.

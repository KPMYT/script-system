### Script X

Strengths (quoted):
- Line 1: "Every board you cut leaves a little of itself behind, and most of it ends up in the bottom of your shop vac." A warm, image-led first line that lands on the $300 within three sentences.
- Line 27: "And don't walk away from melting wax. Not to answer the phone, not to warm up the coffee that went cold on the bench an hour ago." The safety rule becomes a real moment in a real shop. It uses the brief's shop detail and invents nothing.
- Line 47: "Somebody's going to say nobody pays $10 for sawdust. They're right. Nobody does. They pay $10 for a Christmas present that's ready to hand over, with a tag already on it." This answers the objection a listener actually has, and it is the best selling line in either script.
- Line 15: "A screw in a fire starter is no gift to anybody." Line 7: "Foam is plastic, and plastic has no business in anybody's fire." Each rule comes with a short reason, which is how a maker talks.
- Line 31: "plan on melting a fresh block every two and a half trays or so." A practical pacing cue that a listener at the stove can use (the maths is right: 30 cups a pound, 12 cups a tray).

Where it stops flowing:
- Lines 3-11: "The first thing is the sawdust... Second is the wax... Third is egg cartons... Then the packing... One more thing". The materials list is numbered out loud. It's well voiced, but it is still a list.
- Line 18: "Now, back to those egg cartons." The CTA sits after the sawdust sort, and the egg cartons weren't the current step, so the bridge points at something the viewer wasn't holding.
- Lines 45-57: about four minutes of figures in a row (Etsy range, his price, cost per pack, batch total, Etsy fee, advice, where to sell). Online selling also comes up twice, at line 53 ("Online is a different deal") and again at line 57 ("Online comes after all that").
- Line 33: "Skip it if you have to. It's what makes the pack read as a gift, so I wouldn't." This says the same thing as the sentence before it, and the line runs awkwardly.

### Script Y

Strengths (quoted):
- Line 1: "The sawdust sitting in your shop vac right now could fill 30 little brown gift bags of Christmas fire starters, and at $10 a bag, that's $300 if every bag sells." The fastest, most concrete hook of the two.
- Lines 51-53: "how full you pack them decides whether the wax soaks all the way down or just sits on top like a lid. / Quick word while the dust settles..." This opens a question, pitches the book, then comes back with "Right, the cartons." It's a smart mid-roll.
- Line 103: "The bag, the tag and your handwriting, that's the under-a-dollar part from the start." A clean callback to the hook.
- Line 121: "Now, somebody at the table is going to tell you the big-box store has a bag of 72 starter cubes for about $10." Putting the price comparison in a buyer's mouth makes it a scene instead of a statistic.
- Line 109: "a first batch doesn't have either." An honest, specific reason for not charging top price.

Where it stops flowing:
- Lines 23-27, 63, 87: many one-sentence paragraphs, such as "Paper tags on string are about $6 for a hundred." and "All 15 trays take about half an hour." They read like bullet points spoken aloud. The rhythm is choppy, and by ear it sounds like a list.
- Line 101: "You'll need a pencil for that, and mine's never behind my ear when I need it." This gag is dropped in the middle of the packing step. It stalls the flow and feels forced.
- Lines 65-71: the viewer is told to set the can on the burner and start melting, and only after that does "This is the one part of the job that can actually hurt you" arrive. The safety block comes after the action it governs.
- Lines 39-43: "Take 15 egg cartons with 12 cups each... 180... 30 packs... a tablespoon... 30 cups... six pounds." These are six numbers in a row, before any step, and they're hard to hold by ear.
- Line 125: "That's a hell of a markup on cardboard." This is mild profanity for an older audience and for advertisers. X's "heck" does the same job.

## 3. Facts and risk

Both scripts get the core fire safety right: a double boiler only, low heat, an electric burner (a hot plate on a gas stove), no open flame, gloves and glasses, a lid within reach, never leaving the wax, and for a wax fire "lid on, burner off, never water". The test burn is outdoors and lit at the paper edge.

Gaps I'd fix (shared unless noted; this is my reading, nothing here is measured):
- **Neither says to keep the pot's water topped up.** The double boiler is what keeps the wax safe, and a pot that boils dry over a long afternoon of 15 trays removes that protection. Add one line.
- **Neither says to turn the burner off when the pouring is done.** X line 35 says "just walk away" shortly after "don't walk away from melting wax". That's fine once the heat is off, so say so first.
- **Y never says to melt more wax.** It says "break a block of wax into the can" once, but the batch needs six blocks. X covers this ("a fresh block every two and a half trays").
- **Re-waxing a failed tray** (X line 39, Y line 95) means re-melting wax the next day. Both imply it without saying it. That's minor.
- X line 37 adds sensible knife safety ("keep your other hand behind the blade"). It isn't in the brief, but it's harmless and good.
- Nothing either script says is factually wrong about paraffin, and neither uses the banned claim that wax catches "without ever smoking first".

## 4. Verdict

**Publish X, by a moderate margin (49 vs 46).** X sounds more like one man at a bench. Its safety rules come before the risky step, it covers the full six-block melt, and it has the strongest selling line ("They pay $10 for a Christmas present"). Y has the sharper hook, the better mid-roll and a neat callback, but its one-line paragraphs make it choppy by ear. It also has a forced gag, safety after the melt has begun, and a "hell".

- **X: light edit.** Smooth the materials list so it isn't numbered out loud. Fix the "back to those egg cartons" bridge. Merge the two online-selling passages and trim the money run. Add the water top-up and burner-off lines.
- **Y: light edit (on the heavy side).** Join the one-line paragraphs into spoken runs. Cut the pencil gag. Move the safety block ahead of "put a few inches of water in the pot". Add the melt-more-blocks and water top-up lines. Swap "hell" for "heck". Spread the batch arithmetic out.

Best of both: X as the base, with Y's hook sentence (line 1), Y's "Quick word while the dust settles" mid-roll setup and Y's "under-a-dollar part" callback.

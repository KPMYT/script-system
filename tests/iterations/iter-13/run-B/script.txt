Every board you cut leaves a little of itself behind, and most of it ends up in the bottom of your shop vac. Pack that sawdust into paper egg cartons, soak it with melted wax, and fifteen cartons make thirty gift packs of Christmas fire starters. At $10 a pack, that's $300 if they all sell. Here's what you need.

The first thing is the sawdust, and you've already got it. Pine, oak, poplar, whatever you've been cutting, as long as it's untreated solid wood. Never pressure-treated lumber, plywood, MDF, or anything painted or finished, because the glues and chemicals in those don't belong in somebody's wood stove. If treated boards went through the vac lately, empty it and start fresh. Fine dust works, small shavings work. You don't buy a single board for this one. Nothing to cut, nothing that spins, and no good board wasted on something nobody wants.

Second is the wax. Get household paraffin, the plain blocks they sell in the canning aisle at a big-box store, about $4 for a one-pound block. You'll want six of them for the whole batch, so call it about $25. Prices subject to change. Old unscented candle stubs work too, and they're free, but you'll be fishing the wicks out as they melt. I'd buy the blocks. No wicks, no smell, and you know exactly what's going into the can.

Third is egg cartons, and those cost nothing. Save your own, ask the neighbors, ask whoever runs the church kitchen. Paper ones only, never foam. Foam is plastic, and plastic has no business in anybody's fire. Each carton holds twelve cups, so fifteen of them gives you a hundred and eighty starters. That's thirty packs of six.

Then the packing. Small brown kraft gift bags with paper handles run about $24 for fifty, and paper tags with string are about $6 for a hundred. Together that's well under a dollar a pack, and that bag and tag are what turn a pile of wax pucks into a Christmas present.

One more thing, and it's optional. A handful of small, dry pine cones from the yard. They're free, and pressed into the top they make the pack look like a gift instead of a craft experiment. Cinnamon sticks do the same job and bring a nice smell with them, but they cost money.

For tools, there's no saw and no drill on this build, and no cut list either. The recipe stands in for the cut list. You need an old pot and a clean coffee can or a big soup can that stands up inside it. That's your homemade double boiler. Once that pot melts wax, it never cooks food again, so pick one up at the thrift store or use the one with the wobbly handle. You need an electric burner, and if your kitchen stove is gas, get a cheap plug-in hot plate instead. Then a pair of pliers to grip the hot can, an old spoon, a serrated bread knife, gloves and safety glasses. A kitchen thermometer is nice to have, but you can do this without one.

Now the build, and the first job is sorting the sawdust. Tip the vac out onto a scrap board or an old sheet and go through it by hand. Pick out the screws, the staples, the chunks, the knots and anything metal. A screw in a fire starter is no gift to anybody. Keep the dust and the small shavings, nothing bigger than your fingernail. Put a mask on for this, because the fine dust goes straight up into the air the minute you start stirring it around.

Cut the lid and the front flap off every carton, so what's left is a flat tray of twelve cups. Set the trays out on foil or a flattened cardboard box. Melted wax runs, and once it's on your bench, it isn't coming off.

Next, fill the cups. Drop sawdust into each one and press it down with your thumb. Add more, press it again. Leave a little room at the top, though. Pack a cup to the brim and the wax just sits on top instead of soaking down through it, and the sawdust underneath stays dry. All fifteen trays take about half an hour. It's slow, quiet work, and it goes quicker than you'd think once you get a rhythm.

Now the wax, and this is the part to slow down on. Put a few inches of water in the old pot, stand the can in the water, and set the whole thing on the electric burner turned low. Melt wax only this way, in the double boiler on low heat, never in a pan straight on a burner and never over a gas flame or any open flame, because wax that gets too hot can catch fire. Gloves on, glasses on, and keep a pot lid within reach the whole time.

Bring the water up until it's steaming. Then break a block of wax into the can and let it melt slow, stirring now and then with the old spoon. Paraffin melts well below boiling, so if that water is at a rolling boil, it's too hot. Turn it down. And don't walk away from melting wax. Not to answer the phone, not to warm up the coffee that went cold on the bench an hour ago. If it ever does catch fire, put the lid on it and turn the burner off. Never water. Water on burning wax makes it spatter and spreads the fire.

Once the wax is clear and runny, it's time to pour. Grip the can with the pliers, gloves still on, and pour about a tablespoon slowly over each cup. Give it a moment to soak down before you move on to the next one. If it pools on top, wait a minute and add a little more. Done right, the wax reaches the bottom of the cup and you can still just see sawdust on top.

The first tray is slow while you get the feel for it. After that, each tray takes about six minutes. One pound of wax does about thirty cups, so plan on melting a fresh block every two and a half trays or so. Six pounds gets you through all fifteen.

Then comes the dressing up. While the wax is still soft, press one small pine cone into the top of each cup. It burns along with everything else, and it's the bit that makes somebody pick the bag up off the table and look at it. Skip it if you have to. It's what makes the pack read as a gift, so I wouldn't.

Now leave the trays flat overnight to harden. Don't stack them, don't move them, just walk away.

Next morning, take the serrated bread knife and cut each tray apart into twelve single cups. Let the teeth do the work, and keep your other hand behind the blade, because a knife that slips through cardboard keeps going.

Before you bag a single one, test one. Do it outdoors in a fire pit, never inside. Set it under a little kindling and light the paper edge, not the top. It should burn about five to ten minutes on its own, which is plenty to catch the kindling or get a wood stove going. If it won't hold a flame, it didn't get enough wax. Add another tablespoon to the rest of that tray, let it set, and test again.

Once they pass, pack them up. Six to a bag, fold the top over twice, and tie the tag through the fold. Write every tag by hand. Something like "Fire starters. Light the paper edge. One per fire." Handwriting is what makes it read as handmade, and handmade is the only reason anybody pays more than the big-box price.

And while we're on the big-box price, look at what's out there. A big-box store lists a bag of seventy-two starter cubes for about $10. Fair enough. I've got no quarrel with that. But a boutique brand lists four discs of cedar sawdust and wax, the same two things you just put in an egg carton, for about $13. That's over $3 a disc, and most of it pays for the box. That's a heck of a markup on sawdust. Your six-pack sits between the two, and it has the one thing neither of them has, a person's handwriting on the tag. Somebody walking a December market isn't shopping for fire starters. They're shopping for a gift for the person who's hard to buy for.

Now the money. On Etsy, handmade packs of wax fire starters are listed at about $10 to $25. The closest one to this build, six sawdust and wax cubes, is listed at about $10. I'd price yours at $10 a pack on a market table and $12 online. Not the top of the range. That top end belongs to sellers with a photo setup and a stack of reviews.

Somebody's going to say nobody pays $10 for sawdust. They're right. Nobody does. They pay $10 for a Christmas present that's ready to hand over, with a tag already on it.

Your cost per pack is the wax, the bag and the tag, and that comes to less than $2. The sawdust, the cartons and the pine cones are free. So at $10, you keep a bit over $8 a pack at a market, before whatever the show charges for a booth.

For the whole batch, that's thirty packs. If all thirty sell, it's $300. Take off about $45 for the wax, bags and tags, and you're left with about $255. The time is an afternoon of work, plus a night for the wax to harden. That's my estimate, not a promise. And if they don't all sell, your Christmas presents are handled.

Online is a different deal. Etsy keeps a bit over a tenth of a $12 sale, and shipping comes on top of that, so a $12 pack has to pay for its own box and postage. Sell hand to hand first, and list online once the local supply runs out.

One piece of advice before you buy six pounds of wax. Make one carton first. That's twelve starters, two bags. Put them out and see whether your town buys them. If it does, make the other fourteen.

So where do they sell? Start with a holiday market or a church bazaar in November and December, where $10 is impulse money. Set a few out on the table at work. Put them on Facebook Marketplace for local pickup, which costs you no fee. Online comes after all that. And come summer, the same puck with a different tag sells to campers as a campfire starter.

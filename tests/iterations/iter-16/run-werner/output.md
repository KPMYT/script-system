SCRIPT TYPE: Talking Head

The reason your bathroom sink stopper sticks is on its stem, and the cure is a thin film of silicone plumber's grease from a tube that costs about five dollars. You push the rod behind the faucet, the stopper drops, and there it stays. You pull, and it stays up. So you work the knob harder, and a month later it sticks again. You want the cause gone for good, not a fix that comes back. A plumber will come out for this, and the visit alone costs more than the whole repair. But grease will not mend a stem that has cracked through, a pivot ball worn flat, or a drain body so corroded the nut will not seat. Those need a new part. There are four causes, and most people have never looked where they hide.

The first cause is hair, and it starts under the sink, where you cannot see it. Most American bathroom sinks have the lift-rod kind of stopper, the one worked by a rod behind the faucet, so we start there. Open the cabinet and look at the back of the drain pipe, just below the basin. A thin metal rod goes into that pipe through a nut. That is the pivot rod. It crosses the inside of the pipe and lifts the stopper from below, and it sits in the water every time the sink drains.

Now think about what goes down a bathroom sink. Shaving, brushing, the morning wash. One hair comes down and hooks on that rod. The next one catches on the first. Then another, and another, until a gray rope has wrapped itself around the rod and around the bottom of the stopper's stem. The stopper wants to slide down past the rod, and the rope will not let it.

Most people blame the pipe. It is too narrow, they say, or the house is old. But the pipe is fine. The trouble is a rod sitting in moving water with nothing on it to stop hair taking hold.

You can check this without a single tool. Pull the stopper straight up. On some sinks it lifts right out. On others the rod underneath holds it, and it comes free once the rod is out. If it comes, look at the stem. A gray, matted sleeve of hair around the bottom is the first cause, found.

And this is where the money comes in. A plumber charges about $100 just to show up, more or less depending on where you live, and that is before any work. For this one, he lifts the stopper and pulls the hair off. That money was never for the work. It was for knowing where the hair hides.

The second cause is in your toothbrush cup. Toothpaste goes down the drain every morning, and so does soap. Each one on its own washes away. Together, on a wet metal stem, they do something else. They set into a paste. And if your water is hard, it lays a thin white scale on top of that paste every time the basin drains, until the paste has become a crust.

You feel it before you see it. The stopper drags. You pull the knob, the stopper rises halfway, and it stops there, tilted, with water creeping past it. You push it down, and it needs a second push.

Lift it out and run a thumbnail down the stem. A chalky, rough coat, white or a little gray, is this cause. Often it is there together with the hair, the crust holding the hair in place and the hair holding more crust.

So people scrub it. They take an old toothbrush to the stem, the crust comes off, the stopper moves like new, and they are pleased. By the next season it is back. Scrubbing only clears the metal. It does nothing to stop the paste setting again on the same bare stem. What goes onto that clean stem afterwards is the real repair.

The third cause is not dirt at all. It is a wrench. Go back under the sink and look at the nut where the pivot rod enters the pipe. Behind that nut is a small gasket, and the whole thing belongs hand tight. Nothing more. But somebody, at some point, put a wrench on it and gave it one more turn, because tighter feels safer. That extra turn clamps the rod so it cannot pivot. You move the knob, the strap moves, and the stopper does not.

Right beside the nut is the clevis. That is the flat metal strap with a row of holes along it, joining the lift rod behind your faucet to the pivot rod under the basin. It is held by one small screw. Over the years that screw works loose, and the rod slips in the strap. Then you pull the knob and it has nothing to pull against. It goes up and down freely, and the stopper sits where it sits.

There is a quieter version of the same mistake, and it costs more. If the nut is too loose, or if that wrench flattened the gasket behind it, the nut drips. Not much. Once, every time the sink drains. The drop falls onto the floor of the cabinet, and most sink cabinets have a floor of particle board. It drinks the water, it swells, and one day it crumbles under the cleaning bottles. A small water-damage job in one room runs about $150 to $500, depending on how long the drip ran. All from one drop at one nut.

Put a dry tissue under that nut and run the sink. If the tissue is wet afterwards, that is your nut. If it is dry and the knob still moves without the stopper, look at the screw on the strap. Either way, you now know which one it is, and both are yours to fix.

The fourth cause belongs to the other kind of stopper. Some sinks have no rod behind the faucet at all. You press the stopper down to close it, and press it again to open it. No rod in the pipe, no strap, and so no hair rope. People with this kind sometimes think they are safe.

But under the cap of that stopper is a small cartridge, and inside it is a spring. That spring sits in standing water, day in and day out. Hard water leaves its scale on the coils, a little more every week. The click you feel when you press it goes soft. Then it goes mushy. Then one morning you press, and nothing happens. The stopper stays down, the basin fills, and you are fishing at it with a fingernail.

Here is the good news. The spring is almost never broken. It is buried. Under that white scale is a perfectly good spring that only needs to be let out. When your push button stops clicking, do not go shopping for a new drain yet.

So. Four causes, and one thing answers all of them. Silicone plumber's grease. It is a small tube, about $5, and you will find it in the plumbing aisle, next to the washers. The one word to look for on the tube is silicone.

What it does is simple. It leaves a thin film on the metal that water cannot wash off. Hair comes down, meets that film, and slides instead of hooking. Toothpaste and soap reach the stem and cannot get a grip to set. And a spring sitting in standing water moves inside its film. It goes on O-rings, springs and anything that slides while wet.

Now, the trap on the store shelf. Near the grease you will see petroleum jelly and cans of spray lubricant, and they look like the same idea. They are not. Petroleum swells rubber. Silicone does not. Put petroleum jelly on that gasket behind the pivot nut, and the gasket softens. The O-rings soften with it. Soon the nut that was dry starts to drip onto that particle board again. Read the tube. Silicone, and nothing else.

In the German trade this is called armature grease, because it goes on taps and valves, and it is standard kit in a German plumber's bag. And that is the real difference between the two houses. Americans tend to wait for the stopper to stick, and then fix it. In Germany it is usual to grease it before it ever does. Same stopper, same water, same hair. The only thing that changes is when the tube comes out.

The lift-rod kind comes apart in about 20 minutes, with a towel and your two hands.

Before anything else, one question. Did anyone pour a chemical drain cleaner down this sink this week? If they did, gloves on and goggles on before your hands go near that pipe, because that cleaner can still be sitting in the trap. And nothing else goes down after it. One cleaner at a time, always.

Empty the cabinet. Lay an old towel on its floor, because a little water comes out when the nut comes off. Find the pivot nut on the back of the drain pipe and turn it counterclockwise by hand. If it will not move, use pliers, and go gently. Slide the nut back along the rod, and pull the rod straight out of the pipe. Now go up top. The stopper lifts straight out.

Clean the stem with an old toothbrush and a drop of dish soap. The hair, the paste, the crust, all of it comes away. Then take a plastic drain strip, the thin one with little barbs along its sides, about $3. Feed it down the open drain and pull it up slowly. Whatever got past the stopper comes up with it. It will not be pretty. It will be satisfying.

Wipe the pivot rod dry. Put a thin film of grease on the ball of the rod, and on the gasket behind the nut. Thin is the word. Grease piled up only collects the next hair.

Now back together. Drop the stopper into the drain. Feed the rod back through the pipe so its tip passes through the loop at the bottom of the stopper stem. Reach up and push the stopper up and down once by hand. You will feel it catch on the rod. Thread the nut back on, hand tight, two fingers, no wrench. Then the strap. Move the clevis so the lift rod sits in the hole where the knob fully opens and fully closes the stopper, and tighten its little screw.

Test it. Open, closed, open, with one finger on the knob. It moves. It seals. It stays where you put it, and it will keep doing that.

The push button is even quicker. Press the stopper so it sits up. Then turn the cap counterclockwise. A wet cap is slippery, and a rubber jar grip helps more than you would think. Under the cap is the cartridge. Unscrew it from the crossbar down in the drain and lift it out.

Now a bowl of warm tap water, with citric acid stirred in. That is the plain white powder on the canning shelf, about $10 to $15 a pound. Warm, not boiling, so nothing warps. And nothing else in that bowl. No vinegar, no bleach, no other cleaner. One cleaner, on its own, every time.

Leave the cartridge in until the scale goes soft. Rinse it, and brush off what is left with the toothbrush. You will see the coils again, clean and bright.

Dry it. A dab of grease on the spring and on the threads. Screw it back onto the crossbar, cap on, and press. Click. That firm little click you had forgotten about is back.

In Germany, citric acid is the usual household answer to scale. Hausmittel, a household remedy. It costs very little, and it does the job.

Sometimes you lift the stopper out and grease is not the answer. The stem has cracked through. The ball on the pivot rod is worn flat on one side, so it wobbles in the nut. Or the drain body is corroded so badly that the nut will not seat, however gently you turn it. Grease cannot put metal back.

That is still not a disaster. A new pop-up assembly comes in one bag, with the stopper, the rod and the strap. The part costs $10 to $25, and it fits the old drain body the same way the old parts came out. Everything you just learned, in the same order. A plumber fitting that same bag can charge up to a few hundred dollars. The bag is identical. The difference is twenty minutes on your knees.

There is one place to stop. If the drain body itself has rusted through, the whole drain comes out, together with the P-trap, that U-shaped bend under the sink. That one is a call. Knowing where your job ends is part of doing it well.

Which brings me to the German word I like best for all of this. Wartung, maintenance, the check before the thing breaks. Once a year, lift each stopper in the house, wipe the stem, and put a fresh film on the ball of the pivot rod. A push button gets a dab on its spring. About five minutes a sink.

I do mine on the day the heating goes off in spring. I go round the house checking what leaks, what drips and what squeaks, and the stoppers are on that list. It is not a chore. It is the difference between a stopper that sticks next winter and one that simply keeps working.

Think about what this saved. The visit you did not need. The new drain you never had to buy. And a small tube that sits in the bathroom drawer for years, because a thin film goes a long way. The stopper was never the problem. Nobody had told you about its stem.

One small thing tonight. Lift the stopper out of your bathroom sink and look at the stem. That is all. Just look.

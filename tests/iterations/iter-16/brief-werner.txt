TITLE: Why Your Sink Stopper Gets Stuck — The $5 Grease Every German Plumber Carries
CHANNEL: Werner Finkbeiner
BRIEF (talking points):
- The problem: the bathroom sink stopper sticks: push the rod behind the faucet and it stays down, pull and it stays up. Most people never lift the stopper out and look at its stem; that is where the problem hides. The fix that keeps it from coming back is a small tube of silicone plumber's grease, about $5 (the title's figure; the price guide has no row for it).
- Money: a plumber's visit is about $100 just to show up (price guide: $50 to $250, about $100 common), before any work. A new pop-up assembly (the stopper, the rod and the strap in one bag) is $10 to $25; a plumber fitting it is roughly $100 to $350 (price guide, "thin"). Say the opening's price in words where you can: the title already carries $5.
- Honest limit, early: grease won't mend a stopper stem that has cracked through, a pivot ball worn flat or a drain body corroded so the nut won't seat; those need a new part (item 8).
- The four causes (the lift-rod kind first, because most American sinks have it):
  - 1. Hair. Under the sink, a thin metal rod (the pivot rod) goes in through a nut on the back of the drain pipe and crosses the inside of the pipe; it lifts the stopper from below and sits in the water. Hair hooks on it, then more hair on that, until a gray rope wraps the rod and the stopper's stem and the stopper can't slide past. People blame a narrow pipe or an old house; the pipe is fine. Check: pull the stopper straight up and look at the stem.
  - 2. Toothpaste and soap. Each washes away alone, but together on a wet metal stem they set into a paste, and hard water lays white scale on top until it's a crust. The stopper drags, goes halfway and stays. Scrubbing the crust off only buys a season; what goes on the clean stem afterwards is the repair (item 5).
  - 3. The nut somebody tightened. The pivot nut, with a small gasket behind it, belongs hand tight. A wrench adds a turn and clamps the rod so it can't pivot: the knob moves, the stopper doesn't. Beside it, the clevis (the flat metal strap with a row of holes joining the lift rod to the pivot rod) is held by one small screw; it works loose and the knob has nothing to pull. A nut too loose, or with its gasket flattened by that wrench, drips once every time the sink drains onto the particle-board cabinet floor, which swells and crumbles. A small water-damage job in one room runs a few hundred dollars, about $150 to $500 (price guide, section 7).
  - 4. The push button. Some sinks have the other kind: press it down, press it up; no rod, no strap. No rod in the pipe, so no hair rope. But a small spring in a cartridge under the cap sits in standing water; hard-water scale builds on the coils until the click goes soft, then stops. The spring is usually only buried, not broken.
- 5. The grease itself: silicone plumber's grease, a small tube in the plumbing aisle next to the washers. It leaves a thin film water can't wash off: hair slides instead of hooking, toothpaste doesn't set, a spring moves inside it. It goes on O-rings, springs and anything that slides while wet. The word to look for on the tube is "silicone".
  - The store-shelf trap: petroleum jelly and WD-40 look like the same thing, but petroleum swells rubber and silicone does not; they soften the O-rings and the gasket, and the nut drips.
  - German way (from the published video; hedge as here): in the German trade it is called armature grease because it goes on taps and valves, and it is standard kit in a German plumber's bag. Americans tend to wait for the stopper to stick; in Germany it is usual to grease it before it does.
- 6. The lift-rod stopper, apart and back together:
  - Safety first: if anyone poured a chemical drain cleaner down the sink this week, gloves and goggles on, and nothing else goes in after it. One cleaner at a time.
  - Empty the cabinet; an old towel on its floor (a little water comes out when the nut comes off). Turn the pivot nut counterclockwise by hand; pliers gently only if it won't move. Slide the nut back, pull the rod out; the stopper lifts straight out of the top.
  - Clean the stem with an old toothbrush and a drop of dish soap. A plastic drain strip (the thin barbed strip, about $3, price guide) down the open drain brings up what got past; pull it slowly.
  - Wipe the pivot rod dry, then a thin film of grease on the ball of the rod and on the gasket behind the nut. Thin: grease piled up collects the next hair.
  - Rod back through the pipe so its tip passes through the loop at the bottom of the stopper stem; push the stopper up and down once by hand to feel it catch. The nut hand tight, two fingers, no wrench. Move the clevis to the hole where the knob fully opens and fully closes the stopper; tighten its screw. Test: open, closed, open with one finger. About 20 minutes and a towel.
- 7. The push button, apart and back together:
  - Press the stopper so it sits up; turn the cap counterclockwise (a rubber jar grip helps a wet, slippery cap). Unscrew the cartridge from the crossbar in the drain and lift it out.
  - Soak it in a bowl of warm tap water with citric acid stirred in (the plain powder from the canning shelf; price guide: about $10 to $15 a pound). Warm, not boiling, so nothing warps. Nothing else in the bowl: one cleaner on its own. Leave it until the scale goes soft; rinse; toothbrush off the rest.
  - Dry it, a dab of grease on the spring and on the threads, screw it back, cap on, press: the click comes back.
  - German way (from the published video; hedge): citric acid is the usual German household habit for scale.
- 8. When the part is finished: a cracked stem, a worn ball or a corroded drain: a new pop-up assembly ($10 to $25) fits the old drain body the same way the old parts came out. A drain body rusted through means the whole drain comes out with the P-trap (the U-shaped bend under the sink): that one is a call.
- 9. Wartung (maintenance, the check before the thing breaks): once a year, lift each stopper, wipe the stem, a fresh film on the ball; about five minutes. Werner's spring check fits here (the day the heating goes off), if it's the one personal detail used.
- The payoff: the visit he didn't need; the tube in the drawer. One small thing tonight: lift the stopper out of the bathroom sink and look at the stem.

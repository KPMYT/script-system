Citric acid, baking soda and borax are three cheap powders that take most of the scrubbing out of a toilet, a tub or a sink. You know the jobs. The ring in the toilet that is back a week after you scrub it. The white crust round the base of the faucet. The gray line round the tub. So you buy another spray bottle and a stiffer brush, and the ring comes back anyway. The bottle says it cuts through anything. It does not say what that ring is made of. All three cost about twenty dollars. You want a fix that works tonight, not another wasted bottle or a scratched tub. Every job here is yours, with one honest limit. A powder cannot undo a scratch, and a scratched tub becomes a refinishing bill.

Number one is citric acid, and it starts with the toilet. Lift the lid and look at the water line. That ring sits exactly where the water stops, and you have scrubbed it more times than you care to count. Most people reach for bleach. Bleach whitens the stain, but it does not dissolve what the stain is sitting on.

That ring is calcium scale. Hard water carries minerals, and every time the water line dries back a little, it leaves some of them behind on the porcelain. Week after week they build a rough shelf of rock. A brush is made for dirt, and bristles will not scrub rock off. So try this test. Run a fingernail across the ring. If it catches like dried glue, it is scale, and no spray in the cleaning aisle was ever going to shift it.

Left alone, that shelf does more than look bad. Its rough face holds bacteria, and that is the brown and pink you find up under the rim. Then the scale starts to plug the rim jets. Those are the small holes under the rim where the flush water comes out. As they close up, the flush goes weak. A weak flush is the point where many people decide the toilet is done.

A new toilet, put in, runs about three hundred to eight hundred dollars, depending on the toilet you pick and where you live. In forty years in the trade, I have seen plenty of good toilets carried out to the curb for a weak flush. Plenty of those bowls only needed a soak. I think that is a lot of money to spend on a shelf of rock.

The soak starts at the wall. Find the supply valve. That is the small valve on the wall behind the toilet, where the water comes in. Turn it clockwise until it stops. Now flush. The tank empties into the bowl, and with no water coming back in, the level in the bowl drops below the ring.

That step matters more than it looks. A full bowl of water dilutes the powder before it ever reaches the scale, and a weak acid does weak work. With the water down, the ring is sitting out in the air, waiting for you.

Pour half a cup of citric acid straight onto the ring, all the way round. It may fizz for a minute. That is the acid meeting the scale, and it is a good sign. Then leave it overnight. Close the lid, go to bed, and let the powder do the hours of work you used to do with your arm.

In the morning, give the ring one pass with the brush. Open the supply valve, let the tank fill, and flush twice to rinse the bowl. Now run your fingernail across where the ring used to be. Done looks like porcelain your nail slides over with nothing to catch on.

One rule before you pour anything. Citric acid and bleach never meet in the same bowl, because together they make chlorine gas. If a bleach cleaner went into that toilet this week, flush it through a few times first, and only then add the powder. One cleaner at a time, every time.

If a thick shelf is still there in the morning, the ring has had years to grow, and that is where a pumice stone earns its place. It is a cheap stone from the hardware store. Use it only wet, on wet porcelain, with light pressure. Dry pumice scratches the glaze, so never let it go dry against the bowl.

And while you are at the toilet, take the cleaning tablet out of the tank if there is one. Those tablets sit on the rubber parts for weeks. The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank, and it goes soft. A soft flapper leaks, and a running toilet is the next bill. The flapper itself is a few dollars of rubber, but paying a plumber to fit one costs a good deal more.

The ring will come back, and the reason is plain. Your water has not changed. The same minerals are still drying at that line every day. The difference is that now you know what it is. Catch it while it is still a faint line, and the same half cup does the job in one night. That is a bowl you keep, instead of a new toilet on the bill.

Number two is the same powder, on the faucets and the showerhead. Look at the base of your bathroom faucet, where the chrome meets the sink. That white crust is the same scale as the toilet ring. Now look up at the showerhead. Some of the nozzles spray sideways, and some do not spray at all.

A showerhead dries out after every shower. Each time it does, a little scale is left in the nozzles, and they plug one at a time. You do not notice one. You notice when half of them are gone and the shower feels thin.

Most people buy a new head. That is fifteen to forty dollars for the part, and the box promises better pressure. The box does not mention that the same water starts on the new head the day it goes on. A crusted faucet base is worse. Plenty of homeowners get a whole new faucet sold to them over it, and that runs a few hundred dollars once it is put in. To my mind, that is a steep price for a white crust that comes off with a cloth.

The mix is simple. Two tablespoons of citric acid to every cup of hot tap water. Stir it until the water goes clear, so no grains are left at the bottom. Hot tap water is plenty. It never needs to be boiling.

For the faucet base, soak a cloth in the mix and wrap it round the base, snug against the crust. Leave it for an hour. The acid needs that time sitting on the scale, the same as it did in the toilet. Do the same thing on the kitchen faucet, and on the tub spout if it has the same white edge.

For the showerhead, you have two ways. The first way leaves the head where it is. Pour the mix into a plastic bag, pull the bag up over the head so the nozzles sit in the liquid, and tie the top round the shower arm. Leave it one to two hours. The second way is for a head that unscrews by hand. Take it off and soak the whole head in a bowl of the mix for the same time.

Either way, finish the same. Take the bag off, or screw the head back on, and run hot water through it for a minute. That flushes out what the acid loosened. If a few nozzles still look crusted, an old toothbrush takes care of what is left.

Done is easy to see. Every nozzle throws a straight line of water, and the faucet base is bright chrome again. Rinse the chrome with plain water when you finish, so no acid sits on it.

One warning on this mix. Keep citric acid off marble, granite and any natural stone. Acid etches stone, and once it is etched, that dull mark does not come back. If your faucet sits on a stone counter, keep every drip of the mix off the stone around it. Do that, and you have a showerhead you never had to replace, and a faucet that stays where it is.

Number three is baking soda, and this one is for the tub. Run your hand along the gray line where the bath water sits. It feels waxy, not gritty. That tells you it is soap scum, not scale. The soap in your bath water bonds with the minerals in hard water, and together they make a film that catches skin and hair as the water drains.

The usual mistake goes like this. Out comes a green scouring pad, or a gritty cleanser, and a whole Saturday on your knees. And the harder you scrub, the worse it gets. Those pads and cleansers scratch the finish on the tub. Every scratch holds the next line, so each line has more to grab. A scratched tub is a tub you either live with or pay to have refinished, and that is no small bill.

Harder is the mistake. Softer is the fix. Put some baking soda in a bowl and add a squirt of dish soap. Stir it into a paste about as thick as toothpaste. Spread it along the gray line with your fingers or a soft cloth. Then leave it for fifteen minutes and walk away.

The two parts each do a job. The dish soap loosens the grease in the scum. The baking soda is a grit, but a grit soft enough that it will not mark your tub. After fifteen minutes, wipe it off with a damp cloth. The paste lifts the line without a scratch, and you never had to lean on it.

A line that has been building for years may need a second round. Give it more time, never more force. Spread fresh paste, leave it longer, and wipe again. If the whole tub has gone dull, not just the line, do the same paste one section at a time.

In hard water, that line comes back in a few weeks. That is not the paste failing. It is the same soap and the same water meeting again. Fifteen minutes of paste puts it right. That is your Saturday back, and a tub finish that never has to meet a scouring pad again.

That same hard water is what plugs the rim jets and slows a flush. Number four is borax, for the sink and the grout. Look down at your kitchen or bathroom sink. There is a brown stain round the drain. There are tea and coffee marks that never quite lift. And the morning after you clean it, there is a dull film over the whole bowl. Then look at the grout behind the faucet. It has gone gray, and in the corners it has gone black.

That black is mildew, and it is not sitting on top of the grout. It is down in the pores, where a sponge cannot reach. Bleach whitens the top of the grout, so it looks clean for a few days. Then the black comes back up from underneath.

Plenty of people give up and tear out the whole backsplash over black grout. That is a tile job, with a tile bill, and it is a big bill for a problem that lives in a few corners. I would not spend that money until a soak has had its chance.

The soak goes like this. Put the stopper in and fill the sink with hot tap water. Add half a cup of borax, and stir it in with a gloved hand until it dissolves. Then leave it. Let it sit until the water goes cold. Drain it, wipe the sink down, and the stains lift with the wipe.

While the sink is soaking, put that water to work on the grout too. Dip a cloth in it and run it along the grout lines behind the faucet. Let it sit a while, then wipe and rinse. You are using the same half cup for two jobs, and the grout gets the time a quick spray never gives it.

Borax needs some care. Keep it away from children and pets, and it is never swallowed. Put the box on a high shelf when you are done, and rinse the sink well after the soak. And do not confuse it with boric acid. They are two different boxes. Boric acid is the roach powder. Borax is the laundry booster, and it sits in the laundry aisle.

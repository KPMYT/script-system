TITLE: 7 Hair Colors That Make Women Over 60 Look INSTANTLY Younger (The French Way)
CHANNEL: Diane de Chambray
BRIEF (talking points):
- The feeling: putting off the color appointment for fear of what a new shade will do to her face; the wrong color can add years in an afternoon. But color is the fastest tool she has, faster than any haircut. The French way with it: close to the natural shade, glossy, worn on purpose. Seven colors (one promise only: the published hook's "and three that do the opposite" is cut; the three warnings sit inside their colors, unnumbered).
- Color 1, a soft brown, one or two levels lighter than the brown she had at 40, not darker. Many women go darker the week the gray shows (hedged: not "almost every woman"), because dark feels like cover. Warning inside this color: flat, solid box black gives every fine line a dark backdrop and kills the shine; the face takes the blame for the color. A level: one step on the scale from black to the palest blonde. Ask: the same brown family, two levels lighter than she'd pick herself. In France you'll often see women in their 60s and 70s in this kind of soft brown. Check: a daylight photo at the window with hair down; if the hair is far darker than her eyebrows, it's too dark.
- Color 2, warm, lighter face-framing pieces (name it by its tone: a few fine strands a shade or two lighter and warm, at the hairline and the cheek only; not all-over highlights or a full head of foils). Light near the face bounces onto the skin like a lamp. Face-framing: the strands that fall beside the face with the hair down. A shorter, cheaper appointment than a full color (in words; the published "about 40 minutes in the chair" is unchecked, so leave the number out or mark it general). Check: pull the hair back, let it fall; brighter with it down means it's working.
- Color 3, glossy silver, worn on purpose. Letting the gray in can feel like giving up; there's a difference between gray that happened and silver she chose. The heritage line fits here if used ("the way I was raised in Paris, gray was a color you decided on"). The decision is shine: silver with no shine yellows at the ends. Violet shampoo once a week (violet sits opposite yellow on the color wheel), not every wash or it turns faintly lilac; a clear gloss at the salon about monthly (a see-through coat of shine). Warning inside this color: a flat all-over ash or gray rinse drains the face (ash: a cool, almost greenish gray with no warmth); a clear or slightly warm gloss brings the color back. Check: silver that's working catches a band of light when she turns her head.
- Color 4, salt and pepper blended with soft lowlights, for the woman in the middle with a grow-out line at the part. The hard edge tells the eye how long since the appointment. Lowlights: fine pieces taken darker, in a soft brown or soft taupe (a gray-brown), woven through the white so there's no line to grow out; she can go about three months. Ask for soft lowlights with no demarcation line (the colorist's word for that hard edge). If she loves one solid shade, keep it and have the roots softened with a few lowlights.
- Color 5, champagne or soft beige blonde, for the lifelong blonde: "not brassy, not ashy". Brassy is the orange that creeps in; ashy goes gray-green and flat. A hint of warmth puts light on the skin; orange puts heat on it and shows redness. Fix for brass: a blue toner (blue sits opposite orange) at the salon, a blue shampoo at home. If brass returns within weeks every time, mineral-heavy water may be part of it; a shower-head filter may help (hedged: don't say it "takes most of it out"). Warning inside this color: don't chase it lighter and lighter; a cold platinum drains the face like the ash rinse. Check: against a white towel in daylight; orange means toner time.
- Color 6, warm caramel lowlights for the brunette going gray who still wants to be a brunette: caramel is a warm golden brown (the inside of a toffee), fine pieces through the mid-lengths and ends, roots left soft. A warm tone gives back some of the warmth the skin has lost; a cool brown takes more away. Plenty of French brunettes in their 60s go this way rather than black. Keep it to two tones (caramel and her own brown); a third looks stripy. Check: a daylight photo from the side.
- Color 7, two or three tones, never one (name it as a color choice: a base plus a lighter piece or a lowlight, in whichever shade she picked). Real hair is never one color (a child's hair in the sun); one flat tone reads as a bottle. Shine matters as much as shade: a gloss about monthly; for frizz, a smoothing treatment, asking the salon for a formaldehyde-free one (check). Drop the "cool rinse closes the hair" claim (thin evidence). Check: under the bathroom lamp tonight, turn the head slowly; good color moves, flat color sits.
- The audit's reading on the count: colors 2 and 7 in the published video were a placement and a rule, not colors. Name every one by its tone so all seven are paid as colors.
- Personal detail allowed (one, once): she used to think a deep chocolate was the elegant choice once the gray came, and changed her mind.
- Safety: if she colors at home, the patch test on the box every time; if the scalp itches or burns, stop and see a doctor. No dye-chemistry or scalp-health claims beyond that.
- French claims: only the documented ground (gray worn on purpose and kept glossy; low-fuss, natural-looking hair close to the natural shade), hedged ("plenty of", "you'll often see"). The heritage line at most once.

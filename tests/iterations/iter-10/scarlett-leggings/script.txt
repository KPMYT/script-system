TEST (iter-10: Scarlett leggings, the one-page brief)

Leggings are the easiest thing in your closet. You pull them on, they move with you, nothing pinches, and for a while you feel wonderful. Then you walk past a shop window, you catch the whole outfit in the glass, and something about it looks, well, a little frumpy. So the leggings go back in the drawer, marked house only, and the stiff pants come back out.

I bet that's happened at your house, too. And I don't think you should have to choose between comfort and polish, because you don't. Leggings after sixty can look polished, not frumpy, as long as five rules are kept, and every one of them is about the line, never about your body. After the rules, I'll put together seven outfits that keep all five, built from the closet you already have.

So let's start where the trouble usually starts, which is the top.

Rule number one: the top covers your hips and your seat. That means it reaches mid-thigh or longer. A tunic, a long sweater, a shirt dress, or a long cardigan over a longer tank.

Here's why. A legging is a thin layer of stretch sitting right over the fullest part of the body. Put a hip-length top over it, and every line underneath is on show. You know the feeling. You're standing in a line somewhere, and your hand keeps going round to the back of your sweater, tugging it down. That little tug is your body telling you the top is too short.

A longer top fixes it in one move. It makes one smooth line from your shoulder down to your thigh, and the leggings below simply read as a dark, slim leg. The eye doesn't stop at the hip, because there's nothing there to stop it.

And here's how you check, before you ever leave the house. Stand with your back to a full-length mirror and look over your shoulder. If you can see where your seat ends, the top is too short for leggings. That doesn't mean it goes in the bin. It goes in the jeans pile, where it'll be perfectly happy.

Okay, ladies, rule number two, and this is the one that changes the most. Thick, opaque and matte.

When I was designing, I'd hold every new knit up to a window before I let it anywhere near a pattern. Daylight tells you the truth about a fabric faster than any label does. And with leggings, the truth is that thin pairs show everything. They show every line underneath, they cling where you'd rather they skimmed, and by lunchtime they've lost their shape and gone soft at the knee.

What you want instead is weight. Ponte is the best of them. It's a heavy, smooth double knit, which simply means it's knitted thick enough to hold its own line instead of following every curve. Heavy stretch cotton works too, and so does a brushed fabric with real weight to it.

Then there's the finish. Shine catches the light on the thigh and on the knee, so the leg reads as a set of bright patches. A matte fabric keeps the whole leg one even shape, from top to bottom.

The test is simple, and I'd ask you to do it every time you buy a pair. Stand at a window in daylight with a mirror behind you, and bend forward as if you're picking something up off the floor. If the seat goes sheer, those leggings aren't for wearing on their own. Keep them for under a long dress. And please don't trust the fitting room for this. The light in there is very kind. The light in a parking lot is not.

The thick pair will cost a little more. Of everything in this video, it's the best money you'll spend.

Rule number three: a high, wide waistband that doesn't roll and doesn't dig.

Look at the band before you look at anything else. You want it about a hand's width deep, and you want it to come up over your navel. That way the legging starts at the narrowest part of your middle and holds you there gently, instead of cutting across you lower down.

The thin elastic band is the one to walk away from. It rolls over when you sit, it digs in when you stand, and it leaves a ridge that shows right through your top, however long and lovely that top is. So a good top can be undone by a bad waistband, which is why rules one and three belong together.

In the store, do this. Put them on, sit down on the bench, then stand up again. Did the band stay where it was? Can you breathe all the way in? And when you take them off, is there a line pressed into your skin? If there is, that's not your pair. A good waistband is one you forget you're wearing.

Rule number four: ankle length, and no bunching.

The hem should land at your ankle bone or just above it, in one clean line. When leggings are too long, the extra fabric folds down over the shoe in little rings, and those rings cut the leg off at the bottom. The leg looks shorter, and the whole outfit looks unfinished, even when everything above it is right.

If you're petite, look for a seven-eighths length. That's a legging cut to stop a little higher on the leg, so on you it lands just where it should.

And if you've found a pair you love that's simply too long, leggings can be hemmed. You'll want a tailor who works with knits, because a stretch fabric needs a stretch stitch at the hem, so it can give when you move. Not every tailor does that kind of work, so just ask when you bring them in. The ones who do are well worth knowing.

Now, how do you check the length? Not in the mirror. In front of a mirror, you stand a little taller without even meaning to. Have someone take a photo of you from the side, in daylight, with your shoes on, or prop your phone up and use the timer. A photo shows you exactly where that hem falls.

Those two rules, the waistband and the hem, are both in my book, Three Hundred Budget-Friendly Ways to Look Expensive. In the chapter on pants, you'll find a high rise as your default, a waistband that sits without a gap or a dig, and an ankle-grazing length as your universal hem. There are three hundred numbered Ways, and every one gives you the mistake, the swap and the payoff. It's at the link in the description, and there's a QR code on the screen.

Now, rule number five: dark neutrals, with the shoe matched to the legging.

Black, navy, charcoal and chocolate brown. Those four do the job. A dark matte leg gives you the longest line from the waist down, because the eye travels it without stopping. A print on the leg does the opposite. It pulls the eye straight to the fullest part of you, and holds it there. So if you love a print, and I hope you do, move it up near your face. A scarf, a blouse. That's where a print earns its keep, because it brings the eye up to you.

Then match the shoe to the legging. Black leggings, black shoe. Navy leggings, navy shoe. When the color carries on into the shoe, there's no break at the ankle, and the leg just keeps going.

There's one exception, and it's a white sneaker. It works, but only when everything else you're wearing is dark, so the white reads as a finish you chose and not an interruption.

As for which shoes, a low block-heel ankle boot, a knee boot, a loafer, a ballet flat, a leather sneaker or a mule. And if there's a heel, choose a block heel over a thin one, about two inches. It's steadier on a wet sidewalk, and steady always looks more elegant than wobbly.

Keep all five, and leggings stop being house clothes. Let me show you what I mean.

The first outfit is the simplest, and it's the one I'd choose for you if you only tried one. Black ponte leggings, a straight tunic that ends just above the knee, black block-heel ankle boots and a long pendant.

The word that matters there is straight. A tunic that flares out at the hem makes a triangle, wide at the bottom, and a triangle sits heavy. A straight one makes a line, and a line is what you're after. The pendant helps, too, because it draws one more long line down the front.

Push the sleeves up to just below the elbow, and leave your wrists bare, or wear only a watch. A little forearm makes the whole outfit lighter. It looks as if you put it together on purpose, which, of course, you did.

The second outfit is for a cool morning. Charcoal leggings, with a cable-knit sweater that covers your hips. Look at the shoulder seam on the sweater. It should sit right on the point of your shoulder, not dropped halfway down your arm. A dropped shoulder on a chunky knit adds bulk where you don't want it, and the sweater starts to look borrowed.

On your feet, suede loafers in charcoal or a deep brown. Or, if you'd like it a little more relaxed, a pair of spotless white leather sneakers. That's the one exception from rule five, and here it earns its place, because the charcoal and the sweater keep everything else dark.

If you have a fuller middle, the third outfit is the one I'd point you to first. A chambray shirt dress that ends at the knee, worn open over black leggings. Chambray is that soft, washed, denim-blue shirting, lighter than denim and a great deal kinder.

Leave the bottom two or three buttons undone, so the dress opens as you walk and the black leggings show through as a long dark line. The dress skims over your middle instead of sitting on it. Finish with a dark loafer, and you're done.

Keep that dress at the knee, though, not above it. Above the knee, it starts to look like a dress that's a little too short, with leggings brought in to rescue it. At the knee, it looks planned.

The fourth outfit is for the day you have somewhere to be. A lunch, an appointment, a matinee. Thick matte ponte leggings, a long silky blouse, and a blazer that ends below your hip. Pointed flats, and a structured bag carried in your hand rather than on your shoulder.

Here's the check for this one. Once you're dressed, step back about three feet from the mirror. From there, your leggings should pass for slim black pants. If they do, you've got it right.

And about that blazer. It has to end below the hip. A blazer that stops at the hip leaves the seat on show, which takes you straight back to rule one. If that's the blazer you own, and it's a good one, wear it with trousers or a skirt instead, and save the leggings for a longer jacket.

Number five is a column, and it's my favorite. A column simply means one color from shoulder to shoe. Navy leggings, a navy top and navy flats. Then over the top, a long camel duster, or a long navy cardigan if you'd rather stay in one color all the way.

When I was designing, a column was the quickest way I knew to make a woman look taller without changing a single thing about her. The eye runs from top to bottom in one go. The camel duster frames it, with two long lines falling either side, and it looks expensive without trying.

One thing to watch. Keep the flats navy, not black. Black flats under navy leggings make a small break at the ankle, and the column stops short.

The sixth outfit is for an evening out. Matte faux-leather leggings in a thick stretch fabric, with a silky top, a long jacket and block-heel boots. Then one piece of jewelry, a pendant or earrings, but not both. The leggings are already saying something, so the jewelry can speak quietly.

The word matte matters here more than anywhere. Shiny leather leggings read as costume. Matte ones read as a beautiful dark trouser with a little texture to it. And run the bend test from rule two on these especially, because faux leather varies more than any other kind. Some pairs are wonderful, and some go thin in exactly the wrong places.

And the last outfit is for the days you want to be comfortable and still look put together. Polished athleisure. High-waisted leggings, a long dark sweatshirt that covers your hips, and a dark quilted vest that ends below the hip. Spotless white sneakers, and a crossbody bag that stays out of your way.

No logos anywhere. Not on the sweatshirt, not on the leggings, not on the bag. Logos say gym, and you're not going to the gym. You're going out for coffee, or to the farmers market, or to meet a friend. And notice that the vest and the sweatshirt are both dark. That's what lets the white sneakers work, the same rule as before, holding here too.

Leggings like these are only as good as their stretch, so look after them. Wash them inside out on cool and hang them to dry, and skip the dryer altogether, because the heat breaks down the stretch and fades the black. That's how a good pair turns into a tired one. Once a season, I take everything out of my closet and put back only what I'd buy again today. Look after your leggings this way, and they'll keep earning their place in it.

TEST (iter-10: Diane dress young, the one-page brief)

My dear friends, I am going to be a little blunt with you, because I think you can take it. The outfit you bought to take ten years off is very often the outfit that puts them right back on. The short hem, the cropped top, the sneaker with the enormous sole. None of it fools anyone, and the sad part is that it hides the woman who is actually wearing it.

This is not about dressing old, and it is certainly not about giving up. Most French women I have known simply dress the best version of the age they are. They polish instead of disguise. So stop trying to dress young, and let me show you what works instead. There are eight things that tell the world you are trying too hard, and for each one I will give you the French swap.

The first is the very short skirt, and its cousin, the little dress that looks perfectly respectable in the fitting room and then climbs up your thighs the moment you sit down. You know the one. You spend the whole evening with a hand on the hem, tugging and checking, crossing and uncrossing, and you don't hear a word anyone says to you at dinner. That is not a dress. That is a job.

Here is the trouble with it. A very short skirt sends all the attention to the leg, and only to the leg. Your face, your conversation, the lovely earrings you chose, they all lose the fight. A hem works like an arrow, and a short one points down.

The swap is the midi, which simply means a length that falls somewhere between the knee and the ankle. Or, if you like a little more shape, an A-line skirt that is fitted at the waist and then widens gently on its way to mid-calf. It shows that you still have a waist. It moves when you walk, which is half the beauty of a skirt in the first place. And it covers the knee, so you can forget about it entirely and get on with your evening.

Before you wear anything new out of the house, sit down in front of a full-length mirror and watch what the hem does. If it rides up and your hand goes to it, it goes back to the shop. If it stays where it is, you have found your skirt.

Now, the crop top. And I mean any top at all that stops above your waistband, even the polite ones in sensible colors. Plenty of women find that by their sixties the middle has become the widest part of the body. That is simply life. A cropped top draws a straight line right across that middle, and then, for good measure, it adds a little strip of skin underneath it. It does the exact opposite of what you wanted it to do.

When I was designing, we spent hours deciding where a line should fall, because the eye stops wherever a line stops. Put the line in the wrong place and nothing else you do will rescue the outfit.

So the swap is a Breton. That is the striped cotton top with long sleeves, usually navy on cream, with a wide neckline that opens out toward the shoulders. Plenty of women in France have one folded in a drawer. That wide neckline is cleverer than it looks. When the shoulders read a little wider, the hips read a little narrower, and the whole figure settles into balance without you doing a thing.

If stripes aren't for you, take a fitted knit and give it a front tuck, which means you tuck only the front few inches into your waistband and let the back fall loose. It tells the eye where your waist is without making a fuss about it.

Turn sideways to the mirror. The knit should sit at your hipbone, lying smooth, with no bunching and no skin showing. And if you truly love a shorter top, I won't take it away from you. Wear it with high-rise pants, so that the two pieces meet and the gap disappears.

The third one makes me smile, I have to admit. The childish graphic tee. The cartoon cat, the glitter hearts, the slogan about wine or about how much you hate Mondays. I understand the appeal. It feels fun and easy, and somebody probably gave it to you as a present. But think about what happens when you walk into a room. People read the sentence before they see the face. They meet the shirt first and you second, and that is the wrong way round.

The swap is wonderfully simple. A crisp white tee in a heavy cotton, or, better still, a white cotton poplin shirt. Let the collar stand up a little at the back of the neck, and roll the sleeves twice, so the wrist shows. There is something about a bare wrist under a rolled white cuff that looks relaxed and polished at the same time. Plenty of women in France own one white shirt they love, and when it finally wears out, they go and buy the very same one again. No hunting, no wondering. They already know it works.

The only thing to watch is the weight of the fabric. Hold the shirt up to a window. If you can see your hand through it, it is too thin, and it will cling and show everything underneath. A good white shirt should feel as if it has a little backbone of its own.

That white shirt has a whole Secret to itself in my book, The French Way. It is called Invest in the One Blouse That Replaces Ten, and you will find it in the chapter Five Pieces That Do the Work of Fifty. There are one hundred and one numbered Secrets in the book, and each one has a How To Do It and a Skip If. It is at the link in the description, and the QR code is on your screen right now. Now, back to your closet.

The fourth is the low-rise skinny jean, and especially the ripped one. Let's look at what each part of it is doing. The low rise sits underneath the belly instead of holding it. The skinny leg narrows all the way down and stops at the ankle like a sock. And the rips, well, the rips send the eye straight to the knee, which is rarely where you want anybody looking.

But I want to say this next part clearly, because I hear women blame themselves for it all the time. It isn't your body that is wrong for the jean. The jean was cut for a hip that isn't yours. That is the jean's problem, and it was never yours.

The swap is a dark straight jean. One deep blue, or black, with no fading and none of those pale whiskers across the lap. Straight means it is the same width at the knee as it is at the hem. The rise sits at your natural waist or just below it. There is no distressing anywhere. And the hem lands right at the ankle bone. You'll often see this jean in France, and I promise you it works on a woman in her seventies with a pair of loafers and a trench coat. It works beautifully, in fact.

Here is how you know you have the right pair. Sit down on the edge of your bed. The waistband should stay up above your hips, and nothing should push up and over the top of it. If it passes that, it will pass everything else.

Number five surprises people. The little backpack purse. It looks so practical, and I know it frees your hands. But think about where it sits. It sits exactly where a school bag sits, between the shoulder blades, and it turns a grown woman's outfit into a walk to class.

There is a second problem with it, and this is the one I care about more. You can't see it. I still live in Paris, and on a crowded train or in a busy market, a bag you can't see is a bag somebody else can open. I would much rather you kept your things where your eyes can reach them.

So choose a structured leather bag instead, which simply means a bag that holds its shape even when it is empty. Carry it on your forearm, or wear it across your body so that it rests at the front of your hip, where your hand falls on it naturally. No logos, and nothing dangling from the straps. Many women in France carry one bag like this for years, and when a corner wears or a strap gives out, they take it to be mended rather than replaced.

Set it down on the floor. If it stands up on its own, it is the right bag. If it slumps over like a tired dog at the end of a long walk, keep looking.

I can feel a few of you wincing by now, so let me say this again. My quarrel is with the clothes. The woman wearing them, I like very much, and I would like the rest of the world to see her.

Now, your hands. Very long acrylic nails. I understand they feel like a treat, and some of them are little works of art. But when nails are that long, every gesture becomes about them. You pick up a cup, you point at something across the table, you touch your hair, and the eye goes to the nails every single time instead of to you.

The swap is short nails, filed to follow the natural curve of your fingertip, in one single color. Red, if you like a little drama, or nude, which simply means any shade close to your own skin. You'll often see hands like that on women in France, and I think they look wonderfully calm and capable.

Lay your hand flat on a sheet of white paper. If the nail runs past your fingertip by more than a pencil line, take it a little shorter. Your nail salon will be happy to do it for you at your next visit.

Seven is the chunky platform sneaker, and very often the trucker cap that seems to come along with it. Let me take the cap first, because it is quick. It has no swap. It simply goes. If you need shade from the sun, wear a hat with a real brim, one that actually shades your face.

The sneaker takes a little longer. A big, heavy sole puts all the weight of the outfit at the very bottom, and it pulls the whole line of you downward, as if you were standing in a pair of buckets. And there is a practical side to it that matters even more to me. A stiff, thick sole that lifts you up off the ground is simply less steady. Steadiness is worth far more than a few extra inches of height.

When I was designing, I learned that the shoe sets the rhythm for everything above it. A heavy shoe makes a heavy walk, however light the dress.

So, the swap. A loafer, a ballet flat, or a clean white leather sneaker with a low, thin sole. You'll often see ballet flats and loafers on French women. If your feet are wide or sore, look for a loafer in soft leather, and rather than going up a size, take it to a cobbler and have it stretched to your foot. A good cobbler is a friend for life.

Stand up straight and look down at your feet. If you can see the sole sticking out around them, it is too much sole.

And the last one is the sneakiest of all, because it can look so put together. Head-to-toe trends. The top, the pants, the shoes and the bag, all from one season's rack. It feels so current on the day you buy it. But trends expire, and when they go, they take the whole outfit with them at once, and suddenly you look like a photograph from one particular year.

The swap is one trend piece at most, and the rest classic. If you love trends, and some of you truly do, keep loving them. Just love them one at a time. Many women in France add one new idea a season and let it sit among things they have owned for years.

I keep a little rule for myself, and it helps enormously here. One in, one out. When a new piece arrives in my wardrobe, an old one goes into the donation bag that same week. It keeps me honest about what I actually wear.

Before you leave the house, count the pieces you have on that came from the same season. If there is more than one, one of them goes back on the hanger.

And that brings me to the real point underneath all eight of these. Dressing young and dressing modern are not the same thing, and modern is what you are after. In your sixties and seventies, modern and elegant look like this. Neutrals, so navy, cream, gray, black and camel, with one color you love close to your face. Tailoring that fits you at the shoulders and the waist. Natural fabrics, cotton, wool, silk and linen, that fall instead of cling. One jewel that means something to you. A silk scarf. A modern haircut that you barely have to style. And a red lip, if you like one. Here is the good news, my dear friends. Most of it is already hanging in your closet, waiting for you.

TEST (iter-10: Diane hair colors, the one-page brief)

You know the feeling. The gray is showing at the part again, the appointment is overdue, and you keep putting it off, because the last time you tried a new shade you came home looking tired, or harsh, or ten years older than you felt that morning. The wrong color can add years to a face in a single afternoon. That fear is real, and it is fair. But here is the other side of it. Color is the fastest tool you have, faster than any haircut, faster than any new blouse. The right shade can make you look younger the moment you walk out of the chair, and it doesn't take anything drastic. The way plenty of French women handle it is simple: stay close to the shade nature gave you, keep it glossy, and wear it like you meant it. So here are seven hair colors that do exactly that, starting with the one so many women get backwards.

The first color is a soft brown, and the whole trick is in which direction you move. When the gray starts coming through, many women go darker, often the very same week, because dark feels like cover. It feels safe. I understand it completely, because I did it myself. When my own gray arrived, I was sure a deep chocolate was the elegant choice, the grown-up choice. It took me a while, and a few unkind photographs, to change my mind.

Here is what actually happens. Flat, solid box black, or anything close to it, gives every fine line on your face a dark backdrop to sit against. It takes the shine out of the hair, and then your face gets the blame for what the color is doing. People don't look at you and think, her hair is too dark. They think, she looks tired.

So go the other way. Choose a soft brown one or two levels lighter than the brown you had at forty, not darker. A level, if you've never heard the word, is simply one step on the colorist's scale, which runs from black at one end to the palest blonde at the other. When you sit down in the chair, ask for the same brown family, two levels lighter than you would pick yourself. Those are the words. Your colorist will know exactly what you mean, and she may well be glad you said it.

In France you'll often see women in their sixties and seventies wearing this kind of soft brown, and it's a large part of why they look rested rather than done. It isn't that the color is daring. It's that it's kind to the face.

And here is how to check yours. Stand at a window in daylight with your hair down and take a photo. Look at your hair, then look at your eyebrows. If the hair is far darker than your brows, it's too dark. That one comparison will tell you more than any bathroom mirror ever will.

A soft brown takes care of the overall color. But the shade that does the most for your face is often the one sitting right beside it, which brings me to the second.

The second color is a warm, lighter shade placed only where it counts. A few fine strands, one or two shades lighter than the rest of your hair and on the warm side, right at the hairline and down beside the cheek. Not highlights all over. Not a full head of foils. Just the face-framing pieces, which simply means the strands that fall beside your face when you wear your hair down.

When I was designing, we spent hours on the light in a fitting room, because the same dress could look like two different dresses depending on where the lamp stood. Hair works exactly the same way. Light near the face bounces back onto the skin like a little lamp of its own. It lifts the shadows under the eyes and around the mouth, and nobody can quite say why you look brighter. They just know that you do.

The lovely thing is that this is a much smaller job than a full color. It's a shorter appointment, it's gentler on the hair, and it's easy to keep up between your bigger visits. If you've been frightened of a big change, this is the place to begin, because it is very hard to get badly wrong when it's only a few strands.

To check it, pull your hair back off your face and look in the mirror. Then let it fall again. If you look brighter with it down, it's working. If you can't see any difference at all, ask for the pieces to come a little closer to the face next time.

Now, those first two are for the woman who is still coloring. The third is for the woman who has been wondering whether to stop.

The third color is glossy silver, worn on purpose. I know that letting the gray in can feel like giving up. I hear it all the time. Women say to me, I'm just not ready to look like I've stopped trying. And I understand that. But there is a real difference between gray that simply happened to you and silver that you chose. In Paris, where I still live, you'll often see gray treated as a color a woman decides on, not something she surrenders to. And the thing that makes it look decided is shine.

Silver with no shine turns yellow at the ends. It goes dull, and it starts to look a little dry and brittle, and that is what reads as giving up. Silver with shine looks deliberate and bright. So there are two habits to keep. First, a violet shampoo once a week. Violet sits opposite yellow on the color wheel, so it quietly cancels the yellow out. Once a week, mind you, not every wash, or your silver will turn faintly lilac, and that is a look nobody asked for. Second, a clear gloss at the salon about once a month. A gloss is a see-through coat of shine. It doesn't change your color at all. It just lets the color catch the light.

Now, here is the warning that belongs with this color. Some women, wanting to tidy up their gray, ask for a flat, all-over ash or gray rinse. Ash is a cool, almost greenish gray with no warmth in it at all, and laid over the whole head, it drains the face. The skin looks paler and the eyes look tired. If that has happened to you, don't panic. A clear or slightly warm gloss will bring the life back into the color.

Here is your check. Turn your head slowly near a window. Silver that's working catches a band of light as you move. If it just sits there, it needs shine, not a new color.

The fourth color is for the woman caught in the middle, and I suspect there are a great many of you, my dear friends. The gray is coming in, you're still coloring, and every few weeks there's that line at the part. That hard edge is the unkind part, because it tells everyone exactly how long it's been since your last appointment. It's like a date stamp on the top of your head.

The answer is salt and pepper, blended with soft lowlights. Lowlights are simply the opposite of highlights: fine pieces taken darker instead of lighter. Here they're done in a soft brown, or a soft taupe, which is a gray-brown, and woven through the white so the dark and the light mix together. Because nothing is solid, there's no line to grow out. The gray comes in and disappears into the mix, and you can usually go about three months between visits instead of racing back every few weeks.

The words to use in the chair are soft lowlights with no demarcation line. Demarcation line is the colorist's own word for that hard edge at the root, and the moment you say it, she'll know exactly what you're after.

And if you love your one solid shade and can't bear to give it up, you don't have to. Keep it. Just ask to have the roots softened with a few lowlights, so the regrowth melts in rather than marching down your part.

That's the woman going gray from brown. But what about the woman who has been blonde her whole life?

The fifth color is champagne, or a soft beige blonde, and it's for the lifelong blonde who has no intention of stopping now. Good for you. But what you ask for matters more than ever, and it comes down to a few words: not brassy, not ashy.

Brassy is the orange that creeps in over the weeks between appointments. Ashy is the opposite mistake, when the blonde goes gray-green and flat. Champagne sits right between the two. A hint of warmth puts light on your skin. Orange, on the other hand, puts heat on it, and shows every bit of redness you'd rather it didn't.

When the brass comes back, the fix is blue, because blue sits opposite orange on the color wheel. At the salon, that's a blue toner. At home, it's a blue shampoo, used the same way you'd use the violet one for silver. And if the brass keeps returning within a few weeks, every single time, your water may be part of the story, especially if it's heavy in minerals. A filter on the shower head may help.

The warning with this color is the temptation to chase it lighter and lighter, as if the answer to brass were simply more blonde. It isn't. A cold platinum drains the face in just the same way that flat ash rinse does on silver. You want champagne, not ice.

To check it, hold a white towel up beside your hair in daylight. Against the white, any orange shows right away. If you see it, it's time for the toner.

Blondes have their champagne. Brunettes have something every bit as good.

The sixth color is warm caramel lowlights, for the brunette who is going gray but still feels like a brunette, and would like to stay one. Caramel is a warm golden brown, the color of the inside of a toffee when you bite into it. Fine pieces of it go through the mid-lengths and the ends, and the roots are left soft, so the gray coming in at the top blends rather than shouts.

Why warm? Because with the years, the skin loses some of its warmth, and a warm tone in the hair gives a little of it back. A cool brown does the opposite. It takes even more away, and that's when people start asking whether you've been sleeping badly. Plenty of French brunettes in their sixties take this route rather than going to black, and it is softer on the face from every angle.

There is one rule here. Keep it to two tones, the caramel and your own brown. The moment you add a third, it starts to look stripy, and stripes are never what anyone means by natural.

Check it with a photo taken from the side, in daylight. The side view is where you'll see whether the caramel is melting into your brown or sitting on top of it in lines.

And the seventh color is really a way of wearing any color, and it may matter most of all: two or three tones, never one. Whatever shade you've chosen from this list, the base needs at least one companion, a lighter piece or a lowlight, so the color has some depth to it.

Think of a child's hair after a summer in the sun. It's never one color. It's honey on top, darker underneath, paler at the ends. Real hair is never one flat shade, and so the eye reads one flat tone as a bottle, even from across a room. When I was designing, we learned the same lesson with fabric. A wool dyed one perfectly even color looked flat and lifeless under the lights, and the one with a little variation in the thread looked rich. Hair is no different.

With this one, shine matters as much as shade. Keep up that gloss about once a month. If frizz is what dulls your hair, a smoothing treatment can help, and when you book it, ask the salon for a formaldehyde-free one. That's a perfectly fair question, and a good salon will be glad you asked.

One more thing, my dear friends, because it matters. If you color at home, do the patch test on the box every single time, even with a shade you've used for years. And if your scalp ever itches or burns, stop, and see a doctor.

So tonight, stand under the bathroom lamp and turn your head slowly from side to side. Good color moves. Flat color just sits there. And that is the whole difference between hair that makes you look younger and hair that simply makes you look done.

Seven haircuts make women over 60 look younger instantly, and every one of them helps you avoid what, forgive me, we all call the "granny" cut. Get the right one, and people start asking what you've changed.

Your age isn't what does it, ladies, and neither is gray or fine hair. A shape does: short and rounded, set and sprayed, flat at the back and domed on top. All that separates you from the hair you admire is knowing what to ask for. Each cut takes one appointment and the hairdryer you already own. The first changes a face the most, and it's the one most of us fear.

Number one, the textured pixie.

If a short cut frightens you, I understand, because it can feel as if there's nowhere left to hide. But think of the short cuts in your own photographs. The ones that add years are round and tight, set into a helmet that never moves, and the ones that look younger are lifted at the top and broken up at the ends.

A smooth, round outline gives the eye one solid shape to land on, and it sits low around your face like a cap. Lift at the crown draws the eye up to your eyes and cheekbones, piecey ends let light through the edges, and a clean nape, cut close at the back, shows the line of your neck. The women whose short hair you admire keep it short, but they never let it sit still.

When I was designing, I learned that a solid shape with no break in it always looks heavy, however small it is. A little jacket with no seam or dart sits on the body like a box, and the moment you shape it, the same jacket looks lighter. Short hair only adds years when it stops moving.

So what do you ask for? Ask for a textured pixie with lift at the crown, a clean, close nape, a soft side fringe, and the ends point-cut. That means your stylist snips into the ends with the tips of the scissors, so they finish soft. It sounds bold for fine or thinning hair, but it's kind to it, because short, light hair has nothing heavy pulling it flat. One word of care, ladies. Sudden shedding or bare patches are a question for your doctor, not your stylist.

Run your fingers up the back from your nape. If it springs back into one smooth ball, you want more texture next time. My own rule is to book your next trim before you leave the salon, every four to six weeks, because a pixie left alone grows back into a bowl. Kept up, it lifts your whole face, and people see your eyes first.

If you'd never go that short, the next cut keeps your length and only takes the heaviness away.

Number two, the modern layered bob.

Plenty of us have worn a bob cut in one blunt line that stops right at the jaw. In your mirror, that straight edge sits across the widest part of your lower face, and it makes your jaw look heavier and your face look older than it is. The line needs to move, but your jaw can stay exactly as it is.

Your eye goes to wherever hair is fullest. A blunt bob keeps all its weight at the bottom edge, so the eye drops to the jaw and stays there. Internal layers are cut underneath the top layer, so the weight comes out where nobody sees it and your hair swings when you move. Soft pieces around your face then draw the eye back up to your cheekbones, and that lift is what makes you look younger. It's the bob you notice on other women, the one that swings.

A good tailor will tell you a heavy hem in thick cloth never hangs well. So the tailor takes bulk out from underneath, in the seams and the lining, and the outside is free to swing. Internal layering is the hairdresser's version of that trick, and the outer line stays clean and polished.

So which bob do you have now? If it's one solid line, ask for a jaw-length bob with internal layers and two soft face-framing pieces, cut a little shorter at the front so they curve toward your cheekbones. At jaw length, this suits oval and heart-shaped faces best. Shake your head gently in the mirror, and if your bob moves as one block, it still needs more layering.

I'd keep the fullest part of your hair at your cheekbones and never at your jaw. Do that, and your face looks lifted instantly, and someone will ask whether you've been away somewhere. But what if your face is round or square, or you'd like a little more length?

Number three, the collarbone lob, cut a little longer in front.

A lob is simply a long bob, and this one grazes your collarbone. If your face is round or square, you've probably noticed that hair cut straight across at one length makes it look wider in photographs. That level line repeats the width of your face right underneath it, which is why I'd avoid it on a round face.

But tilt the line a little, and your face changes with it. When the front is a little longer than the back, the edge runs down and forward toward your collarbone, and your eye follows it down instead of across. That small diagonal makes a round face look longer and a square jaw look softer, and it shows off your neck too.

In my years of travel, the women I noticed with round faces, in Rome or in Tokyo, rarely wore their hair cut level at the chin. They wore it a little longer, falling forward toward the collarbone. It works the way a V-neck does on a sweater. A crew neck draws a circle under your chin, and a V-neck draws a line down that lengthens everything above it.

So how much angle do you want? About an inch, and no more. Ask for a lob that sits on your collarbone at the front and about an inch shorter at the back. I know a sharper angle can look striking in a photograph, but anything steeper swings into your face every time you look down, and it looks fussy and dated. Stand side-on to the mirror with a hand mirror behind you, and follow the edge from your nape to the front. You want a gentle slope, not a ski slope.

The rule I hold to is an inch of angle and not a fraction more. Get it right, and your face and neck both look longer instantly, and the whole cut looks deliberate and expensive.

This next one fixes something you can't see from the front, and all it takes is the right words to your stylist.

Number four, the soft stacked bob.

When did you last see the back of your own head? Very few of us ever do, and that's exactly where this one hides. Hair often lies flatter at the back over the years, and a cut that's one length all over falls straight down from the crown. From the side your head looks flat behind, and a flat back makes your whole profile look older, even when the front is lovely.

I take one last look in the mirror by my front door before I go out, and I always turn sideways, because that's where a flat back gives itself away. When I was designing, we judged every garment from the back as well as the front. A jacket can look perfect head-on but hang flat and lifeless from behind, and the woman wearing it never knows. The back of your hair is half of what everyone else sees.

The fix is gentle graduation, which means the hair at the back is cut in short, stacked layers that get longer toward the front. That builds a soft curve at your crown and tucks in at your nape, so your head has a rounded, lifted shape from the side. The women you notice from behind nearly always have that little curve at the crown.

So what do you say in the chair? Ask for a soft stack, not a sharp wedge. A wedge has its fans, but it's cut steep and tight, and it can look severe in a way a soft stack never does. At home, hold a hand mirror behind you, face your hall mirror and turn your head to the side. You want one smooth curve from your crown to your nape, with no flat stretch and no hard shelf.

I always say a cut has to work from every side, because people see you from every side. With a soft stack, your profile looks lifted, polished and younger, whether someone's behind you in the grocery line or beside you at lunch.

And if you've been told you're too old for long hair, this next one is your permission.

Number five, long hair with soft layers that start at the cheekbone.

Length is fine after 60, and I'd never ask you to avoid it. But what adds years is long hair cut in one heavy line, all one length, hanging straight down from the crown. All that weight sits at the bottom, it pulls your hair flat on top, and it drags the eye down past your jaw, so your face looks tired and longer than it is.

For years I believed long hair after a certain age dragged a face down, and I'm afraid I said so to anyone who asked. But I changed my mind once I looked properly, because what I'd been seeing all along was weight sitting at the bottom of the hair. Long hair with movement looks soft and current on a woman of any age, and long hair that hangs still looks heavy on anyone.

If you change one thing about long hair, make it where the layers begin. Ask for soft layers that start at your cheekbone and fall from there, so the shortest piece frames your face and the rest grow lighter toward the ends. Ask for the ends to be point-cut too, so they finish soft and wispy rather than in a thick, blunt edge. You keep almost all of your length. Only the weight goes.

At home, pull a section of hair forward over your shoulder. If the shortest layer touches your cheekbone and the ends look light, the cut is right. If it's one thick line from top to bottom, it isn't yet.

My rule is to keep your length for as long as it still swings when you turn your head. Cut this way, long hair looks fresh and younger, it frames your face instead of hiding it, and you can still put it up whenever you like.

This next one is the easiest of all to look after, and it was made for the hair you already have.

Number six, the soft shag with curtain framing.

A shag is a cut with lots of soft layers all over, lighter at the ends, so your hair falls into its own shape. Curtain framing means bangs parted in the middle that sweep away to each side, like curtains drawn open. If your hair has any wave in it, I'd choose this cut first, because it lets the wave do the work.

Think about what a set does. Rollers, a hood dryer and a can of spray give you the same rounded shape every time, and that stiffness is a big part of what makes hair look older. But wavy hair cut in a shag does the opposite. The layers let each wave sit where it wants, the curtain pieces open your face at the eyes and cheekbones, and your whole head looks relaxed and current.

Over thirty-five years designing in London, I chose every fabric for the way it fell on its own. A cut that fights the fabric never looks easy, but a cut that follows the drape looks effortless even when it took hours. Your hair has a drape too, and cut to follow your own wave, it looks expensive with almost no effort.

So how do you wear it day to day? Ask for a soft shag with curtain pieces that start around your cheekbones and blend into the layers. After washing, scrunch your hair gently with your hands and let it air-dry, which takes minutes with this cut. If you'd rather speed it up, smooth a heat protectant through first and keep your dryer on a low setting, especially on fine or gray hair. Then leave it alone. If it dries into a soft shape without a brush, the cut is right.

With this one, I'd say let the cut do the work your rollers used to do. It looks younger instantly, and you get your mornings back.

This last one suits almost any face, and it's the gentlest change of the seven.

Number seven, the chin-length bob with a wispy side-swept bang.

Plenty of us grew a heavy, blunt fringe years ago, straight across the forehead and down to the brows, and kept it because it felt safe. But a solid bang like that is a curtain of hair across the top of your face. It cuts your face in half, it sits right on your eyebrows, and it shadows your eyes, so your whole face looks heavier and older than it is. You don't need to avoid bangs, though. You only need lighter ones.

A wispy bang works the other way. It crosses your forehead on a soft diagonal, and a diagonal line lifts the eye where a straight one stops it. A little forehead shows through, your brows show, and your eyes look brighter and more open. Paired with a bob that ends at the chin, the line of the cut sits above your jaw, so your whole face looks lifted.

Stylists have a working rule for bangs that I've always liked. If you can't see any forehead through them, they're too heavy, and if they cover your brows completely, they're hiding the most expressive part of your face. A bang should frame your eyes, never sit on top of them.

In the chair, ask for a chin-length bob with a few wispy pieces cut on the diagonal and brushed to the side, thinned so they don't lie flat in a block. If you're growing out a blunt fringe, this is also the kindest way to do it, because the side sweep hides the awkward weeks. Then take a photo of yourself straight on, by a window, in daylight. If you can see your brows through the bang, the weight is right.

Lighter at the front, always, is how I put it. It takes years off instantly, and it's the change people notice first without ever quite knowing what it is.

## 1. Flow first: listening start to finish

### Script X

**How it sounds:** quick, tidy and correct. It feels like a checklist. Every cut follows the same template: "Number N. Name." Then a definition, then "ask for...", then "In the salon/chair, ask for...", then a mirror test, then a one-line payoff. The template is clear the first time. By cut four the listener can hear the pattern coming. The opening lasts 31 seconds. It names the granny cut in one sentence and never shows the listener a picture of it.

**Strengths (quoted):**
- "If you've worn one short style for years, changing it can feel like giving up the only thing you knew how to manage." (real empathy, and it sounds spoken)
- "And if a short cut has never stayed up for you, the round shape was the trouble all along." (takes the blame off her)
- "But what if your face is round, or a little square? Then jaw length isn't the length I'd choose for you." then the book aside, then "Now, back to that round or square face." (the best bridge in either script, because the book sits inside an open question)
- "Maybe you've heard that long hair is a mistake after sixty. That advice can sting ... Length is fine. If you love it, keep it."
- "Keep the length you love. Just give it somewhere to move."
- "it can make you look younger the first time you catch yourself in a shop window."

**Where it stops flowing (quoted):**
- The opening joins don't connect: "Which of the two is in your mirror right now? / And this part surprises most women." The "And" picks up nothing. The granny cut gets a one-line list ("short and rounded, set or sprayed, flat at the back, with a dome on top") and the script goes straight on to cut one.
- "Number one. The textured pixie. / The rounded short cut sits on the head as one solid shape..." The listener has just heard "pixie" and is now hearing about a different cut. It takes a sentence to catch up.
- It sounds like a glossary: "Then a clean nape, that's the hairline at the back of your neck"; "An oval face is a little longer than it is wide. A heart shape is wider at the forehead and narrower at the chin."; "That's a spray or cream that coats the hair so the heat is gentler on it."; "That just means every hair was cut straight across..."; "That's the word stylists use for..." There is a definition in almost every cut.
- The same pattern repeats: "In the salon, ask for..." / "In the chair, ask for..." (twice). There is also a mirror test in nearly every cut: "shake your head gently in front of the mirror", "Stand side-on to the mirror", "Turn your head from side to side in front of the mirror", "look straight into the mirror", plus the hand-mirror routine in cut four. Each one is fine on its own. Seven of them sound like a form being filled in.
- Lines that are awkward for a voice: "That's the back." (cut four; it hangs on its own) and "This is the mark of that old rounded set I'd most like to see go."
- The book aside is clunky: "...the very first Way in the hair chapter of my book, Three Hundred Budget-Friendly Ways to Look Expensive. That chapter is called The Hair Hack." This names the chapter twice in two steps.

### Script Y

**How it sounds:** like one woman talking you through it. It opens on a scene the viewer has lived ("quietly turned the phone face down on the table"). It explains *why* the shape ages a face. Then it uses the granny cut as the measuring stick for every cut that follows, so each one connects back to the opening. The transitions are spoken bridges, not labels. Its weak spot is pace: the first cut doesn't start until about 2:56, and the setup makes one point twice.

**Strengths (quoted):**
- "...but a week later someone took a photo at lunch, and you looked at it for a second and quietly turned the phone face down on the table." (pulls her in; she feels seen)
- "Hair that moves looks young. Hair that's been set into place looks, well, set."
- "Nobody sets out to get a granny cut. It just creeps up on you, one appointment at a time." (no blame on her or the salon)
- Bridges: "Now, if a pixie feels like too big a leap, the second cut is the one I'd point most women toward first." / "Now let's go back to that flat back of the head ... That's what the fourth cut fixes." / "And the seventh cut brings us right back to the front of your face."
- "A soft stack looks like your hair simply has body. A wedge looks like it was engineered."
- "If it hangs, it doesn't need to be cut off. It needs layers."

**Where it stops flowing (quoted):**
- The setup repeats itself: "It isn't your age, and it isn't how short your hair is. It's the shape." Two paragraphs later comes "Now notice what I didn't say. I didn't say short hair is the problem ... I didn't say long hair is the problem either." This is the same point said twice, inside a 2 minute 56 second run before cut one. A listicle viewer may drift.
- It mixes up an image: the blunt bob is bad "like a curtain that's been hemmed", long hair should fall "instead of a curtain", then the shag's "curtain framing ... like a pair of curtains drawn open" is good. The same picture means bad, then bad, then good.
- "That ease is exactly what reads as young." This is a house phrase, and the line is only there to land the title word.
- "That swap ... is exactly the kind of small change I gathered into my book". This is the banned provenance line (see section 2), and it sounds like a pitch.
- The ending has two questions, and the second one sounds tacked on: "...the good one or the one you'd rather not remember? And which of these seven are you thinking about for your next appointment? Tell me in the comments."
- "Every one of these swaps lives in The Hair Hack chapter..." The text-to-speech voice may read "lives" as the noun (rhymes with "hives"). The claim is also not checked (section 4).

### Why Y flows and X doesn't (in one line)
Y joins the cuts with *reasons* ("if that feels too big a leap...", "let's go back to that flat back..."). X joins them with *numbers* and repeats the same inner template seven times.

## 4. Facts: anything wrong or risky

**X**
- "the very first Way in the hair chapter" = Way 197, "Choose a Layered Bob Over a Blunt One". The brief gives the chapter as Ways 197 to 207. Correct.
- "Every Way in the book gives you the swap and the payoff": true per the profile.
- "Has your face filled out a little at the cheeks over the years?" **Tone risk.** It puts the adjective on her face, not the cut. The profile says she never shames a body. Reword to the face shape ("If your face is on the round side...").
- "on the spot that softens first with time" (the jaw): mild, but it is the same kind of risk.
- "it's the angle people see most" (the side profile): an unsupported claim. Soft, but it can be cut.
- "Before I go out, I take one last look in the mirror by my front door": approved personal detail 1, used once. Fine.

**Y**
- "I gathered into my book": banned provenance line (profile, CTA setup). Change it to "is the kind of small change in my book".
- "Every one of these swaps lives in The Hair Hack chapter": **not verified.** The brief's book match covers only Ways 197 to 199 (layered bob, textured pixie, length with movement). Nothing given shows that the stacked bob, collarbone lob, shag or side bang are in that chapter. Narrow it to "There's more like these in The Hair Hack chapter..."
- "both of the cuts we've just talked about are in it" (pixie, layered bob): true (Ways 198, 197).
- "every one gives you the mistake, the swap and the payoff": the profile says *most* Ways open on the mistake. A slight overstatement, though the profile's own promise line says "each with the mistake". Low risk.
- "a heavy line sitting right at the jaw draws a ruler across the one place you'd rather people didn't stare": mild body-adjacent tone. Acceptable, but it could go on the cut instead.
- "Fine and gray hair can be more fragile": hedged with "can". Acceptable.
- "short, piecey hair hides the scalp far better than long hair that's been stretched thin": a styling claim, plausible. Fine.

Neither script makes a medical, diet or price claim. Both give correct heat-protectant and low-setting advice.

## 5. Verdict

**Publish Y.** On the owner's own test ("it didn't flow"), Y wins clearly: 8.0 against 6.6 as a listener. It sounds like one woman who understands the viewer. X sounds like a well-made checklist. That is close to the problem the owner already heard.

**Y needs a short edit first (about 10 minutes, all sentence-level):**
1. "I gathered into my book" becomes "in my book" (banned line).
2. Delete "And which of these seven are you thinking about for your next appointment?" so it ends on the question about her (hard-rule fail).
3. "Every one of these swaps lives in The Hair Hack chapter" becomes "There's more like these in The Hair Hack chapter" (unverified claim and a text-to-speech heteronym).
4. Cut "granny" from 6 uses to 2 or 3, with one "forgive the word" near the start.
5. Trim the "Now notice what I didn't say..." paragraph so cut one starts nearer 2:15 than 2:56 (this also moves the book closer to a third of the way in).
6. Change "instead of a curtain" (cheekbone layers) to keep the curtain image for the shag only. Optional: one "instantly" in the opening and drop "reads as young".

**Would a light edit fix X?** No. X's rules are clean, but its flow problem is in its structure: a 31-second opening with no picture of the granny cut, and the same define / ask-for / mirror-test / payoff template seven times. Fixing it means writing a real opening, replacing "Number N." with reasoned bridges and cutting about half the definitions and mirror tests. That is a medium rewrite, not a light edit. X's best pieces could be carried into Y: the round/square-face cliffhanger around the book aside, "Keep the length you love. Just give it somewhere to move." and the shop-window line.

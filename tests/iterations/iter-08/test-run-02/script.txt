The right haircut after 60 can take ten years off your face, or it can frame the very things you'd rather nobody noticed.

Which one is your cut doing right now?

A haircut flatters through three things: where its length ends, where the volume sits, and what the bangs and the part do.

Get those three right, and these cuts can flatter any face shape.

These five French haircuts are built exactly that way, and every one of them dries on its own.

Cut number one. The French bob.

It sits somewhere between the chin and the jaw, the ends are a little undone, and plenty of French women wear it with bangs.

What it doesn't have is a stacked, shaved back.

You know the one, short and tight at the nape and piled high at the back.

That shape was made to be set and sprayed, and I'd leave it behind, because it's the version that makes a bob look dated.

So how do you make the French one fit your face?

There's an old trick for finding your shape, and it's rough, but it helps.

Pull your hair straight back, stand close to the bathroom mirror, and trace the outline of your face on the glass with a bar of soap or an old lipstick.

An oval face suits almost anything.

A round face wants length past the chin and an off-center part.

A square face wants soft ends around the jaw.

A long face wants bangs and some width at the sides.

And a heart-shaped face, wider at the forehead, wants fullness down at chin level.

Now, the bob itself. On an oval, heart or square face, have it cut just below the jaw, with the ends point-cut.

That means the stylist snips into the ends with the scissor tips, so they finish soft.

A hard line right at the jaw puts a straight edge across the widest part of a square face, but a soft edge breaks it up.

On a round face, take it past the chin and sweep it into a side part.

The extra length pulls the eye down, so your face looks longer.

But in the chair, your words matter more than any photo, because "bob" covers very different cuts.

Ask for a chin-length bob cut to air-dry, with soft, textured ends and no graduation at the nape.

Graduation is that stacked layering at the back.

If you smooth it with a round brush or a flat iron, put a heat protectant on first, and keep the heat lower on fine or gray hair.

If your old bob only looked right on the day it was cut, it was built for a blow-dry almost nobody does at home.

The way I was raised in Paris, a haircut had to dry on its own and still look right weeks later.

Many French women still cut their hair that way, with the color close to their own shade or the gray worn on purpose.

A bob like that grows out without a fight, and it looks like you chose it on purpose.

If bangs make you nervous, the next cut is the gentlest way to try them.

Cut number two. Curtain bangs on a shoulder-length cut.

If the last bangs you had took months to grow out, I understand why you'd hesitate.

These are a different animal.

They're parted in the center, shortest just below the brow and longest at the cheekbones, and they sweep away to both sides like a pair of curtains.

Because they open in the middle, they never sit flat across your forehead like a heavy straight row.

They take the eye to your eyes and your cheekbones, which is exactly where you want it.

I used to think bangs belonged to younger faces, and I changed my mind.

Each face shape gets something different from them.

On a long face, they shorten the length, because they cover part of the forehead.

On a heart-shaped face, they balance the wider forehead.

On a square face, they work as long as the ends of the cut fall past the jaw, so nothing stops right at the corner.

And if your face is round, keep the part a touch off center and let the longest pieces fall below the cheekbones.

So what do you say in the chair?

Ask for curtain bangs that start just below the brow at the center and blend into layers at the cheekbones.

The word "blend" matters, because it means the bangs grow into the rest of the cut instead of hanging there on their own.

They need a trim every four to six weeks, but plenty of salons will tidy bangs between haircuts, so ask about that when you book.

When they're damp, push them out to each side with your fingers and let them dry.

If they fall open again by themselves the next morning, they were cut well.

They frame your face with your hair down or pulled back into a low ponytail, and your eyes look brighter either way.

Don't be surprised if someone asks what you've changed and can't put a finger on it.

Now, if your hair is fine and every cut seems to go flat by lunchtime, the next one was made for you.

Cut number three. The collarbone lob.

Lob is short for a long bob, and this one ends right at the collarbone, with long face-framing pieces starting at the cheekbone.

Face-framing pieces are the front sections, cut a little shorter than the rest, so they fall around your face instead of straight down.

If I had to choose one length for almost every face, I'd choose this one.

Here's why it works on so many faces.

The length ends well below the jaw, so it lengthens a round face.

The pieces around the jaw are soft, so a square jaw looks gentler.

And the weight stays at the ends, which is the part that matters most if your hair is fine.

Fine hair looks thin when the ends are thinned out, because light passes straight through them.

But when the ends are left full, the whole length looks thicker, right up to the crown.

So the most useful words you can say in the chair are about the ends.

Ask for collarbone length, long layers from the cheekbone down, and ends left full, not thinned out.

If you say only one sentence, say "please don't thin my ends."

Thinning shears are the scissors with teeth that take out bulk, and fine hair has no bulk to spare.

If your hair has thinned over the years, a cut like this makes the most of what's there.

But if it's coming out suddenly or in patches, take that to your doctor before you take it to a haircut.

Then there's the styling.

It air-dries.

Rough-dry it with your fingers, tuck one side behind your ear, which I love on this cut, and you're done, without a round brush and without holding your arms up behind your head.

Sit down in front of the mirror and take a photo from the front.

The ends should rest on your collarbone, and the front pieces should start around your cheekbones.

If the ends look see-through in the photo, ask for them to be left fuller next time.

If any cut on this list is going to take ten years off, my bet is this one.

Hair that moves when you turn your head looks younger than hair that's held in place, and this cut moves.

The next one sounds like the biggest step of the five, but plenty of women find it the easiest to wear day to day.

Cut number four. The soft pixie.

If short hair frightens you, it's probably because of one particular short cut.

The tight perm, set in rollers, flat at the sides and sprayed stiff at the crown.

For a long time, the advice handed to women of a certain age was to go short and have it set.

The soft pixie is short too, but it's the opposite of that cut in every other way.

It's two to three inches on top, the sides and the nape are cut with scissors, and wispy pieces fall at the ears, the nape and the forehead.

Nothing is set and nothing is sprayed.

The length on top gives fine hair lift at the crown, where it tends to go flat first.

And the soft edges keep your face from looking boxed in.

It's at its best on oval and heart-shaped faces, because those faces carry short sides easily.

But a round face can wear it as well, with more height on top and the front swept to one side, so the eye travels up and across instead of around.

On a long or square face, I'd pick the bob or the lob first, and there's nothing wrong with that.

Ask for a soft pixie with length on top, no clippers, and wispy pieces at the ears and nape.

"No clippers" is the part I'd say twice, because clippers leave a hard, close edge that looks severe on a softer face.

Is your hair silver, or on its way there?

Then this cut does a lot for you, because a sharp short cut makes gray look intentional, as if you picked the color yourself.

If your silver has gone dull, ask your colorist about a clear or silver gloss, which is a thin coat that adds shine.

A silver gloss is still color, so do a patch test 48 hours before, at home or in the salon, as the box instructions tell you, because a reaction to dye can be serious.

And if the gray turns yellow, which colorists call brassy, a purple shampoo now and then takes it back toward silver.

With the gray shining and the top lifted, your whole face looks brighter and more awake.

But maybe you've heard all this and you still want your length.

Then keep it, because the last cut is for you.

Cut number five. The long layered cut that pins up.

Have you worn your hair long for most of your life?

You don't have to cut it short because you've turned 60.

Long hair goes wrong when it's one length all the way down, because that single heavy line pulls the face down with it.

This cut keeps the length and takes away the drag.

It sits at the shoulder or a little below, with long layers that start at the chin, so the hair moves around your face instead of hanging in a sheet.

It suits oval, square and long faces, and I think it's the most elegant way to wear long hair.

On a long face, the layers add width at the sides, and if you add the curtain bangs from earlier, your face looks shorter too.

On a square face, the layers that start at the chin fall around the jaw and soften the corners.

On a heart-shaped face, those same layers put fullness at chin level, right where it's needed.

And on a round face, wear the part off center and keep a little lift at the crown.

On most days, let it air-dry.

On the other days, twist it into a loose, low bun at the nape, what the French call a chignon.

A low chignon is kinder to stiff shoulders than a high one, because your arms don't have to reach far over your head.

It lifts your whole face, it shows your neck and your earrings, and it takes a couple of minutes.

Ask for long layers from the chin, enough length for a low bun, and no heavy blunt line.

A blunt line is where every hair ends at the same length. It can look crisp on a short cut, but on long hair it makes the ends look thick and heavy.

Before you leave the chair, twist it up once and see whether the shorter layers fall softly around your face.

If they stick out at odd angles, the layers start too high, and that's an easy fix while you're still sitting there.

Worn down, it moves with you, and pinned up, you look polished without a single tool.

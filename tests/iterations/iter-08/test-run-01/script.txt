Plenty of women over 60 decide jeans make them look older, and they stop wearing them.

What's really aging them is one mistake, the wrong pair worn the wrong way.

Fix that, and three pairs can take ten years off.

You know the look. A soft, faded pair that narrows at the ankle, a long tunic on top, and the hem bunched on your shoes. It feels comfortable, but in the mirror the outfit looks older than you feel.

All of that comes from the jeans and the top, and all of it can change.

So here's the mistake, piece by piece, then the three pairs to wear instead.

The mistake comes in four parts, and the first part is the wash.

A light or faded pair puts the palest color on the widest part of your leg. Whiskering makes it worse. That's the trade's name for the pale streaks fanned across the lap and the tops of the thighs. Pale colors come forward and dark ones sit back, so the eye goes straight to the one place you'd rather it skipped.

You can check your own pair in a minute. Stand at a window in daylight and look down at the front of your jeans. Wherever the denim is palest is where the eye lands first, yours included.

The second part is the cut. The tapered mom jean and most pull-on styles feel easy to wear, but they're full through the hip and narrow at the ankle. That narrow ankle does the damage. Set next to it, the hip looks wider than it really is, by contrast alone.

The third part is the hem. A full-length pair left long seems harmless, but the extra denim gathers in folds on top of your shoe. Those folds cut the leg off at the bottom, and a shorter leg makes the whole figure look shorter and wider. They also swallow the shoe, which is a shame when it's a good one.

The fourth part is the long tunic. I understand why you reach for it. If your middle has changed, covering the waistband feels like the safe thing to do.

But a top that stops at the hip cuts your body into two equal halves, and an even split makes the figure look shorter than an uneven one. Stylists work to a rule of thirds instead. One part on top and two parts below, or the other way round, looks longer than an even split.

Put the four together and you have the outfit in that mirror. Does it sound like the pair you reach for on a busy morning?

None of this means the soft pair has to go. Keep it for the garden or a slow Sunday at home. I'd just stop choosing it for the days you want to be seen.

The next thing to look at is smaller. It's the details that date a pair even when the wash and the cut are right.

Rips and heavy distressing are the first. They tie a pair to a trend from years back, and they pull a polished top down with them. Back pockets with rhinestones, stitching swirls or flaps are the second. They look like a fun detail, but they pull the eye to the seat and date the pair to the year it was bought.

Then there's the jegging. It's thin, very stretchy denim, and it shows every line underneath, and by afternoon it has bagged at the knees and the seat. I'd let the jegging go altogether. The mid-calf capri is another one. It stops at the widest part of your calf, so your leg looks shorter there, and the whole outfit looks older for it. If you love a cropped jean in summer, choose one that ends at the ankle, where the leg is narrow, instead of mid-calf.

And the low rise. If you've ever seen a soft roll above the waistband and blamed yourself, look at the rise first. A low rise sits below the softest part of your middle and pushes it up and over. That roll is the jean doing it.

If buttons are hard on your fingers some mornings, a pull-on can stay. Just look for one in a dark wash with a straight leg, so the taper and the fading go.

So if the faded, tapered pair goes to the garden, what goes in its place?

The first pair is the everyday jean, and it's the one I'd buy first.

Look for a dark indigo in a clean rinse, with no fading and no whiskering. One even, dark color from waist to hem keeps the leg as one long, unbroken line, and nothing pulls the eye to the hip.

The leg should be straight or slim-straight. It falls from the hip in a straight line, so there's no narrow ankle making the hip look wider. Two well-known straight legs to try on are the Levi's five-oh-one and the N Y D J Marilyn Straight. I name them only as examples of the shape. You don't need premium denim for any of this.

Then the rise. The rise is the distance from the crotch seam up to the top of the waistband, and you want a mid-to-high one. A mid rise is roughly eight to ten inches, and a high rise is about ten and a half inches and up. Labels vary, but the stated front rise is a number you can go by, so read that before you trust the name on the tag. A rise in that range sits at or above the softest part of your middle, so the waistband lies flat instead of pushing anything up.

The hem matters most. Have it end at the ankle bone or just above it, so the narrowest part of your leg shows. The eye follows the leg down to that point and stops, and the leg looks longer for it.

Check the stretch on the label too. Some stretch helps, but too much is how a jean turns into a jegging. About one to two percent elastane lets the denim move with you and still keep its shape after a long day.

Now, how to wear it. Tuck just the front of your top in, or wear a short knit that ends near the waistband, and you have the one to two split back. Add a blazer or a trench as the third piece. I keep one good coat by the front door, and over dark jeans it does the same job a blazer does. Finish with a loafer, a pointed flat or a low block heel. The low block heel is the steady choice if your balance isn't what it was.

Stand side-on to a full-length mirror and look at where the hem lands. You want to see the ankle bone, or just above it.

Get those details right, and this is the pair that takes ten years off before you've even chosen your earrings.

If your hips or your tummy are the part you'd rather balance, the second pair was cut for you.

It's the elongator: a high-rise wide-leg, or a trouser jean, which is a jean cut like a tailored trouser with a fuller, straight leg. Choose a dark wash or a clean medium wash, again with no fading.

Which of the two? The trouser jean has a cleaner front and looks dressier, so it goes to lunch or the theater as easily as the market. The softer wide-leg is the more relaxed of the two. Both do the same job for your shape.

Maybe you've avoided wide legs because you worried they'd add bulk. I'd ask you to try one anyway. It sounds backwards, but a wide leg skims the hip and falls straight down from its fullest point. Nothing narrows below it, so there's no contrast to make the hip look bigger, and the width at the hem balances the width above.

This pair is worn full length. Have it hemmed to about half an inch off the floor, in the shoes you'll actually wear with it. Take those shoes to the tailor. Half an inch clear also keeps the hem from catching your heel on the stairs.

The leg is generous, but the top should be neat. A tucked top or a fitted knit shows where your waist is, and the wide leg does the rest. If you'd rather not tuck, wear a top in the same color as the jeans. A navy knit over dark indigo makes one long column from shoulder to floor, and the eye travels the whole length without a break.

For shoes, a slight heel or a platform loafer. If you choose the platform, pick one with a broad, flat base you can walk in steadily.

Walk across the room in the finished outfit, then step up onto a stair. The hem should clear the floor, and the toe of your shoe should just show.

The result is the longest line you can get from a pair of jeans, and you'll look taller in it.

Those first two are dark. The third pair goes the other way, and it's the polished one.

It's an ecru or cream jean with a straight leg. I'd choose cream over the bright optic white, because cream is softer next to the skin and the eye doesn't stop on it the way it stops on a glaring white.

Keep the same rise as the first pair, mid to high. Ankle length or full length both work, but let your shoes decide, using the same hem rules as the first two pairs.

One check matters more here than anywhere. Light denim needs to be a heavier weight, or the pocket linings show through at the front. In the fitting room, look straight at the front of the jeans in good light. If you can see the outline of the pockets, put that pair back.

What if light color on your lower half makes you nervous? Wear a dark third piece over it, like a navy blazer or a trench left open. The two dark edges down the front frame the cream, and they keep the eye moving up and down instead of across.

Then wear it with navy, camel, gray, or a striped knit. Each of those has enough depth to keep the cream looking fresh and expensive, and none of them fights it. A navy and cream stripe is my favorite of the four, because it carries the cream up to your face and ties the whole outfit together.

Have you been saving light jeans for summer only? Cream looks like a warm-weather color, but a cream pair with a navy knit works from spring right through fall.

Before you buy any of the three, there are a few checks to run in the fitting room.

First, the waistband. It should lie flat all the way round, with no gap at the back when you stand. If a pair fits your hips but gaps at the waist, don't walk away from it. A tailor can take in a waist.

Second, the back pockets. Most stylists suggest medium pockets, centered on each side and not set low. Pockets set low make the seat look lower and longer.

Third, sit down. Have you ever bought a pair that felt fine standing up and dug in the moment you sat in the car? Sit on the fitting room bench for a minute. The waistband shouldn't dig in, and the back shouldn't slip down when you lean forward. If it passes sitting, it'll pass the whole day.

A lifetime of dressing well comes down to this with jeans: the fit and the hem matter far more than the price. A hem is the best money you'll spend in your closet. Think about how often you'd wear a pair that fits. Twice a week for two years is a lot of wearing, and each time you put it on, it costs you very little. Ask for an original hem if you want the jean to look untouched. The tailor keeps the jean's own worn edge and sews it back on at the new length.

And the shoes finish it. Athletic sneakers are comfortable, but they drag any of these three pairs down and make a good outfit look older, however well the jeans fit. I'd keep them for walking. If you want the comfort of a sneaker, choose a clean white leather one. A loafer or an ankle boot works too.

If you change one thing after this, make it the first pair, and have it hemmed at the ankle bone before you wear it out.

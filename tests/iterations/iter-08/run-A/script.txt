A good haircut can make a woman over sixty look younger almost instantly. The wrong one, and forgive me for the word, is the "granny" cut, and it adds years before you've said a thing.

Which of the two is in your mirror right now?

And this part surprises most women. The years come from the shape of the cut, so you can keep your length and still lose them. That shape is short and rounded, set or sprayed, flat at the back, with a dome on top.

Number one. The textured pixie.

The rounded short cut sits on the head as one solid shape, with the same smooth outline from every side. The eye travels around that outline and finds nothing to stop on, so the whole look feels set in place.

If you've worn one short style for years, changing it can feel like giving up the only thing you knew how to manage. But you can keep your hair short. The change is in the shape of the cut.

A textured pixie is just as short, but it's cut to move. Ask for lift at the crown, the high point at the back of your head. Ask for piecey ends, which means the tips are cut into separate little pieces instead of one smooth edge. Then a clean nape, that's the hairline at the back of your neck, and a soft side fringe across your forehead.

I think this is the best short cut there is for fine or thinning hair. And if a short cut has never stayed up for you, the round shape was the trouble all along. It was built for thick hair that holds a set, and fine hair was never going to hold it. Piecey ends weigh almost nothing, so the crown stays up without spray, and that lift looks like more hair.

A cut can make thin hair look fuller, but sudden shedding, or a new patch where the scalp shows, is a question for a doctor before it's a question for a stylist.

A pixie wants a trim every four to six weeks. Leave it longer, and it grows out into a bowl, which puts you right back where you started.

In a photo of yourself from the side, you want a little height at the crown and a tidy neck underneath. That's a cut that looks lighter, lifted and younger.

Number two. The modern layered bob.

The bob you may remember from years ago was a blunt cut. That just means every hair was cut straight across to end at the same place along the jaw. The trouble is where that line sits. It draws a heavy, straight edge exactly at the jaw, and the eye stops right there, on the spot that softens first with time. A blunt bob can look neat on the day it's cut, but the ends get heavy, and heavy hair hangs flat against your cheeks.

So what does a current bob look like?

It has internal layers. Those are shorter pieces cut inside the hair, underneath, where you can't see them. They take the weight out, so the bob swings when you turn your head. Then it has soft face-framing, a few pieces at the front cut a little shorter so they curve in toward your cheekbones. Those pieces draw the eye up, toward your eyes, instead of down to the jaw.

At jaw length, I love this cut on an oval or a heart-shaped face. An oval face is a little longer than it is wide. A heart shape is wider at the forehead and narrower at the chin.

In the salon, ask for internal layers, soft pieces around the face, and no blunt line at the jaw.

At home, shake your head gently in front of the mirror. If the ends swing and settle, the layers are working. If the whole bob moves as one block, there's still too much weight in it. And if you love a sleek, smooth bob, you can keep it sleek. Just ask for the weight to come out underneath.

But what if your face is round, or a little square? Then jaw length isn't the length I'd choose for you.

Number three. The collarbone lob, a little longer in front.

A lob is a long bob, and this one ends around the collarbone. The detail that makes it work is at the front. It's cut slightly longer than the back, so the line slopes gently forward and down toward your chin.

On a round or square face, that slope does something a straight line can't. A straight line across the bottom sits at the same width as the cheeks or the jaw, and it repeats that width. A line that angles forward draws the eye down on a diagonal. The face looks longer and a touch narrower, and the neck looks longer too.

But keep the angle small. About an inch, no more. Any steeper and the front hangs in two long points while the back sits short, and then the cut is the first thing anyone notices instead of your face.

In the chair, ask for collarbone length, about an inch longer in front, with soft ends.

It's also a comfortable length if you've been nervous about going shorter, but you still want a change you can see. There's enough hair to tuck behind an ear, or to pull back on a hot day, and it still sits off your shoulders.

Stand side-on to the mirror, or ask someone to take a photo of your profile. The line should dip toward the front just enough that you notice it.

Has your face filled out a little at the cheeks over the years? Then this is the length I'd start with.

Number four. The soft stacked bob.

Have you ever seen a photo of yourself from the side and wondered where the shape of your head went? That's the back. On plenty of women, the back of the head falls almost straight from the crown to the neck, with no curve at all. Hair that's gotten a little finer at the crown makes it flatter still. You can't fix that with more spray, but you can fix it with the cut. And this is the one part of a haircut you almost never see head-on, while everyone behind you sees it every day.

A flat back changes the whole profile. From the side, the head loses its natural curve, and the cut looks limp and heavy, even when the front is styled beautifully. This is the mark of that old rounded set I'd most like to see go.

A stacked bob fixes it with graduation. That's the word stylists use for hair cut shorter at the nape and a little longer as it goes up, so each layer rests on the one below, like shingles on a roof. Those layers build a soft curve right where the head was flat.

But there's a version of this cut that looks harsher than the flat back ever did. That's the sharp wedge, a steep shelf at the back with the hair very short underneath and very full on top. It looks severe, and it can look like a cut from another decade.

So the words you use matter. Ask for a soft stack, not a sharp wedge. Ask for the graduation to be gentle, and for the back to stay rounded rather than pointed.

And if you've ever left a salon with a back that didn't look the way you pictured, bring a photo. Bring a soft stacked bob seen from the side and from behind. A picture of the back says what words can't, and it gives your stylist something clear to work toward.

Before I go out, I take one last look in the mirror by my front door, and the back is what I check. You can do the same with a hand mirror. Turn your back to a large mirror and hold the small one up in front of you. You're looking for a gentle curve from the crown down to the nape. If holding a mirror up is a strain on your arms, a phone photo taken from behind does the same job.

With a curve back there, the head looks fuller, the neck looks longer, and the cut keeps its shape with very little styling. From the side, that soft curve takes years off, and it's the angle people see most.

Now, if your hair is long and you love it, the next one is for you.

Number five. Long hair, with soft layers that start at the cheekbone.

Maybe you've heard that long hair is a mistake after sixty. That advice can sting, especially when your hair is the part of your look you're proudest of. So let me be plain about it. Length is fine. If you love it, keep it.

What ages long hair is one heavy line. When every strand ends at the same place, all the weight collects at the bottom. It pulls the hair flat against the head, and it pulls the eye down the face with it. The face looks longer and more tired than it really is.

So what changes? The layers do, and the length stays.

Ask for soft layers that start at the cheekbone and fall from there. The shortest layer touches your cheekbone, so it lifts and frames the face right where you want the eye to go. Then ask for the ends to be point-cut. That means the stylist snips up into the ends with the tips of the scissors. They finish soft and a little feathered, instead of in one hard edge.

I'd always choose movement over length, but with layers like these, you don't have to choose.

Turn your head from side to side in front of the mirror. Long hair that's cut well swings out and falls back into place. Hair that hangs in one flat sheet needs the layers.

Keep the length you love. Just give it somewhere to move.

Number six. The soft shag, with curtain framing.

This one is for wavy hair, and for anyone who's tired of fighting her waves with a round brush every morning. Do you have waves you've been blowing straight for years?

A shag is a cut with lots of layers through the crown and the sides, shorter on top and longer toward the ends, so the hair has texture all the way through. Curtain framing is a fringe parted in the middle that sweeps away to both sides, like a pair of curtains pulled open.

On wavy hair, all those layers let each wave sit where it falls, instead of being pressed flat by the weight of the hair below. The waves get their spring back. And the curtain framing opens up the face and draws the eye out toward the cheekbones.

But a heavy, choppy shag can look messy rather than current. So ask for a soft shag, with the layers blended and the curtain pieces long enough to tuck behind your ears.

I love this cut for how little it asks of you. Wash it, scrunch in a little product, and let it air-dry in minutes. No round brush, no arms held up through a long blow-dry, and no fighting your own hair.

If you do reach for a dryer or a curling iron, put on a heat protectant first. That's a spray or cream that coats the hair so the heat is gentler on it. And on fine or gray hair, keep the tool on a low setting.

The result looks soft and relaxed, and it takes almost no work to get there.

The last cut puts all the attention on your eyes.

Number seven. The chin-length bob, with a wispy side-swept bang.

A heavy, blunt fringe is a thick block of hair cut straight across the forehead. It's a strong look, but it draws a hard line right across the face. It sits heavy on the brows and puts the eyes in shadow. That straight line also makes the face look shorter and wider.

A wispy side-swept bang does the opposite job. It's a lighter fringe, cut thin enough to see through, that falls on a slant from a side part. That slant is a diagonal, and a diagonal lifts. It points the eye up and across, toward the eyes and the cheekbones. You still get the softness of a fringe, but your brows show through it.

Pair it with a bob that ends at the chin, and the bang and the ends of the bob both curve in toward the cheekbones. The whole face looks lifted.

If you've never had a fringe, I think a side-swept one is the gentlest way to try. It's long enough to tuck away on a day you don't want it, and it grows out more gracefully than a blunt one.

In the chair, ask for a thin, wispy bang swept to one side, and a chin-length bob with soft ends. Then look straight into the mirror. If you can see your eyebrows through the bang, it's light enough.

Of all seven, this is the most instant. It changes the face the moment the bang falls into place, and it can make you look younger the first time you catch yourself in a shop window.

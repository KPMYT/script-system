SCRIPT TYPE: Talking Head

The way I was raised in Paris, a woman fixed the fit long before she worried about the size.

So you'll rarely see a French woman over 60 look frumpy, and it has nothing to do with her weight.

Frumpy comes from fit, proportion, fabric and finish, and every one of those can be fixed at any size, from your own closet.

Here are the five rules they dress by, and how to borrow each one with American clothes.

Rule number one. Fit comes before size.

Stand in front of your mirror in the top you wear most. Where does the shoulder seam sit?

On a lot of women, it hangs down past the shoulder, the sleeves cover the knuckles, and the trousers pool on top of the shoe.

If your body has changed and nothing in the store seems cut for it, going up a size feels like the safe choice. I understand that completely.

But the extra fabric doesn't hide anything. It adds width at the shoulder, the bust, the hip and the hem, so the whole shape looks bigger than you are, and the outfit looks tired.

Nobody tells you that store sizes are cut on one average body. Almost nobody matches it at every point, at any age, so nearly everything off the rack needs a small change somewhere.

So what do French women do about it?

Most of them see the tailor as a normal part of buying clothes. They buy the size that fits at the shoulders, because shoulders are the hardest thing to alter, and they have the rest adjusted. I'd spend on the tailor long before I'd spend on a bigger size.

The trousers get hemmed. The waist gets taken in. The sleeves come up to the wrist bone.

A good tailor starts every jacket at the shoulder seam, and you can use the same rule in the fitting room.

Put the jacket on and find that seam. It should sit right at the edge of your shoulder bone.

Then reach forward as if you're hugging someone, and sit down in it. If it pulls across the back or rides up at the neck, it's too small. If you could slip a thick sweater underneath with room to spare, it's too big.

For trousers, the hem should just brush the top of your shoe, or stop at the ankle bone if they're cropped.

Fit doesn't mean tight, and this is where a lot of women push back, so I'll be clear. If your middle is fuller, the right fit skims past it and falls straight. It doesn't cling, and it doesn't balloon.

And if buttons have started giving your fingers trouble, choose a pull-on trouser that fits well at the waist, and let the tailor take care of the hem.

None of these five rules asks you to lose weight, and this first one shows why. Your body stays exactly the same, and only the cloth changes.

Do it for the three pieces you wear most, and someone will ask if your jacket is new. It's the same jacket, with the sleeves taken up.

But fit can only do so much when the colors in your closet won't go together.

Rule number two. Keep a small wardrobe of neutrals, with one accent color.

Open your closet and count the colors. There might be a red top, a teal cardigan, three different prints and a mustard jacket. Each one is lovely on its own, but each one needs its own trousers and its own shoes.

That's why a full closet can still leave you with nothing to wear. Every outfit starts from scratch. The pieces that don't match get worn with whatever's clean, so the colors fight and the eye jumps from one to the next.

Most French women go the other way. They keep a smaller wardrobe built on neutrals, and they buy fewer, better pieces and repeat them.

The neutrals are navy, black, gray, camel, cream and white. You don't need all six, but pick two or three that look good next to your face, and let them carry almost everything.

If black looks harsh against your skin in daylight, navy or gray will do the same job more gently.

Then add one accent color, and put it where it does the most good, in a silk scarf or on your lips.

So what about the woman who loves color? You keep it. Make your favorite color the accent, and wear it against the neutrals, so it gets to be the star instead of fighting three others.

And repeating the same outfit twice in one week is fine. Plenty of French women have a uniform, a few combinations they wear over and over, and nobody thinks less of them for it.

I like this rule for a practical reason too. A good coat that goes with everything gets worn all winter, every winter, so it costs less each time you put it on than a bargain you wore twice.

My own habit is one in, one out. When something new comes into my closet, something old leaves.

Take any top you own this afternoon, and see whether it goes with three of your bottoms. If it only goes with one, that top is the piece making your mornings harder.

How many of your tops would pass?

Rule number three is the one that changes your shape in the mirror the fastest. Add a third piece, and give the outfit a line.

Think of the long, loose tunic over wide trousers. It's comfortable, and I understand why it feels safe when your middle is fuller. It covers the part you'd rather not think about.

But it falls in one width from your shoulders to your knees. There's no point where the body narrows, so the eye takes the whole thing in as one wide block, and you end up looking wider and shorter than you are.

What works better is a column with a frame around it.

Wear your top and your trousers in one color, or two shades that sit close together, like navy on navy, or cream with white. That unbroken color from neck to shoe makes one long, straight line.

Then put a third piece over it, and leave it open. A trench, a well-cut blazer, or an open cardigan. I'd start with whichever one you already own.

The open edges make two more long lines down the front of you, and the column underneath looks narrower.

If you want to show a waist, try a front tuck. That's tucking just the front few inches of your top into the waistband and leaving the back loose. It tells the eye where your waist is without squeezing anything.

If you don't like tucking, don't. A top that ends at the hip, or a jacket that stops there, gives you the same line.

Dark straight jeans work well here, because the straight leg falls cleanly from the hip and the dark color keeps the column long.

And under that open blazer, you'll often see French women in the striped Breton top. It started as part of the French navy uniform back in the nineteenth century, and it still looks crisp under a plain jacket.

The open cardigan is the softest way into this rule. There are no buttons to fight, it's warm, and it still frames you.

Take a full-length photo on your phone, from the front and from the side, once without the third piece and once with it. Look for the place where your body narrows. If you can't find it in the first photo but you can in the second, the rule is doing its job.

You'll look taller, and longer through the middle, in the same trousers you already own.

Once the outfit has a line, the eye travels to the two ends of it, your feet and your face. We'll start at the feet.

Rule number four. Polished shoes, and quiet accessories.

Look down at your feet in the mirror. If you wear athletic sneakers with everything, that thick white sole is often the heaviest thing in the whole outfit.

It pulls the eye straight down, and it makes a nice pair of trousers look like they're on the way to the gym.

I'd keep the running shoes for real walks. For every other day, you'll often see French women in ballet flats, loafers or a low heel.

Comfort comes first, and I mean that. Feet change, and so does balance. If you're less steady than you used to be, choose a shoe with a back and a sole that grips, and keep any heel low and wide.

A heel can look lovely, but a steady flat looks better than any heel you're nervous in. A loafer has a closed back and a firm sole, so it's one of the easiest swaps you can make.

Then the accessories. The French habit is one statement at a time.

That might be a silk scarf, which plenty of French women tie on almost every day, in the accent color from rule two. A silk scarf near your face puts color and a little shine right where you want people to look.

If the scarf is the statement, the earrings stay simple and the necklace stays home.

The bag counts too. A structured bag with no visible logo, in one of your neutrals, looks finished with anything. A big logo, or a soft tote stuffed to bursting, pulls the attention onto the bag and away from you.

Before you leave the house, count the things trying to get noticed. The print, the necklace, the earrings, the bag, the shoes. If more than one of them is loud, take one off.

I think that's the hardest habit to break, but it's the one that makes an outfit look calm and expensive without anyone knowing why.

From the feet, we go up to the other end of the line.

Rule number five. Keep your hair and your face low maintenance.

Think about how long your hair takes in the morning. If it needs rollers, a round brush and a cloud of hairspray to look right, and it's flat again by lunch, the style is working against you.

A stiff, set style belongs to a different decade, and so does a harsh, dark dye against softer skin. Both take effort that shows, and effort that shows is what makes a look seem older.

The French way is the opposite. You'll often see a cut that looks good dried on its own, and still looks right as it grows out.

So what do you say in the stylist's chair? Tell her you want a cut that dries well without much work, and still holds its shape six weeks later. Then ask her to show you where she plans to cut before she starts.

If your hair is gray, or going gray, wear it on purpose. I love gray with a sharp cut and some shine, but it needs both, or it can look flat.

Ask your colorist about a gloss, which is a sheer treatment that adds shine, and use a light shine spray at home.

If you've colored your hair for years, the thought of stopping can feel like a much bigger step than it sounds. That's normal, but nobody's asking you to decide today.

And if you love your color, keep it. But choose a shade a little softer than the one you've always used, so it doesn't fight your skin.

The face follows the same rule. Many French women keep it close to bare, with groomed brows, a little mascara and a red or berry lip.

Skincare comes before makeup, and plenty of them buy it from the pharmacy shelf rather than a department store counter.

Check your hair and face by a window in daylight, not under the bathroom light, because daylight is the light everyone else sees you in.

Behind all five rules sits an attitude the French have a phrase for, being comfortable in your own skin, at your own age. I'd say that's the real reason frumpy so rarely gets a foothold.

When your hair dries on its own and your face takes a few minutes, you walk out the door looking rested, and looking like yourself.

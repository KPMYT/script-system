Folks at a craft fair pick up your work, turn it over and set it right back down.
It happens to everybody, and this build fixes it: $21 and change in big box parts, no saw cuts at all, and you've got an 18-inch lazy Susan I'd price at $90.
And it isn't the wood that sells it.

First, one 18-inch pine round from Lowe's, about $14.
It's solid pine, glued up from strips at the mill, and it already has a rounded edge milled all the way around.
So there's nothing left to cut, and no router to buy.
Lowe's even lists lazy Susans as one of the things it's made for.

Next, the bearing.
In the cabinet hardware aisle, grab a 6-inch square lazy Susan turntable, about $8.
That's two steel plates with ball bearings running between them.
A bearing that size is generally rated for tops from about 15 to 30 inches across, so this round sits comfortably inside that range.

The rest is finishing supplies, and you may have half of it on a shelf already.
A small can of pre-stain wood conditioner, a half pint of oil-based stain, wipe-on polyurethane, a sheet each of 150 and 220 grit sandpaper, four number eight wood screws five-eighths of an inch long, and a pack of one-inch felt pads.
For the name on top, one oil-based paint marker, about $6 at an office supply store.
Prices subject to change.

Tools are short, too.
A sander or a sanding block, a drill with a five sixty-fourths bit, a screwdriver, a tape measure, a pencil and some clean rags.
There's no cut list on this one, because there's nothing to cut.

Now the build, and it starts with sanding.
The round comes surfaced smooth, so skip the coarse paper.
Go straight to 150 grit on the top and around the edge, then finish at 220.
Sand with the grain, every pass.
Any scratch you leave across the grain turns into a dark line the minute stain hits it.
Wear a dust mask while you do it, because fine sanding dust isn't something to breathe all afternoon.
Then wipe the dust off the top, the edge and the bottom.

Right now it's a clean pine circle, and a clean pine circle won't get anybody a good price.
Two things change that, and the first one goes on before any color does.

The first thing is pre-stain wood conditioner.
Pine drinks stain unevenly, soaking it up in some spots and barely taking it in others, and that blotchy look reads as cheap from across a room.
I've seen enough blotched pine to know it's the first thing the eye lands on.
Wipe the conditioner on the top and the edge, give it 15 minutes, and get your stain on within two hours.
Why pine needs that step is on page 87 of my book.

Before the stain touches the top, try it on the underside.
That's your test board, and it faces the table anyway.
Every board takes stain a little differently, even two from the same tree.
The finishing chapter of my book calls skipping that test a common mistake, and it's an easy one to make when you're in a hurry.

Now the stain.
Wipe it on with a rag, then wipe the extra off right away.
The color you keep is what soaked in.
Leave stain sitting on the surface and it dries into a sticky film that never cures right.
Give it about two hours.

Those stain rags need handling, by the way.
Oily rags can heat up and catch fire on their own, so lay them flat outside to dry, or put them in water in a sealed metal can.
Never wad them up in the trash.
My book puts that warning right in the stain technique, on page 89.

Here's the second thing, and it's the reason this gets the price.
Put a name on it.
A family name, a monogram, the year they got married, the year they bought the house.
That turns a kitchen turntable into a wedding gift or a housewarming gift, and a gift with somebody's name on it is a different sale than a spinning board.

Print the name at the size you want, then shade the back of the paper with a pencil, right over the letters.
On a dark stain, use chalk instead, so the outline shows up.
Tape the paper face up on the round, and trace every letter hard with a pen.
Lift the paper, and there's the outline on the wood.
Fill it in with the paint marker.
I'd go dark stain with white letters, because that reads from across a fair aisle.
If you're nervous about the lettering, practice the name once on scrap paper first.

Let the letters dry, then the topcoat goes over everything.
Wipe-on poly, in thin coats.
A thick coat skins over on top, stays soft underneath and never fully hardens.
Three thin coats on the top and the edge, about three hours apart, with a light pass of 220 between coats.
I'd give the bottom one coat too, so the wood's sealed all the way around.
Page 91 of my book calls flooding it on to save a coat a common mistake.
Keep the garage door open while it dries, because solvent fumes build up fast in a closed shop, and the book's safety note on page 92 says the same.

One more honest thing about the finish.
Neither store says what glue holds the strips in these rounds together.
So I'd sell this as a turntable for jars, bottles and serving dishes, and I'd tell the buyer to keep food in a dish and knives off the top.

Once the last coat's dry, it's time to mount the bearing.
Lay the round face down on an old towel, so the new finish doesn't scratch.
Set the bearing on the underside, and slide it until all four corners measure the same distance to the edge.
That's centered.
Mark the four holes in the plate that touches the wood.
Drill pilot holes with the five sixty-fourths bit, about half an inch deep.
The round is only about an inch thick, so a deeper hole risks coming through the top you just finished.
If the other plate covers a hole, turn it until the hole's clear.
Drive the four screws in snug.
Then stick a felt pad on each corner of the loose plate, the one that rides on the table, so there's no bare steel on somebody's dining table.

Flip it over and give it a spin with your hands on the wood, not near the plates, because the gap between them pinches.
Check all four screws once more.
A loose one can let the top come off when somebody carries it by the edge.

That's the whole build.
Sand, condition, stain, a name, three coats and four screws.

While one round's coat is drying, you're sanding the next.

TITLE: You've Been Using Hydrogen Peroxide WRONG (It Stopped Working)
CHANNEL: Roger the Pipe Guy
BRIEF (talking points):
- The problem: people heard a cup of hydrogen peroxide takes the ring out of a toilet bowl, poured one in, and the ring was still there in the morning. Peroxide does work on a toilet, but the way most people buy, store and pour it, it never had a chance. Five mistakes kill it.
- Money: a bottle of plain 3% peroxide is about $1 to $3 at any grocery store or pharmacy. A toilet visit for a clog runs about $100 to $275 depending on where you live; a new toilet installed is about $300 to $800 (price guide). People replace toilets over stains that a $2 bottle and a night of waiting would have lifted.
- Mistake 1, the bottle itself: the brown bottle in the cabinet has probably been there for years. Peroxide is water with one extra oxygen that wants off. Unopened, a bottle keeps about 3 years; opened, it starts losing strength within about a month, and somewhere between 1 and 6 months it is weak or close to plain water. Heat and light speed that up (a sunny windowsill, a warm spot over a radiator).
  - The test: splash a little into a sink drain. Good peroxide fizzes white on the grime; dead peroxide just goes down. Another test: a capful on the cut face of a raw potato foams right away if it is good (potatoes are full of an enzyme that breaks peroxide apart).
  - The fix: buy the plain 3% brown bottle, never the stronger salon or "food grade" kind (those burn skin). Write the date you opened it on the label, keep it in a cool, dark cabinet with the door shut, buy the small bottle so it gets used up.
- Mistake 2, pouring it into a full bowl: a cup of 3% dropped into a bowl of water is thinned to a trace. The ring sits at and above the waterline; the peroxide never touches it at full strength.
  - The fix: turn the shut-off valve behind the toilet (the small oval handle near the floor or wall) clockwise until it stops; if it's stiff and hasn't moved in years, don't force it (old valves can snap): just flush and scoop the bowl low with a cup into a bucket instead. Flush with the valve shut and the bowl drops to a puddle and stays there. Pour the peroxide straight onto the ring so it runs down the porcelain; soak paper towels in peroxide and press them on the ring at the waterline so it stays wet up there.
- Mistake 3, no time: the fizz is the peroxide being used up on contact; when it stops, that layer is spent and the layer under it untouched. Flushing or scrubbing during the fizz throws the work away.
  - The fix: 30 minutes is the floor, overnight is better. Bowl empty, lid down, bathroom door shut so nobody flushes by habit. In the morning, open the valve, let the tank fill, flush: the gray, pink and black comes off in the flush or wipes off with a brush and no muscle.
  - The tank: peroxide in the tank is fine as a short sit then a flush, never all night. The flapper (the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank) stiffens when a strong cleaner sits against it for hours, stops sealing, and the toilet runs.
- Mistake 4, the clear spray bottle on the counter: poured into a clear bottle by a bright window, it's dead in about a week. Light breaks peroxide down fastest; the brown bottle is a sun block. A clear bottle of water looks exactly like a clear bottle of peroxide, so you'd never know.
  - The fix: the trigger from a cheap spray bottle screws onto most brown peroxide bottles (the threads usually match; try another trigger if the first doesn't fit). Keep it under the sink. Spray the ring, under the rim and the seat hinges where the pink slime starts, then give it time. Test any clear bottle before trusting it.
- Mistake 5, asking it to do a job it doesn't do: peroxide kills and lifts living things: the pink slime (bacteria that float in the air and love damp porcelain), the black mold under the rim, the bacteria that make a bowl smell sour, and the gray or brown stains they leave. It does not dissolve rock.
  - The fingernail test: slick means living stuff (peroxide eats it); rough like sandpaper means mineral (lime and iron from the water), and peroxide fizzes on it and leaves it.
  - For mineral: an acid. The gentle one is citric acid, a powder near the canning jars or with cleaning supplies, about $10 to $15 a pound (price guide). Same method: water out, powder on the ring, time. A separate night from the peroxide.
  - Safety: never mix peroxide with vinegar or any acid in one container (peroxide plus vinegar makes peracetic acid, a corrosive that burns skin and eyes); never with bleach either. One cleaner at a time, a flush between.
  - A clog is not a peroxide job either: if the bowl water sits high and won't go down, it's a plunger job, the flange plunger (the rubber flap folds out of the cup and seals in the drain hole); a flat cup plunger doesn't seal in a toilet. If a chemical cleaner is already in the bowl, don't plunge (it splashes back). Before plunging, the tub test: if the tub gurgles when the toilet flushes, or water comes up in the shower, that's two drains backing up at once, the main line: stop running water everywhere and call. Pushing on it moves sewage into the tub.
- Where it ends: match the fix to the problem: slime, smell and the stains they leave, peroxide; the hard ring, citric acid on its own night; the clog, the plunger after the tub test. Tonight: pull the bottle out and splash a little in the sink; if it fizzes, shut the valve, flush, pour it on the ring before bed; if not, pour it out and put a new bottle on tomorrow's list.

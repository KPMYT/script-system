TEST (iter-19)

Your bathroom sink stopper is not stuck because the sink is old. It is stuck because of something on its stem, and you have never seen that stem, because nobody ever told you to lift the stopper out and look.

You push the rod behind the faucet, and the stopper stays down. You pull it, and the stopper stays up. So you jiggle the rod, you push harder, and one day you just leave it open.

Nobody mentions that the cure is a small tube of grease that costs about five dollars. That silence is the expensive part.

I am going to give you nine fixes, from the causes to the cure, and the ninth takes five minutes once a year and keeps this from ever coming back.

One honest word first. Grease does not mend a part that has cracked or worn through. That needs a new part.

Number one. Hair.

Lean under the sink and look at the back of the drain pipe, just below the bowl. You will see a nut about the size of a bottle cap, with a thin metal rod going into it. That is the pivot rod. It goes in through the nut, crosses the inside of the pipe, and lifts the stopper from below. Which means it sits in the water every time the sink drains.

And hair hooks on it. One hair catches on the rod. The next hair catches on that one. Week after week they twist together, until a gray rope is wrapped around the rod and around the stem of the stopper above it. The stopper cannot slide past the rope. It drags, then it sticks, then it stops.

Most people blame the pipe. They say the pipe is too narrow, or the house is too old. The pipe is fine. The pipe was never the problem. The problem is a rod sitting across the water, catching whatever goes past.

Here is how you check. Pull the stopper straight up and out of the drain. On many sinks it simply lifts out. On others it is held from below by that rod, and you will free it in number six. Look at the bottom of the stem. If there is hair on it, there is more hair on the rod underneath.

Now you know where the trouble hides. Not in the pipe. On a rod you have never looked at.

Number two. Toothpaste and soap.

Run your finger down a stem that has been in a sink for a few years, and it does not feel like metal. It feels like chalk with something soft underneath.

Toothpaste washes away on its own. Soap washes away on its own. But put them together on a wet metal stem, morning and night, and they set into a paste. Then hard water does what hard water does. It lays a thin white scale on top, layer by layer, until the paste is sealed under a crust.

You feel it before you see it. The stopper drags. You lift the knob and it goes halfway and stays there, and the water drains slowly around it. You push the knob down and the stopper will not quite close, so the sink empties while you are still using it.

Most people scrub the crust off, and for a while that works. Then the crust grows back, because the stem is bare and wet twice a day again, and nothing stops the paste from setting. Scrubbing only buys you a season.

The repair is what goes onto the clean stem after the scrubbing, and that is the grease. A clean stem with the right film on it gives the paste nothing to hold.

Number three. The nut somebody tightened.

Go back under the sink to that nut on the back of the drain pipe. Behind it sits a small rubber gasket, and the nut belongs hand tight. Not wrench tight. Hand tight.

But somebody always has a wrench. Maybe a previous owner, maybe someone chasing a drip in a hurry. The wrench adds one more turn, and that one turn clamps the rod so it cannot pivot. You pull the knob behind the faucet, and the knob moves. The stopper does not.

Right beside that nut hangs the clevis. That is the flat metal strap with a row of holes in it. It joins the lift rod, the one you pull behind the faucet, to the pivot rod that goes into the pipe. One small screw holds the two together. Over the years that screw works loose, the strap slips, and the knob has nothing left to pull.

Then there is the other side of the same nut. Too loose, or with its gasket flattened by that wrench, it lets out one drop every time the sink drains. One drop is nothing. But a sink drains many times a day, and each drop lands on the floor of the cabinet.

Most cabinet floors are particle board. Particle board drinks water, swells, and then crumbles. Open the cabinet and press the floor under the drain with your thumb. If it is soft, or puffed up like wet cardboard, that drop has been busy for a long time.

A small water-damage repair in one room runs about $150 to $500, depending on how far the water spread. The drop cost nothing to start. The fix for it was two fingers on a nut.

Number four. The push button.

Some of you are looking at your sink right now and thinking, I have no rod behind my faucet. You press the stopper down, and it closes. You press it again, and it pops up. No rod, no strap, no nut underneath.

That is the other kind, the push button. And you are right that the hair rope cannot form there, because there is no rod across the pipe for hair to hook on.

But under the cap of that stopper sits a small cartridge, and inside the cartridge is a little spring. The cartridge sits down in the drain, and the drain holds standing water. Hard water leaves its scale on the coils of that spring, a little at a time.

At first the click goes soft. You press, and the stopper comes up slowly, or only partway. Then one morning you press, and nothing happens at all. The stopper stays down with a full sink of water above it.

So people decide the spring is broken, and they buy a whole new stopper. Usually it is not broken. It is buried. Under the scale it is the same spring it was the day it went in, and it can come back.

Number five. The grease itself.

Here it is. Silicone plumber's grease. A small tube, usually hanging in the plumbing aisle right next to the washers, for about $5.

What it does is simple. It leaves a thin film on metal and rubber that water cannot wash off. Hair slides along it instead of hooking. Toothpaste has nothing to grip, so it never sets. A spring moves freely inside it. It belongs on O-rings, on springs, and on anything that has to slide while it is wet.

When you pick up a tube, look for one word on it. Silicone. That word matters, because the shelf has a trap on it.

Next to the grease sit petroleum jelly and a can of WD-40, and they look as if they would do the same job. They do not. Petroleum swells rubber. Silicone does not. Put petroleum on that gasket behind the nut, and it goes soft and swollen. The nut stops sealing, and you are back to the drop on the cabinet floor.

In the German trade it is called armature grease, because it goes on taps and valves, and it is standard kit in a German plumber's bag. The habit around it is different too. Americans tend to wait until the stopper sticks. In Germany it is usual to grease it before it does. The same physics on both sides of the ocean, and two different moments to reach for the tube.

That one tube goes onto four small parts of your sink in the next two numbers. And the last of them brings back a click you thought was gone for good.

Number six. The lift rod stopper, apart and back together.

This is the rod kind, the one most American sinks have, and it takes about twenty minutes and an old towel.

Before you touch anything, think back over the week. If anyone poured a chemical drain cleaner down this sink, put on gloves and goggles before the nut comes off, because what comes out of that pipe can burn skin and eyes. And nothing else goes into that drain after it. One cleaner at a time, never two.

Empty the cabinet and lay the old towel flat on its floor. A little water comes out when the nut comes off, and the towel takes it instead of the particle board.

Find the pivot nut on the back of the drain pipe and turn it counterclockwise by hand. If it will not move, use pliers, gently. Do not force it. Slide the nut back along the rod, and pull the rod straight out of the pipe. Now go up top. The stopper lifts straight out of the drain.

Take it to the counter with an old toothbrush and a drop of dish soap. Scrub the stem until every bit of hair and crust is gone. Then look down the open drain. Some of what was on the stem got past it.

A plastic drain strip, the thin one with little barbs down both sides, costs a few dollars. Feed it down the open drain and pull it up slowly. Slowly, so the barbs keep what they catch.

Wipe the pivot rod dry. Now the grease. A thin film on the ball of the rod, the round part partway along it, and a thin film on the gasket behind the nut. Thin is the word. Grease piled on thick becomes a sticky shelf for the next hair.

Drop the stopper back into the drain. Then, from below, slide the rod back through the pipe so its tip passes through the loop at the bottom of the stopper stem. Push the stopper up and down once by hand, from the top, and feel the rod catch it. If it does not catch, the tip missed the loop. Draw the rod back and try again.

Now the nut. Hand tight, two fingers, no wrench. The greased gasket does the sealing, and it does not need muscle.

Last, the clevis. Move the strap to the hole where the knob fully opens the stopper and fully closes it. Try a hole, test it, and move one hole up or down if the stopper does not seat. Then tighten the small screw.

Test it. Open, closed, open, with one finger. Watch the towel under the nut while the sink drains. It stays dry.

A plumber's visit is about $100 just to show up, before anyone opens the cabinet. You spent twenty minutes, a toothbrush and a thin film of grease. You did not pay for a repair. You paid for knowing where to look, and now you know.

Number seven. The push button, apart and back together.

Press the stopper so it sits up. Grip the cap on top and turn it counterclockwise. A wet cap is slippery, and a rubber jar grip from the kitchen drawer helps a great deal. Under the cap is the cartridge. Unscrew it from the crossbar down in the drain, and lift it out.

You will see the white crust on the spring right away.

Fill a bowl with warm tap water and stir in some citric acid until it dissolves. That is the plain white powder on the canning shelf, about $10 to $15 for a pound. Warm, not boiling, because boiling water can warp the plastic of the cartridge. And nothing else goes in that bowl. No vinegar, no bleach, no second cleaner of any kind. One cleaner, on its own.

In Germany, citric acid is a Hausmittel, a house remedy, and it is the usual household habit for scale. It does the slow, patient work that scrubbing cannot.

Lay the cartridge in the bowl and leave it there until the scale goes soft. Then rinse it under the tap, and take the toothbrush to whatever is left on the coils.

Dry the cartridge completely. Put a dab of grease on the spring, worked in so it coats the coils, and a dab on the threads. Screw the cartridge back into the crossbar. Cap on. Press.

The click comes back. That is the spring everyone said was broken, clicking the way it did the day it went in. And it will keep clicking, because the spring now moves inside a film instead of sitting bare in the water.

Number eight. When the part is finished.

Sometimes you lift the stopper out and the stem is cracked right through. Sometimes the ball on the pivot rod is worn flat on one side, so it wobbles no matter how much grease it gets. And sometimes the drain body itself, the metal pipe under the bowl, is so corroded that the nut will not seat on its threads anymore.

Grease will not fix any of those. A new part will.

A new pop-up assembly comes in one bag, with the stopper, the rod and the strap together. It runs about $10 to $25, and it fits the old drain body the same way your old parts came out. You have already made every one of those moves in number six, in the same order. Having someone else fit that same bag can run $100 to $350.

A drain body rusted right through is the one bigger job on this sink. The whole drain comes out from below, together with the P-trap, the U-shaped bend of pipe under the sink. It is a longer afternoon, with a bucket under the trap and the towel still on the cabinet floor. Take it slowly, and do not force a nut that has rusted solid.

Either way, the sink gets a fresh start. And this time you know exactly what is inside it.

Number nine. Wartung.

Wartung means maintenance, the check before the thing breaks. This is the one that keeps all of this from coming back.

Once a year, lift each stopper in the house straight out. Wipe the stem, and look at the bottom of it. For the rod kind, slide the nut back, put a fresh thin film of grease on the ball, and turn the nut hand tight again. About five minutes a sink.

I tie it to one day, the day the heating goes off in the spring. That is the day I go round the house checking what leaks, what drips and what squeaks, and the stoppers are on that list.

Five minutes a year, and the hair never gets time to become a rope. The toothpaste never gets time to become a crust. The stopper never gets the chance to stick, and you never take this sink apart in a hurry again.

One small thing tonight. Go to the bathroom sink, lift the stopper straight out, and look at the stem.

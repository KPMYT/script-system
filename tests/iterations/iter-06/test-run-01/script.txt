Running a dirty filter.

That filter's job is to catch the dust before it gets to the blower, the big fan that pushes warm air through the house, but once it's caked, the furnace is trying to breathe through a wool blanket.
Air can't move across the hot metal fast enough, the furnace overheats, and the limit switch, a safety switch that shuts the burners off when the furnace gets too hot, cuts it out.
That switch is protecting the furnace, so never bypass it.

Let that go long enough and you're paying $75 to $200 for somebody to come out and tell you the filter's dirty.

Number two.
It's cold, the house is quiet, and the furnace isn't making a sound.

The first is the furnace switch.

The second is a tripped breaker in your panel.
A dead-quiet furnace feels like something big has gone wrong, but before you pick up the phone, check both.

Number three feels like thrift, and it works against you, because it's closing the vents in the rooms you don't use.
There's a spare bedroom down the hall nobody's slept in since the holidays, so you shut the vents in there and close the door.
You'd think the heat you're not sending in there goes to the rest of the house and the bill goes down.
But your furnace and its ducts are sized for the whole house.
Close off the supply vents, the ones that blow warm air into the room, and the blower is pushing the same air into less space.
That builds static pressure, the pushback the blower feels from the ducts, a bit like blowing through a coffee stirrer instead of a straw.
The air slows down, the furnace runs hot, and you're right back to the limit switch shutting it off.
Same overheating, and the same visit as number one.
So open them back up, all of them or close to it.
If one room really does run too warm, crack its vent partway instead of shutting it, and leave the rest alone.
The furnace stops fighting its own ducts, and the whole house evens out.

Number four is the other end of the same problem.
Walk around your living room and find the big grille in the wall or the floor with no warm air coming out of it.
It's called a return vent, and it pulls air back to the furnace.
Hold a hand near it while the furnace runs and you'll feel the pull.
A lot of folks figure it's just a vent, so the couch goes right in front of it, or a rug goes over it.
But that grille is the furnace's air supply.
Block it, and you've done the same thing as a dirty filter, only from the other side.
The furnace starves for air, runs hot, and shuts itself down, and that's a visit you'll keep paying for as long as the couch stays put.
So pull the furniture away from it.
Nothing flat against the grille, no rug over a floor return, no curtains hanging down in front.
While you're there, vacuum the grille, because the dust sitting on it is dust headed for your filter.
Can you name where every return in your house is?
If you can't, walk the house tonight and find them all.
Five minutes of moving furniture, and the air moves the way the house was built for.

Those first four are all about air.
Number five is about water, and it only applies to one kind of furnace.
That's the high-efficiency furnace, also called a condensing furnace, the kind with white plastic pipes running out of it.
It pulls so much heat out of its exhaust that the exhaust turns to water, several gallons of it on a cold day.
That water drains out through the condensate line, a small plastic drain line, and on the way it passes through the condensate trap, a plastic loop or box on the furnace that always holds some water.
Picture the furnace trying to start, running a few seconds and quitting, or a puddle on the floor beside it.
Over a season, slime and sludge build up in that trap.
When it clogs, the water backs up, and the pressure switch, a small safety switch that checks the exhaust is flowing before the burners light, shuts the whole furnace down.
You'd never guess a little plastic drain could leave you with no heat, but it can.
A tech will usually charge $100 to $250 to clear it, and more at night or on a weekend.
Here's how you do it yourself.
Turn the furnace off at its switch, and make sure it's really off with a non-contact voltage tester, an inexpensive pen-shaped tester from any hardware store.
Take the cap off the trap, or the screw cap on the drain line, and pour about a cup of white vinegar through it.
Give it half an hour, then flush it with plain water until it runs clear.
Then, before the power goes back on, fill the trap back up with water.
That water is a seal.
If the trap's dry, exhaust gas can push out through the drain and into the house, and the furnace may not light at all.
Furnace makers say to fill it before start-up.
And never pour anything into the little rubber hoses that run to the pressure switch, or into the inducer, the small fan motor that pushes the exhaust out.
Power back on, and the furnace should light and keep running.
Pennies of vinegar against a visit, and I'd make that trade every fall.

That trap is a part nobody's paid to mention.

Number six.
Does your furnace click, light with a whoosh, and then lose its flames a few seconds later?
And then try again, over and over?
That's often the flame sensor.
It's a thin rod of metal sitting in the flame, and its whole job is telling the furnace the gas really lit.
Dust in the air bakes onto that rod over time, and once the coating's thick enough, the furnace can't tell there's a flame, so it shuts the gas off to keep you safe.
Call a tech for it and you can pay $100 to $250 for a flame sensor job.
That's a lot of money to wipe off a rod, because a lot of the time that sensor doesn't need replacing at all.
It needs cleaning.
Turn the power off at the furnace switch and prove it's off with that tester.
Turn the gas valve on the furnace to off.
Take the front panel off.
The sensor sits near the burners, usually held in by one small screw.
Back the screw out, slide the rod out, and rub it gently with a green kitchen scrub pad until it's clean and shiny.
Very fine emery cloth works too, but it's optional.
Skip anything coarse, because scratches give the dust something to grab.
Put it back, panel on, gas valve on, power on, and turn the thermostat up.
The burners should light and stay lit.
If they still light and drop out after a good cleaning, the sensor may be worn out, and that's a cheap part and a short visit.
Ten minutes and a scrub pad, and there's a good chance you've got heat again.

Igniters wear out.

Number eight is the scariest phrase a homeowner can hear in this trade, and it deserves a straight answer.
The tech comes back up from the basement, shaking his head, and tells you the heat exchanger's cracked.
The heat exchanger is the metal chamber inside the furnace that keeps the fire and its exhaust on one side and the air you breathe on the other.
A real crack is serious.
It can let carbon monoxide into your house, and I'd never tell you to shrug it off.
But a few words spoken in your basement aren't proof, and a new furnace runs anywhere from about $3,500 to $9,000, depending on the model.
That's far too much money to hand over on somebody's say-so.
So before you sign anything, ask them to show you where it's cracked.
A photo, a camera inspection of the heat exchanger, something you can look at with your own eyes.
Then get a second opinion from another company before you agree to a new furnace.

And if your carbon monoxide alarm ever goes off, the quote stops mattering.

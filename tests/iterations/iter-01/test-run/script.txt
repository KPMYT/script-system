$21 in big box parts gets you a lazy Susan, and with a family name burned into the top, the personalized ones are listed online from $70 to past $100.

Start with an edge-glued pine round from Home Depot. The tag says 18 inches, it's really seventeen and three-quarters across and a full inch thick, and it runs $14.58.

That's your whole top. Already cut to a circle, which is the lazy part.

Next is the lazy Susan bearing. Get the Everbilt six-inch square one, rated for 400 pounds spread evenly. It's $7.87 by itself, but the six-pack is $31.30, which works out to $5.22 apiece, and if you're planning to sell these you're building more than one anyway.

Then a 12-pack of number six, five-eighths inch flat head wood screws for $1.47. You'll use eight per lazy Susan.

Those three add up to $20.78.

Now, the bearing maker says it should be mounted on a base, and they don't recommend sticking rubber feet on it instead, so you need one small square of wood underneath. A one-by-eight does it. In the five-pack, a premium pine one-by-eight by eight foot works out to about $18.36 a board, and one board gives you a dozen bases, about $1.53 each. Or go dig through the scrap bin, the one I swear I'll use someday, because any offcut of one-by-eight works just as well.

Grab a 16-pack of one-inch felt pads for $4.97. You'll use four.

For the finish, Watco Butcher Block Oil in clear, $18.98 a pint. It's food safe once it's dry, and that matters here, because sooner or later somebody's setting a cheese plate on this thing.

Last, sandpaper in 150, 180 and 220 grit, plus one sheet of 400. A four-pack of Diablo 150 runs $9.99 at Ace. Prices subject to change.

Tools are short. Something to make one crosscut, a drill with a three-quarter inch spade bit, a screwdriver, a clamp and a sanding block. A wood burning pen is optional, and it's for the version that brings the real money.

One cut in this whole build. Mark the one-by-eight at seven and a quarter inches, the same as its width, so the base comes out square.

Clamp it down and make the cut. Let the blade stop completely before you lift the saw, and keep gloves and loose sleeves away from it.

Done. That's the only time the saw comes out.

Now the access hole, the one fussy part. The bearing is two plates that spin against each other, and once the top's on, you can't reach the screws for the top plate. So you drill a hole through the base to get at them.

Set the bearing in the middle of the base, square to the edges. Spin the plates until one screw hole in the top plate isn't covered by the bottom plate, and mark the base through that hole with a pencil. Which, if you're anything like me, starts with hunting for the pencil, because it's never behind my ear when I need it.

Move the bearing aside, clamp the base to the bench, and drill a three-quarter inch hole straight through on your mark. Don't hold a small piece in your hand for this, because a spade bit will grab it and spin it.

Put the bearing back, line it up the same way, and screw the bottom plate to the base with four of your screws.

Spin it by hand. Looking up through the access hole from underneath, you should see a top-plate screw hole come around.

So the base is built and the bearing turns. The top's what sells it, though, and before it goes on, there's a name to put on it.

Sand the round top, bottom and edge, 150 grit first, then 180, then 220. Wear a dust mask for this, because pine dust is fine and it hangs in the air. Vacuum every bit of it off before anything else touches the wood.

Here's where it pays. Plain, this is a nice lazy Susan. With a family name across the top and the year they got married under it, it's a gift, and gifts get bought.

Burn it before any oil goes on, because you don't burn over a finish.

Before you trace anything, find the seams. That round is glued up from strips, and you can see the lines between them. Keep your letters inside one strip so the tip never crosses a glue line.

Print the name out at the size you want, and trace it onto the wood light, so the pencil doesn't smear under the tip.

Pine fights you a little here, and I won't pretend it doesn't. It bleeds resin and it burns uneven, so burn a test word on the underside first and set your heat there. Then go over your lines on top and keep the pen moving. Slowing down to darken a line just leaves ridges, so if the marks come out pale, turn the heat up a touch instead, and if the tip leaves dark dots where a stroke starts, turn it down.

A 30-watt burning kit from Walmart runs $39.22, and its tip gets up to 1,050 degrees, so the pen goes back in its stand between letters, every time, and you work somewhere the smoke can clear. You buy that kit once, so it stays out of the per-piece cost.

Now the oil. Wipe a coat of the butcher block oil onto every face and the edge with a soft rag. Give it six hours, scuff it lightly with the 400, and wipe on a second coat.

Watch your rags with this stuff. It's linseed based, and oily rags wadded up in a pile can catch fire on their own, so drop them in a bucket of water and let them dry out flat, outside, before they go in the trash.

Then leave it alone for 72 hours before anybody puts food on it.

Lay the top face down on a towel. Set the base on it, bearing side down.

Center it by measuring from each corner of the bearing out to the edge of the round. When all four match, you're centered.

Line the access hole up over a top-plate hole and drive a screw through it into the top. Turn the base a quarter turn to the next hole and drive another. Do the same for the last two.

Keep your fingers out from between the plates while you turn it. That's a pinch point, and it'll get you.

Stick a felt pad on each corner of the base so it won't scratch anybody's table.

Flip it over and give it a spin. Then pick it up by the top. If the base hangs there solid, all four screws are holding. If anything shifts, tighten it now, because a top that lets go while somebody's carrying it to the table is a real mess.

Which brings me to whether anybody actually pays for this.

On Etsy, an 18-inch family name round is listed at $70. An engraved one with a last name, a monogram and a date is listed at $72.95. A personalized 20-inch is listed at $99.90, and a wine barrel ring version is listed at $118. Those are asking prices, not sales.

I'd put the personalized one at around $90. That's in the middle of the pack, nowhere near the top. Plain, no name, I'd ask around $50.

And on a big box shelf, a finished 14-inch lazy Susan runs $54.80. Smaller than yours. That's big business for you, selling you the finished thing at a markup and hoping you never walk over to the hardware aisle. So do the damn math.

Your three parts are $20.78. Add $1.53 for the base, $1.24 in felt pads, about $3.16 of oil and about $1.25 of sandpaper, and you're at about $28 all in. The oil and sandpaper are my estimates, since the oil maker doesn't give a coverage number for pine.

At a craft fair, at $90, that leaves about $62 a piece before your booth fee. Plain at $50, it's about $22, and that gap is the whole case for burning a name in.

On Etsy at that same price, figure about $9 in fees between the 20-cent listing fee, the 6.5% transaction fee and the 3% plus 25 cents for payment processing. That puts you around $53 before shipping, and if one of Etsy's offsite ads brings in the sale, they can take another 15%.

The bearings come six to a pack, so build six. If all six sell personalized at a fair, that's about $372 left before the booth. And that's only if they all sell.

And the burning kit? If the first one sells, the $39.22 is covered, with change left over.

Time is my estimate, worked out from the steps. About half an hour of hands-on work for a plain one, another 30 to 45 minutes to burn a name, and then 72 hours of waiting on the oil, which is the laziest part of all.

When you list it, lead with the name. Wedding gift, housewarming, anniversary, with an "established" year under the family name. And tuck in a card that says hand wash only, no dishwasher, because that's what the oil maker says.

As for where, a craft fair is the best fit. No platform fee, they stack flat on the table, and I think a name sells best when people can pick it up and see it. Facebook Marketplace charges no selling fee on local pickup. Etsy works too, but a round this size doesn't go in a small box, so price your shipping before you list it.

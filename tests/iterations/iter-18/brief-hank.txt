TITLE: I Turned a Bag of Sawdust Into $300 of Christmas Fire Starters
CHANNEL: Hank Hardwood
BRIEF (talking points):
- The product: fire starters for wood stoves, fireplaces and fire pits. Each one is a single cup cut from a paper egg carton, packed with shop sawdust and soaked with melted paraffin wax, with a small pine cone pressed into the top. Sold six to a small brown paper gift bag with a handwritten tag, as a Christmas gift.
- The angle: the main ingredient is the sawdust already in the viewer's shop vac or dust collector. No lumber to buy, nothing to cut, nothing that spins, no board wasted on something nobody buys. The bag and tag (well under a dollar) are what turn it from a bag of pucks into a gift.
- The batch: 15 egg cartons of 12 cups = 180 starters = 30 packs of six. About a tablespoon of melted wax per cup; one pound of wax does about 30 cups (check), so about six pounds for the batch (check).
- What you need:
  - Sawdust from untreated solid wood only (pine, oak, poplar, whatever's been cut). Never pressure-treated lumber, plywood, MDF, or anything painted or finished: glues and chemicals that don't belong in a wood stove. If treated boards went through the vac recently, empty it and start fresh.
  - Household paraffin wax blocks (the kind sold in the canning aisle), about $4 a one-pound block at a big-box store (check); six blocks, call it about $25 (check). Old unscented candle stubs work too and are free; fish the wicks out as they melt. Hank's opinion: buy the blocks (no wicks, no smell).
  - Paper egg cartons, free: save your own, ask neighbors, ask the church kitchen. Paper only, never foam.
  - Small brown kraft gift bags with paper handles, about $24 for 50 (check), and paper tags with string, about $6 for 100 (check): together well under a dollar a pack.
  - Optional: a handful of small, dry pine cones from the yard, free; they make the pack read as a gift rather than a craft experiment. Cinnamon sticks do the same with a smell but cost money.
  - Prices subject to change.
- Tools: an old pot and a clean coffee can or large soup can standing in it (a homemade double boiler); the pot never cooks food again (thrift-store pot or the one with the wobbly handle). An electric burner; on a gas stove, a cheap plug-in hot plate instead. Pliers to grip the hot can, an old spoon, a serrated bread knife, gloves and safety glasses. A kitchen thermometer is optional. No saw, no drill; no cut list on this build: the recipe stands in for it.
- The steps:
  - Sort the sawdust: tip the vac out onto a scrap board or sheet and pick out screws, staples, chunks, knots and anything metal. Wear a mask: the fine dust goes in the air. Keep dust and small shavings, nothing bigger than a fingernail.
  - Cut the lid and front flap off every carton, leaving a tray of 12 cups. Set the trays on foil or a flattened cardboard box: melted wax runs, and it doesn't come off a bench.
  - Fill each cup with sawdust, press it down with a thumb, add more, press again. Leave a little room at the top: a cup packed to the brim leaves the wax sitting on top instead of soaking through. All 15 trays take about half an hour (check).
  - Set up the double boiler: a few inches of water in the pot, the can standing in the water, on the electric burner turned low. Bring the water to steaming, then add the wax: break a block into the can and let it melt slowly, stirring now and then with the old spoon. Paraffin melts well below boiling, so a rolling boil means it's too hot.
  - Pour: grip the can with pliers, gloves on, and pour about a tablespoon slowly over each cup. Let it soak down before moving on; if it sits on top, wait a minute and add a little more. Done right, wax reaches the bottom of the cup with sawdust just showing on top. The first tray is slow; later trays take about 6 minutes each (check).
  - Finish (the dressing up): while the wax is soft, press one small pine cone into the top of each cup; it burns along with the rest.
  - Leave the trays flat overnight to harden.
  - Next morning, cut each tray into 12 single cups with the bread knife.
  - Test one before bagging any: outdoors in a fire pit, under a little kindling, light the paper edge, not the top. It should burn about 5 to 10 minutes on its own (check), long enough to catch kindling or start a wood stove. If it won't hold a flame, it didn't get enough wax: add another tablespoon to the rest of that tray.
  - Pack: six to a bag, fold the top over twice, tie the tag through the fold. Handwrite the tag ("Fire starters. Light the paper edge. One per fire."). Handwriting reads as handmade, which is why anyone pays more than the big-box price.
- Safety (the dangerous part is the hot wax):
  - Melt wax only in the double boiler on low heat: never in a pan straight on a burner, and never over a gas flame or any open flame.
  - Overheated wax can catch fire. Gloves and glasses on, a pot lid within reach the whole time, and don't walk away from melting wax (sensible; check).
  - If wax catches fire: lid on it, burner off (check). Never water: water on burning wax makes it spatter and spread the fire.
  - Sawdust only from untreated, unfinished solid wood (see above). Paper cartons only.
  - Test burn outdoors, in a fire pit.
- The money:
  - On Etsy, handmade packs of five or six wax fire starters are listed at about $10 to $25 (check). The closest to this build, six sawdust-and-wax cubes, is listed at about $10 (check). No shop names.
  - Hank's price (his opinion): about $10 a six-pack at a market table, $12 online; not the top of the range, which belongs to sellers with a photo setup and a stack of reviews.
  - Cost: wax, bag and tag come to about $1.50 a pack (check); the sawdust, cartons and pine cones are free. At $10, about $8.50 a pack is left at a market, before any booth fee (whatever the show charges).
  - Batch: 30 packs, if all 30 sell, is $300; less about $45 for wax, bags and tags (check), about $255 (time: an afternoon plus a night for the wax to harden, an estimate). If they don't all sell, they're Christmas presents.
  - Online: Etsy keeps a bit over a tenth of a $12 sale, and shipping comes on top, so sell hand to hand first and list online once the local supply is gone.
- The honest comparison (the adversary: big business's markup): a big-box store lists a bag of 72 starter cubes for about $10 (check): fair enough, no quarrel. But a boutique brand lists four discs of cedar sawdust and wax, the same two ingredients, for about $13 (check): over $3 a disc, mostly paying for the box. The six-pack sits between the two and has what neither has: a person's handwriting on the tag. Point: buyers at a December market are looking for a gift for someone hard to buy for, not for fire starters.
- Where to sell: a holiday market or church bazaar in November and December first ($10 is impulse money there); a few on the table at work; Facebook Marketplace for local pickup (no fee); online second, since a $12 pack has to pay for its own box and postage. Come summer, the same puck with a different tag sells to campers as a campfire starter.
- Advice: make one carton first (12 starters, two bags) and see whether the town buys them before buying six pounds of wax; then make the other 14.

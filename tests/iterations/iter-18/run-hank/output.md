Start with the sawdust, and be fussy about where it came from. It has to be from untreated solid wood, like pine, oak or poplar. Never pressure-treated lumber, and never plywood or MDF either. Nothing painted or finished goes in. Those carry glues and chemicals that have no business inside somebody's wood stove. If treated boards went through the vac not long ago, empty it out and start a fresh load. That part's free.

Down the canning aisle at the big-box store you'll find household paraffin wax, sold in one-pound blocks for about $4 apiece. This batch takes six of them, so call it about $25. Old unscented candle stubs work too, and they cost nothing, but you'll be fishing the wicks out as they melt. I'd buy the blocks. No wicks to chase, no smell to deal with.

Then egg cartons, the paper kind, and they're free. Save your own, ask the neighbors, ask whoever runs the church kitchen. Paper only, never foam, since foam is plastic and plastic doesn't belong in a fire.

For the part that sells it, pick up small brown paper gift bags with paper handles, about $24 for a pack of 50. Add paper tags with string, about $6 for a hundred. Split across the batch, that's well under a dollar a pack.

Last on the list, and optional, is a handful of small dry pine cones from the yard. They're free, and they make the bag read as a gift instead of a craft experiment. Cinnamon sticks do the same job with a nice smell, but those cost money. Prices subject to change, of course.

Tools now, and you probably own most of them. You need an old pot and a clean coffee can or a big soup can that stands up inside it. That's a homemade double boiler, which just means the wax sits in the can and the hot water around it does the heating. The wax never touches the burner. That pot never cooks food again, so use the one with the wobbly handle, or pick one up at the thrift store.

Run it on an electric burner. If your kitchen has a gas stove, get a cheap plug-in hot plate instead, because wax and an open flame don't mix. Add a pair of pliers to grip the hot can, an old spoon, and a serrated bread knife. Gloves and safety glasses round it out. A kitchen thermometer is nice to have, but you can do without one. There's no saw and no drill on this one, and no cut list either. The recipe does that job.

The first real step is sorting the sawdust, and it's the dull one. Tip the vac out onto a scrap board or an old sheet. Keep the dust and the small shavings, and nothing bigger than your fingernail goes in.

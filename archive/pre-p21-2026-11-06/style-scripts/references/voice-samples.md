# Voice samples: style

**PROVISIONAL until the operator's listen.** The operator will listen and choose (HANDOFF 2026-10-28/29): Tracy Campoli or Melissa Murrell for Scarlett Starling, Marie-Anne Lecoeur for Diane de Chambray. Until then these four stand. They were picked from the transcripts for what they teach on the page; nobody has listened to them yet for accent, age or pace against the channels' voices.

The voice anchor for every style script (core `voice-anchoring.md`). Four stretches of real women's narration, each from a winning video, copied verbatim from the collected transcripts in `studies/style/data/` and checked by script: every stretch appears word for word in its transcript, starting within 3 seconds of the range given. Nothing is cleaned: caption slips, missing capitals, the fillers ("um", "like", "you know") and, in sample 4, the missing punctuation are kept as the captions have them.

**Who they come from:** real women on camera only (operator, 2026-10-28: voice samples stay with real women; a man's voice doesn't carry to a woman persona). Never Justin Hickox (M06, M08, M10, M12), never a faceless or AI-voiced video (the F files, structure only). All four teach an item; none is a try-on, a client story or a sponsor read.

**Test every sentence of a draft against these.** The copy guard applies: never 8 or more words in a row from this file, and the same guard covers `excerpts.md`, `SKILL.md` and every model file. Borrow how the sentences are built, never what they say: every fact, swap, brand and number comes from the brief.

**What all four share** (the shapes to anchor to):
- **The point first, then the reason,** in plain words a friend would use at the mirror.
- **"But" turns everywhere,** joining two sentences where the thought turns (about 6 to 8 per 1,000 words in the models; ours ran about 1).
- **"You" for the fix,** and the reason said about her body or her closet, not about women in general.
- **Permission inside the item,** not as a slogan: what she may keep, then the fix.
- **A feeling voiced in one plain sentence,** or a question to her, then straight back to the clothes or the hair.
- **Run-on, spoken sentences,** with the odd self-correction; never a polished pair of one-liners.

**What none of them gives us** (left behind every time): their own body, age, height, size, hair or face as proof; their city, family, celebrities they admire and their past; pointing at what they wear; links and channel asks. The persona has none of these (SKILL "Proof").

**Pace:** the channels' voices run at 169 words a minute (Scarlett) and 172 (Diane). Samples 1, 2 and 4 speak at about 163 to 172; Marie-Anne Lecoeur (sample 3) speaks at about 121, with long pauses. Take her sentence shapes, not her pace.

## Sample 1: Tracy Campoli, the outfit with no "conversation"

**Source:** M15 · Tracy Campoli · video m7pfd4OxPzs, "7 Items That Make You Look FRUMPY After 50 (Stop Wearing These!)" · [9:31]-[11:01] (90 seconds, 258 words, about 172 a minute) · 3.55x normal · fashion

**Suits: Scarlett.** Once the fillers go, this is the polished American register: a settled idea (balance), both extremes laid side by side, then a decisive "here's what we do instead" and a rule she can use on any outfit. Too chatty for Diane's brisk beats.

This one, bear with me here, but this is really something subtle that I don't think people talk about enough and it's like when you don't have a conversation in your outfit. So let me explain. If everything is big, an oversized top, you know, oversized kind of flowy bottoms and shoes that aren't intentional, you kind of just get lost in the fabric and there isn't any intention and any cohesiveness in your outfit. And on the flip side, being a girl that lives in Miami, we see this one a lot. When everything is small, tight, like just showing all the bits, it just looks a little try-hard, and it does not read as chic. So, instead, what we want to do is create balance, cohesion, a conversation with your clothes. It's actually really easy. So, if you're wearing like large oversized bottoms, which can look incredibly chic, you want to wear something that's a little bit more fitted on the top, or at least cinch in your waist. I think Victoria Beckham like nails this, and she is like a style icon for me. That woman just looks incredible in like really chic oversized pieces, but you still will see conversation. She'll cinch in the waist, right? Or she will leave the jacket that Maybe she has an oversized jacket, oversized pants, but she will have a shirt tucked in, so you can still see a little bit of figure that oversized. There's got to be sort of like a push and a pull, right?

**What to borrow:**
- **The setup that asks for patience,** then explains: a short "let me explain" before a subtle point, instead of a big claim.
- **Both extremes, side by side,** each with its reason in plain words (lost in the fabric; trying too hard), then the fix on "So, instead".
- **"You" for the fix,** as an instruction with a condition: if the bottoms are big, the top is fitted or the waist is cinched.
- **"Right?" tag questions** that keep her with you, two or three a stretch, not every sentence.
- **The rule she keeps** at the end, in everyday words (a push and a pull), not a slogan.
- **Long, run-on sentences** joined by "and" and "so", with the "but" turns where the thought changes.

**What not to borrow:**
- **Her life and city:** "being a girl that lives in Miami". The persona has no city.
- **A celebrity as her icon:** Victoria Beckham "is like a style icon for me". A name comes only from the brief, and never as the narrator's own taste story.
- **"Does not read as chic":** "reads as" is our old house vocabulary and banned (SKILL "Banned").
- **Other women as the villain:** "it just looks a little try-hard", "showing all the bits". The adjective goes on the outfit, never on women.
- **The fillers at her density:** "like", "kind of", "you know", "sort of". Keep the run-on rhythm; drop most of the fillers.
- **The self-correction that loses the thread** ("leave the jacket that Maybe she has"): real speech, but a voiceover reads it as an error.

## Sample 2: Melissa Murrell, keeping the haircut current

**Source:** M20 · MM Personal Styling · video gfbWOw00Y-M, "10 Style Mistakes That Instantly Make You Look Older with Personal Stylist Melissa Murrell" · [28:46]-[30:05] (79 seconds, 214 words, about 163 a minute) · 28.54x normal · hair

**Suits: Scarlett,** with a caution. The register is calm and settled: a verdict, permission folded into it, the turn back on "But", and the why last. She is a British stylist, so her words need making American (see below); the operator's listen decides between her and Tracy Campoli.

And finally, it is keeping updated with your hairstyle. There is nothing more aging on a woman over sort of 50 years old whereby you don't keep up with not necessarily the trends, but just having that regular haircut. It's not necessarily about dying your hair. I'm not talking about the grays, although I obviously that does bother some people, but it doesn't bother others. That's absolutely fine. But just a more modern haircut. Um, gone of the days where we have set blow dries that are perfect in appearance. We want them now flicked. We want them to move. We don't want them hair lacquered in a permanent state of not moving. Um, you know, I'm I'm looking at my own hair now in this camera and I'm thinking, "Oh my goodness, it's now pitch black and I started this video at like 7:00 this morning and it's now 5 at night. So, my hair is definitely not looking the best. So, I'm hoping this isn't coming across as a lecture around hair. But just being aware of modern hairstyles, I think, is worth noting. As we get older, our shape of our face changes, and often it means a haircut is perhaps more regularly needed than it was when we were younger.

**What to borrow:**
- **The point in the first breath,** then what it isn't ("not necessarily the trends, but just having that regular haircut").
- **Permission inside the item:** what she is not being told to do (the grays), said plainly, "that's absolutely fine", then "But" back to the point.
- **A short run that counts real things:** the old set against hair that moves, in three short sentences. A list of real things, not a drumbeat.
- **The softener that heads off pushback:** she checks she isn't lecturing. This audience pushes back on rules, so one line like it, in fresh words, earns trust.
- **The why last, in one plain line:** the face changes, so the cut needs refreshing more often.

**What not to borrow:**
- **Her own hair and her long day:** "I'm looking at my own hair now in this camera". Her own head is never proof (SKILL "Proof").
- **The age as a label:** "a woman over sort of 50 years old". An age only where the title says it.
- **"Nothing more aging":** a superlative the brief can't back. Say it ages, and why.
- **British words:** "blow dries", "hair lacquered", "flicked". Ours is American: a set, hairspray, hair with movement.
- **"dying your hair":** a caption slip for "dyeing". The script spells it for the voice.
- **"Um" and the doubled "I'm I'm":** real, but not for a voiceover.

## Sample 3: Marie-Anne Lecoeur, comfort without the clumpy shoe

**Source:** M21 · Marie-Anne Lecoeur - The French Chic Expert · video ixlexruRC6c, "7 Things That Scream 'Old Lady' (Harsh But True): Style Over 50" · [3:44]-[5:07] (83 seconds, 168 words, about 121 a minute) · 12.36x normal · fashion

**Suits: Diane.** Brisk, frank and warm about comfort: short beats, a concession then a hard "But", a one-line inside view from France tied to a practical point, and a pro tip as the result. This is the French lens as a habit she has seen, not a claim of superiority. For the listen, a second Marie-Anne stretch with more teaching and less lens is M23 (video G-RW7ih19Y4, "How To Look Slimmer"), [3:40]-[5:16], on color blocks against one color head to toe.

I told you I was going to be harsh. Not on you, but your clothes. Cruel to be kind and all that. Sometimes you have to shock people in order for them to change and to rebuild. The next thing that screams old lady is clumpy shoes. Comfort is non-negotiable. I agree with you. You don't have to suffer to be stylish. But those clunky, sensible shoes, no mercy. Here in France, we women even in our 70s and over, wear lovely shoes. They may be flat or with a small heel, but clumpy, they are not. So, what can you do instead? Here is the truth that many women don't want to hear. You can have comfort and style. Look for elegant flats, smart sneakers, or low heeail boots. Nothing too wide or clumpy looking. Say no to gladiator sandals or hobbit feet styles. And remember ladies, no Crocs, no stocks. My pro tip is you can have comfort and style and look youthful by doing so.

**What to borrow:**
- **Short beats,** most under ten words, each one a point; this is Diane's "short beats and one-line verdicts".
- **The concession, then "But":** she agrees comfort comes first, then turns hard on the shoe. Permission and the verdict in four short sentences.
- **The harsh flag aimed at the clothes:** harsh on the clothes, not on her, said once.
- **The inside view tied to a practical point:** what women in France wear, used only to show the swap (flat or a small heel, never clumpy).
- **Frankness announced:** "the truth that many women don't want to hear", then the swap as a short list of real shoes.
- **The result as one closing line** that pays the promise (comfort and style together).

**What not to borrow:**
- **The unhedged national claim and her own age group:** "Here in France, we women even in our 70s and over". Diane hedges ("many women here") and uses her one heritage line at most once (profile).
- **The jibes at shoes women wear for sore feet:** "gladiator sandals or hobbit feet styles". Commenters with wide or painful feet pushed back; give the steady, good-looking swap without the mockery.
- **The brand jab as a rhyme:** "no Crocs, no stocks". An opinion against a brand is allowed only as hers and in plain words, where the brief allows it.
- **Shock as a method:** "Sometimes you have to shock people". Ours is frank, never shaming.
- **The hard word as a refrain:** "screams old lady". At most once a script, flagged (SKILL "Emotion" rule 10).
- **Her turn question:** "So, what can you do instead?" is hers; the scanner allows it once a script at most, and better not at all.
- **Caption slips and "look youthful":** "low heeail boots" is a slip; say the look plainly ("lighter", "more current").

## Sample 4: Diana GOSS, the color next to the face

**Source:** M27 · Diana GOSS · video NMvN5bjcwks, "How To Look Instantly Younger | 5 Colour Hacks You Need To See" · [1:50]-[3:06] (76 seconds, 214 words, about 169 a minute) · 23.56x normal · fashion. Her captions run without punctuation, so the stretch reads as one long line.

**Suits: Diane** as a second anchor (brisk, frank verdicts from a European stylist, a rule stated flat), and either channel for the mechanism lines. She speaks English as a second language: borrow the order of the moves, not the grammar.

the thing is it doesn't really matter which colors you wear on the bottom but the colors you wear in the portrait zone are really essential and if you still believe that color analysis is some old-fashioned Theory and it doesn't work just try at least once to take your picture standing with your face turned to the shadow good colors work as a reflector they highlight our face making us look Bolder fresher younger they really emphasize our features and they conceal all the imperfections whereas bad colors work in the opposite way they project Shadow to our face making us look dull more tired they emphasize all the imperfections and eventually make us look older the main criteria for choosing colors is the temperature if you're cool type like me and you surround your face with warm Hues such as this color combo of warm nude and camel colors just clash with your natural coloring hair color skin undertone eye color as a result your skin start looking darker iron hair color become more muted and overall impression is quite unflattering naturally the older you get the more obvious it becomes you can also notice that with the wrong colors next to your face even slightest wrinkles and pigmentation become more visible

**What to borrow:**
- **The point that narrows the job:** the bottom half hardly matters; the color by the face does. One "but" sets it up.
- **The check as a dare to the doubter:** if she doesn't believe it, one photo will show her.
- **The mechanism as a pair said plainly:** good colors reflect light onto the face, bad ones throw a shadow. A real cause and its opposite, not a poster line.
- **The rule stated flat:** what matters most is temperature (warm or cool), then the term explained by the parts it covers (hair, skin undertone, eyes).
- **The result in look words:** brighter, duller, more tired, more muted.

**What not to borrow:**
- **Her own coloring:** "if you're cool type like me". The persona's face is never the example.
- **Pointing at what she wears:** "such as this color combo". Name the colors instead.
- **Skin and flaws:** "conceal all the imperfections", "wrinkles and pigmentation become more visible". Skin is the makeup and skincare pack's ground, and the line puts the harm on her face; ours keeps the harm on the color.
- **Age as decline:** "naturally the older you get the more obvious it becomes". Say aging only where the title does, and kindly.
- **Second-language grammar:** "the main criteria", "your skin start looking", "iron hair color" (a caption slip for "your hair color").
- **"Younger" stacked in a list:** "Bolder fresher younger". One title word, said where it does a job, is enough.

# HVAC price guide

Typical US prices for the jobs, parts, replacement quotes and shelf items these channels talk about, plus the safety facts. Researched 2026-10-27 by four researchers (two or more independent publishers or retailers per figure), then re-checked job by job by four other researchers against publishers the first ones didn't use. The full research, every source and every verdict are in `studies/hvac/price-research-2026-10/`. Re-check the guide once a year: prices drift, and refrigerant rules change.

## How to use it

- **The brief comes first.** Use this guide only for a price the brief doesn't give. On safety, the brief wins.
- **Always an estimate.** Say a rounded range or "about", never an exact price and never cents ("about $150", not "$149.99"). The figures here are already rounded. The one exception is the cost-per-use device (`SKILL.md`, "Money framing").
- **Say what moves the price, or the top of it:** "anywhere from $150 to $400, depending on where you live and the time of day", "on a big house this can pass $15,000". Every figure here is a national typical range; local prices vary, and nights and weekends cost more.
- **At most one price per item.** Most items carry their cost in words.
- **"In words" means don't give a figure.** The sources disagree or there's only one, so the script says it in words ("a few hundred dollars", "get a quote"), as the row suggests.
- **Part, or part plus the visit.** The "DIY / part" column is what the part costs on the shelf or online. The "With a tech" column is the whole bill: the visit, the labor and the part.
- **Store brand or name brand.** Shelf prices are for the store brand unless a brand is named. If the script names a brand, use that brand's price.
- **Don't name the source** unless the row says "can cite". Many figures came from cost-guide pages that couldn't be read directly, so they're fine as estimates but not as quotes.
- **Check** column: **confirmed** (the sources agree and the second researcher agreed), **adjusted** (the second researcher moved it; the range shown is theirs), **thin** (usable, but light support, or the second researcher didn't re-check it), **in words** (no figure).

## 9. Facts and safety (not prices)

| Fact | Say | Check |
|---|---|---|
| A carbon monoxide alarm sounds | 1) never ignore it, and don't go looking for the source; 2) get everyone out into fresh air right away, outside; 3) count heads, make sure every person is out; 4) from outside, call 911 or the fire department; 5) stay out until the responders say it's safe to go back in; 6) if they find the furnace or another appliance is the cause, don't run it again until a trained tech has fixed it; 7) if anyone has a headache, feels dizzy or sick, get them medical help. The real alarm is loud: four quick beeps, a pause, over and over. A single chirp every so often is the battery or the alarm wearing out (CPSC, NFPA; Indiana Homeland Security for the four beeps): can cite. Leave out the old "silence it and open the windows if nobody feels sick" | confirmed |
| Carbon monoxide alarms: where | one on every level of the house, and one outside each bedroom area; linked alarms are best (CPSC, NFPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Carbon monoxide alarms: how long they last | in words: "most last about 5 to 10 years, depending on the model; the date's on the back". Near the end it chirps and a new battery won't stop it: replace the whole alarm, "according to the maker's instructions" (NFPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Carbon monoxide deaths | more than 400 Americans die each year from accidental carbon monoxide poisoning not linked to fires; more than 100,000 go to the emergency room and more than 14,000 are hospitalized (CDC): can cite | confirmed |
| Carbon monoxide symptoms | headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion; like the flu but without a fever (CDC): can cite. Warning signs, in words: more than one person sick at once, the pets seem ill too, you feel better away from home, it starts when the furnace kicks on | confirmed |
| A gas leak | get everyone out now, on foot, and move well away; don't flip light switches, don't use your phone or the garage door opener, don't light anything, and don't start the car; once you're well away, call 911 and the gas company, and don't assume someone else did; don't go back in until they tell you it's safe, and don't try to find the leak yourself. Some people can't smell the gas, and the smell can fade. Leave out "open the windows" (a utility says not to) and "shut it off at the meter yourself" | adjusted |
| Generator: how far | outdoors only, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, exhaust pointed away (CPSC, CDC): can cite | confirmed |
| Generator or car in a garage | never run a generator in the garage, and never run the car in an attached garage, not even with the big door open (CDC, NFPA, EPA): can cite. There's no official distance for a car: say it in words | confirmed |
| Never backfeed the panel | never plug a generator into a wall outlet to power the house: it can electrocute line workers and neighbors on the same transformer. A transfer switch fitted by a licensed electrician (CPSC): can cite. "Interlock" in words | confirmed |
| Space heaters: the fire share | space heaters and heating stoves cause about 3 in 10 home heating fires, but more than 7 in 10 of the deaths (NFPA, 2020-2024): can cite. Never "9 out of 10" or "88%": that's the older 2016-2020 figure | confirmed |
| Space heaters: the rules | at least three feet from anything that can burn; plugged straight into a wall outlet, never an extension cord or power strip (CPSC, USFA, NFPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Filters: how often | check it every month, change it when it looks dirty, and at least every three months (ENERGY STAR): can cite. With pets, about every month or two: in words | confirmed |
| Filters: the EPA's MERV advice | if you upgrade, go to at least MERV 13, or as high as your system's fan and filter slot can handle; a tech can tell you (EPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Thermostat setback | turning it back 7 to 10 degrees for 8 hours a day can save up to 10% a year on heating and cooling (Department of Energy): can cite. Or: roughly 1% off the heating bill for every degree, for an eight-hour setback (the Department of Energy's heating and cooling guide): can cite as "heating bill" only. With a heat pump, no deep setbacks unless the thermostat is made for heat pumps | confirmed |
| Ceiling fans in winter | clockwise on low: it pushes the warm air at the ceiling back down; off when you leave the room (Department of Energy). "Set the thermostat a degree or two lower" in words | thin |
| R-22 | production and import ended in the US on January 1, 2020; an old R-22 system can still be repaired with recycled or stockpiled R-22, and the EPA doesn't make you replace it (EPA): can cite. "Scarce and expensive" in words | confirmed |
| R-410A | new central air and heat pumps are now built for newer refrigerants, R-454B or R-32 (since January 1, 2025, EPA): can cite. If you have R-410A, you're fine: you can keep running it and repairing it. Never say whether leftover R-410A systems can still be installed: federal rules allow it now, but some states don't | confirmed |
| The newer refrigerants | R-454B and R-32: about a quarter to a third of R-410A's climate impact (EPA): can cite. They're mildly flammable, so they only go in equipment built for them | confirmed |
| Refrigerant is a licensed job | only EPA-certified (Section 608) technicians can buy refrigerant for home AC and heat pumps (EPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Condensing furnace water | in words: "on a cold day a high-efficiency furnace can make several gallons of water, up to about 8 to 10 when it runs hard, and it's slightly acidic" | confirmed |
| Why the condensate trap holds water | the water in the trap is a seal: if it's dry, exhaust gas can push out through the drain into the house, and the furnace may not light. Furnace makers say to fill the trap before start-up (Carrier, Goodman): can cite as "furnace makers say" | confirmed |
| Lifespans | in words: a gas furnace often 15 to 20 years or more; a central air conditioner about 12 to 15; a heat pump about 10 to 15, because it works all year. ENERGY STAR says to have it looked at once an AC or heat pump passes 10 years, a furnace 15: can cite | adjusted |
| Watts a gas furnace needs | in words: "roughly 400 to 800 watts to run, mostly the blower, with a short start-up surge that can reach 1,400 or more on an older motor; check the blower's label" | thin |
| Capacitors hold a charge | a capacitor can stay charged after the power is off, enough to hurt you; techs discharge it and test it before touching it (the OSHA work rule): can cite | confirmed |
| Air sealing | sealing air leaks and insulating can save about 15% on heating and cooling, about 11% of the total energy bill (ENERGY STAR): can cite. With an older furnace or water heater that vents up a chimney, get a combustion (draft) test after sealing, because tight houses can pull exhaust back in (ENERGY STAR): can cite | confirmed |
| Indoor humidity | below 60%, ideally 30 to 50% (EPA): can cite | confirmed |
| Federal tax credits | the federal tax credits for new heat pumps, furnaces and central air are gone: they only covered systems installed by the end of 2025 (IRS): can cite. Point to state or utility rebates instead, without a figure | confirmed |

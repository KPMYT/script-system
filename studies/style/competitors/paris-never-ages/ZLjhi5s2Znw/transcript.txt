There are eight colors hanging in your wardrobe right now that quietly take the light out of your face. And a Parisian woman over 60 never wears a single one of them on its own. She pairs each one with a second color that gives the light back. No logos, no new wardrobe, just two colors, wellchosen, close to the face. Try it once in front of your own mirror and you will see the difference at once. Bonjour, my dear friends. I'm Helenom. I've spent 30 years with the clothes of a Paris couture house. And if those years have taught me one thing, it is this. When a lovely woman looks tired in her clothes, it is almost never her. It is the color sitting next to her face not doing its job. So today, eight color pairs. For each one, I'll tell you which color in your wardrobe has stopped helping you, what to wear with it or in its place, and a little test you can do tonight by a window in 5 minutes. You very likely own half of these pieces already. Let's begin with the color most of us reach for without even thinking. Black. The black turtleneck. The black sweater. The black knit top worn right up under the chin. For years, it was your best friend. It made you look slim, serious, chic, ready for anything. And then one morning you caught yourself in the mirror and thought, why do I look so tired today? Here is what happened. And it is very simple. After 60, our faces lose a little of their natural contrast. The eyes, the brows, and the lips stand out a little less against the skin. Black is the darkest color there is. And right under the chin, it sends no light back up to your face. So every shadow looks deeper. Every fine line, every shadow under the eye, black underlines it like a pencil. It isn't you, Sheree. It's the black. The swap is navy and white. Navy is a deep dark blue. Dark enough to slim you almost as well as black does, but much softer next to the skin. and white near the face works like the little reflector a photographer holds up. It sends light back onto your skin. So, a navy crew neck sweater with the collar of a white cotton shirt showing at the neck. A navy blazer over a white t-shirt. On a Saturday, a navy cardigan, a white shirt, and your jeans. Nothing to buy very likely, only a few things to move around. In March 1858, the French Navy made a blue and white striped knit part of its sailor's uniform. The rule was precise. 21 white stripes on the body, each one twice as wide as the blue stripes between them, and no buttons at all, so a sailor could move freely among the ropes. You may have heard that the 21 stripes stand for Napoleon's 21 victories. A charming story and only a legend. Nobody truly knows why the Navy chose stripes, but I find it rather comforting. Navy and white have been doing hard work in wind and salt water for more than 150 years. They can certainly do a little work for you at lunch. Now, your test tomorrow morning, stand by a window in daylight, not under the bathroom lamp, which lies to all of us. Hold something black under your chin. Look at the shadows under your eyes. Then hold something navy. Most women see it in a second. With navy, the face comes forward and the shadows step back. And if you own a striped top in navy and white, wear it with joy. It's the same pair, simply in stripes. Of course, when black becomes too hard, many of us run to the very opposite. And that brings me to our second color. Beige. Beige trousers. a beige sweater, a beige coat, a beige bag, beige shoes, all in the same soft tone, and very often the very same tone as your skin. It feels gentle and safe and elegant, and it can be. But when everything from your collar to your toes is the color of your skin, something curious happens. You begin to fade. The eye finds no edge anywhere. No beginning, no end. The outfit isn't wrong. It simply has no full stop. I see this in the fitting room almost every week. A woman comes in dressed head to toe in oatmeal, beautiful fabrics, a lovely cashmere, and she asks me why she looks washed out. I don't take one thing away from her. I add a single line of black and she straightens up in front of the mirror as if somebody had switched on a light. That is the swap. Beige with one clear touch of black. A thin black leather belt on a beige dress, a fine black knit under a camel coat, black ballet flats or a black bag with beige trousers. One touch, two at the very most. And notice this black is not a big block under your face. Here, black is the punctuation. So look at your beige and ask, where is its full stop? Find one place for it. The belt, the shoes, the bag, even the frames of your glasses. Here's the check. Put on your all beige outfit in front of the long mirror and look at yourself for 3 seconds. Then add a black belt and look again. Your waist comes back. Your whole figure has a shape again. Beige has a cousin that plays the very same disappearing trick and it's probably folded in your drawer right now. Before we go on, one little thing close to my heart. Gray is a beautiful color, calm, clever, modern. But a flat medium gray worn on its own near the face, especially in a matte wool has one terrible habit. It lends its grayness to your skin. And if your hair has gone a lovely silver, gray on gray on gray turns everything into one single tone, like a photograph that has lost its color. The swap is soft gray with blush pink at the face. Blush. I mean, the pink of a cheek after a walk in the cold. a dusty muted pink with a little gray or beige in it. Not candy, not bubblegum. Blush brings warmth back to the cheeks, and gray keeps the pink grown up so it never looks too sweet. Together, they're tender and quietly strong. How to wear it? A blush silk blouse under a soft gray cardigan or a gray blazer. A pale pink cotton shirt under a gray crew neck, just the collar showing. a blush scarf knotted at the neck of a gray coat. With it, pearls, silver, or a little rose gold. That's gold with a soft pink tint. I'll make a small confession. For years, I didn't wear pink. I thought it was for little girls and wedding cakes. Then during a fitting, a young colleague in the atier handed me a dusty pink silk scarf and told me to hold it against my gray coat. I wanted to argue with her, and I couldn't. I didn't look younger by some magic. I looked rested, and that, my dear friends, is worth more. Christian Dior loved this very pair. His salon on the Avenue Monten was dressed in pale gray and white, and in his little dictionary of fashion, published in 1954, he praised gray as the most practical and the most elegant of the neutral colors. And he had warm words for pink, too. 70 years later, I can only agree with him. Here's how to choose the right pink. Hold it up near your face. If you see your cheeks warm up, it's the right one. If all you see is the pink, if it arrives before you do, it's too bright. Choose a softer one with a little gray in it, like a rose that has been dried in a book. Now, let's go from the sweater to the thing that sits on top of everything else for half the year. Your winter coat. The black wool coat buttoned up to the chin. For four or five months of the year, it's the first thing anyone sees of you, and it sits right under your face. All winter long, under gray skies, a big dark mass at the face makes the skin look pale and the eyes tired. Here, the swap is the easiest one in this whole video. A light color at the collar. You keep your dark coat, black, navy, or charcoal. And you put something cream, ivory, or soft camel, right under your face. A wool scarf, a knitted snood, the collar of a cream sweater showing at the neck. The coat stays practical. The light goes to your face, like candle light. And if you are choosing a new coat this winter, choose it the way a Parisian does, by your face, not by the hanger. In the store, stand by the window and hold the collar against your chin. A soft camel, an oatmeal, a warm gray, a deep navy. Each of them suits somebody. The right one makes your skin look rested. The wrong one sends you looking for more makeup. And now a question many of you ask me. Helen, must it be white? Can it be cream? And can my beige be camel? Oh yes, and very often it's better. A bright pure white. The kind called optical white can be a little harsh next to mature skin. Cream and ivory are softer. They have a drop of warmth in them. Camel and beige are sisters. Choose the shade that makes your skin look rested and trust your mirror. So this winter, keep the coat, change the collar. From something soft and warm, let's go to a color that is never shy. Red. The bright fire engine red sweater. The red jacket you bought for courage. I love red. It's joy. It's nerve. But a pure bright red right at the face arrives in the room before you do. People see the jacket first and you second. And a strong red has a cruel little gift. It picks up every bit of redness in the skin around the nose on the cheeks and makes it louder. Women often ask me almost sadly. I've always worn red. Must I give it up? Never. You don't give it up. You deepen it. The swap is burgundy and cream. Burgundy is the deep red of a good glass of wine. It's still red, still warm, still brave, but it doesn't shout. And cream beside it lightens the whole picture. A burgundy fine-nit sweater with cream trousers. A cream silk blouse under a burgundy cardigan. a cream coat with a burgundy scarf at the neck and if you like a lipstick with a little berry in it. It ties the whole look together very discreetly. The one rule here, let one color lead and the other follow. Don't split it half and half. A burgundy dress with a cream wrap, yes. Cream trousers with a burgundy sweater, yes. The eye likes to know who is the star. Your check. Ask a friend or a grandchild to take your photo in your red sweater and then in something burgundy. Look at both pictures. Where does your eye go first? To the sweater or to your face? In the right color, it goes to your eyes every time. And speaking of eyes, our next pair is the best friend they'll ever have. Joas, or that electric bright royal blue, especially in a shiny synthetic fabric. the polyester top that looks so fresh on the hanger. The trouble here is double. The color is louder than it needs to be and the shine catches the light in hard little spots on the bust, the shoulders, the tummy, exactly where we don't want the eye to stop. The swap is soft teal and charcoal. Teal is blue with a little green in it, the color of deep water, slightly muted. And charcoal is a very dark gray, the color of a charcoal drawing. Softer than black with the same slimming power. Charcoal is the frame. Teal is the picture. Teal is remarkable at the face. It brings out blue eyes, green eyes, hazel eyes, and it's wonderful with silver or salt and pepper hair. Wear it in matte silk or in a fine marino wool, a soft matte knit. A teal silk blouse under a charcoal blazer. A charcoal sweater with a teal scarf. A charcoal coat with teal at the neck. A soft teal scarf costs about $30 and it will change everything gray in your wardrobe. With it, silver jewelry. It echoes the coolness of the color. The first time I wore my teal scarf to fetch my grandchildren from school, my granddaughter told me I looked like a mermaid. I decided to take it as a compliment. I still do. And here is the fabric test for any color. Under a lamp, look at the fabric. If it shines like the inside of a plastic bag, it isn't your friend near the face. Rub it between your fingers. Matte silk, cotton, wool, those let the color glow softly instead of glaring. Our next pair is the one that comforts warm skin the most. Khaki with beige. Khaki trousers, a sand colored top, a stone jacket. Comfortable, practical, the outfit for the garden center or a long walk. But two muddy tones side by side cancel each other out. Together they make one flat, dusty field, and your face lands right in the middle of it. The swap is olive and ivory. Olive is the deep green of an olive, richer and clearer than khaki. Ivory is a soft white with a little warmth in it like old piano keys. The rule, the deep color away from the face, the light color at the face. So, olive trousers with an ivory silk blouse. An olive jacket over an ivory knit and ivory jeans. An olive skirt with an ivory cashmere sweater. Here's the simplest test I know, straight from the fitting room. Take a gold bracelet and a silver one. Hold the gold against your face and neck in daylight, then the silver. If gold makes you glow, your skin is most likely warm. Olive and ivory, camel and cream will love you. If silver flatters you more, you're most likely cool. Teal and charcoal, navy and white are your friends. And if both look lovely, lucky you, you can wear them all. I see it in fittings all the time.

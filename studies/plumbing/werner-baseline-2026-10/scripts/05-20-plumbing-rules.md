Somewhere in your house there is a valve that has not been turned in years, and on the day you need it, it will not move. You did not decide that. Nobody handed you a schedule and you skipped it. The schedule was never handed to you at all. So why does a house in Germany have that same valve turned twice a year, on a fixed date, by a person with no training? Because over there it is a rule, and the rule costs nothing. Twenty of those rules today, and the average American house breaks most of them.

  

I am Werner Finkbeiner. This is for anyone whose house has started talking back, in drips and knocks and a bill that keeps climbing, and every one of the twenty is something you can do with your hands this month.

  

A few of them will sound wrong at first. One keeps the water in your tank hotter than you have been told is safe, on purpose. One is a hose that costs about $30 and stands between you and the single most common flood in an American home. And there is a metal stick inside your water heater right now that you have never seen, and it is quietly being eaten, which is exactly what it is for. Underneath all twenty there is one rule about German plumbers themselves, and it explains why the other nineteen exist. That one I keep for the end.

  

Number one. A filter, right after the water meter.

  

Open the wall where the water comes into a German house and the first thing after the meter is a fine filter. Not a cartridge you throw away. A screen, sixty or so microns, with a clear bowl and a handle on the bottom. German drinking water standards require it in every new house. The reason is simple. The water main under your street is old iron, and every time the utility opens a valve two blocks away, sand and rust flakes and bits of scale come loose and ride into your house. Where do they stop? In the cartridge of your kitchen faucet. In the little screen at the tip of every tap, the aerator, which is why the water sprays sideways after a year. In the solenoid valve of your dishwasher and your washing machine, where one grain of grit holds the valve open a hair and the machine fills slowly and then not at all.

  

So. One filter catches all of it at the door. Once a month you turn the handle at the bottom, the water runs backward through the screen for a few seconds, the grit goes out a little hose to a drain, and the screen is clean again. That is the whole maintenance. The filter is around $80. A service call to replace a faucet cartridge is $150 to $300, and the cartridge was fine before the grit arrived.

  

Number two. A pressure reducer at the entry, set to 3 or 4 bar.

  

In psi, that is 45 to 60. Many American houses run at 70, some at 80, and the house feels great. The shower is strong. The hose reaches the far bed. And every rubber seal in the building is being pushed on, all day, every day, at a pressure the seal was never asked to hold. The washer inside a faucet. The flapper in the toilet. The braided supply line under the sink. The plastic line to the ice maker. They do not fail all at once. They fail one at a time, over years, and each one is a small mystery and a small invoice.

  

The German house has a brass regulator right after the filter with a dial on top, and it is set once, and the whole house lives at 4 bar. You can check your own pressure with a gauge that screws onto an outside hose bib. If it reads over 60, a reducer is about $60 in parts. And here is a sound to listen for. A pipe that whistles or sings when a tap opens is usually a high pressure pipe. It is not broken. It is complaining.

  

Number three is the one that sounds like a mistake, because the German tank runs hotter than the American one on purpose, and the reason has a name.

Number three. Hot water stored at 140 degrees, delivered at 120.

  

The American advice is to turn the water heater down to 120 degrees Fahrenheit so nobody gets scalded at the tap. Sensible. Except that 120 in the tank is the temperature where a bacterium called legionella is most comfortable, and a tank of warm standing water with a layer of sediment on the bottom is exactly where it grows. The German standard solves both problems with one fitting. The tank is held at 140, which the bacteria do not survive. And at the outlet of the tank there is a thermostatic mixing valve, a brass fitting about the size of your fist, that blends a little cold water in and sends 120 degree water to every tap in the house. The tank is hot. The tap is safe. The valve is around $100 and takes an afternoon.

  

So the tank is doing the job it should have been doing the whole time, and the shower does not know the difference.

  

Number four. The anode rod, checked every two years.

  

This is the metal stick. Inside your water heater, hanging from a hex head on top, there is a rod of magnesium or aluminum about as long as your arm. Its only job is to rust before the tank does. Water eats metal, and it eats the softest metal first, so the rod gives itself up and the steel tank stays whole. After a few years the rod is a wire with lumps on it, and after that the tank starts rusting from the inside, and one morning there is a puddle under it and the tank is finished. A water heater replacement runs $1,500 to $3,000. The rod is around $30 and it unscrews with a socket wrench. Every two years in Germany somebody pulls it and looks at it. If it is still fat, back in it goes. If it is a wire, a new one. It is the ugliest part in the house. That is the point of it.

  

Number five. No fat down any drain.

  

Not down the kitchen sink. Not down the toilet. Not chased with hot water and soap, which is the American trick, and it works for about three feet. The grease is liquid in the pan and liquid in the first warm section of pipe, and then the pipe goes cold under the floor and the grease does what grease does. It sets. A thin layer. Then a thin layer on the layer. The German kitchen keeps a tin or a jar by the stove, the pan gets wiped with a paper towel, and the jar goes in the trash when it is full. That is the whole system. A sewer line replacement is $3,000 to $15,000, and the line did not break. It filled.

  

Number six. A strainer in every drain.

  

Hair in the shower. Rice in the kitchen. The little basket costs a few dollars and sits in the drain opening, and once a week you lift it and empty it. Every drain, no exceptions, including the tub and the bathroom sink, which is where the toothpaste cap and the earring go. What goes into the pipe has to come out of the pipe, and the only two ways out are the strainer today or the auger next year. The strainer is quieter.

  

Number seven. Press fittings inside walls, push fittings only where you can reach.

  

Push fittings are the ones you press a pipe into and they hold with an O ring and a ring of teeth. Brilliant for a repair under a sink, where you can see it and get at it. The German trade will not put one inside a wall. Inside a wall goes a press fitting, a copper or steel sleeve crimped onto the pipe with a tool, permanent, no rubber, nothing to age. The logic is plain. A fitting you cannot see should be a fitting that cannot fail, and a fitting you can see may be a fitting you can service. Push fittings are for the cabinet. Press fittings are for the wall. Two different jobs.

  

Number eight. Hemp and paste on tapered threads.

  

Americans use white tape. Germans use a wisp of hemp fiber wound into the thread and a smear of sealing paste over it. Both work when the fitting goes on straight. The difference shows up when the fitting has to be turned back a quarter turn to line up with the wall, which happens on every job. Tape unwinds and the joint weeps. Hemp swells when it gets wet and keeps swelling into the gap, and the joint holds even after the fitting is backed off. A small bag of hemp lasts years. It looks like something from a barn. That is fine.

  

Number nine. A backwater valve on any drain below street level.

  

If you have a floor drain in the basement, or a toilet or a sink down there, that drain sits lower than the sewer in the street. In a heavy rain the sewer fills and the water looks for the lowest exit, and the lowest exit is your basement floor. A backwater valve is a flap in the drain line that swings one way. Out is open. In is closed. In Germany this is required for any fixture below the backup level of the street, and it is checked once a year, lid off, flap moved by hand. It is the difference between a wet spring and a ruined basement.

  

Number ten. A waterproofing membrane under every wet zone tile.

  

Tile is not waterproof. Grout is not waterproof. Water goes through grout the way it goes through a sugar cube, slowly and completely, and in an American shower it lands on drywall or on plywood, and five or eight years later the floor is soft and the wall behind the tile is black. The German shower has a membrane under the tile, a painted on liquid or a sheet, sealed at every corner and around the drain, and the tile on top is decoration. Water that gets through the grout runs on the membrane and finds the drain. The tile is the coat. The membrane is the skin.

  

So. Halfway. Stop a moment and look at the first ten together. A filter, a reducer, a mixing valve, a rod, a jar by the stove, a basket in the drain, a press sleeve in the wall, hemp in the thread, a flap in the drain line, a skin under the tile. Every one of them is a cheap thing placed between an expensive thing and the thing that destroys it. That is the whole German idea, and it has a word, Vorsorge, which means seeing to it in advance. The first ten were parts. The second ten are habits, and habits are cheaper still.

  

Number eleven. Rubber lined pipe clamps.

  

Turn a tap off fast and the water column stops and bangs the pipe. That is water hammer, and in an American house you hear it through the walls because the pipe is touching wood, or metal, or nothing at all. The German clamp has a rubber insert. The pipe sits in rubber, the rubber sits in steel, and the steel screws to the wall. The pipe still moves. Nothing hears it. A clamp is a dollar or two and the difference in a quiet house is complete. And a pipe that is held cannot rub, and a pipe that cannot rub cannot wear a hole in itself.

  

Number twelve. The main shutoff, exercised twice a year.

  

This is the valve from the beginning. Spring and fall, close it all the way, open it all the way, done. Ten seconds. A valve that sits for ten years grows a crust on the seat and the stem, and the day a supply line bursts under the sink, you run to the basement and it will not turn, and the water keeps coming while you look for a wrench. The German house has that date on the calendar next to the smoke detector batteries. Nobody thinks about it. It is simply done.

  

Number thirteen. An auto shutoff hose on the washing machine.

  

Listen, because this is the number one source of water damage in American homes, ahead of roofs and ahead of pipes. The rubber hose behind the washer is under full house pressure twenty four hours a day, and it is the cheapest hose in the building, and it is the one nobody looks at. When it lets go, it does not drip. It floods, at full pressure, for as long as nobody is home. The German hose has a small valve at the tap end. If the flow suddenly goes past what a washing machine could ever use, the valve slams closed by itself. Thirty dollars. The average insured water damage claim is over $10,000. That gap is the whole reason the hose exists.

  

Number fourteen. Foam insulation on every hot line.

  

A grey foam sleeve, slit down one side, pushed over the pipe. In the basement, in the crawlspace, wherever the hot pipe runs. The water in that pipe cools while you are not using it, and every time you open the tap you dump the cold water and wait for the hot to arrive, and the tank heats a fresh batch to replace what you poured away. Foam keeps the pipe warm between uses. Hot water arrives sooner, the tank works less, and on the cold lines the same sleeve stops the summer sweating that drips onto the floor below. A bag of sleeves and an afternoon.

  

Number fifteen. Bathroom silicone treated as a maintenance joint.

  

The bead of silicone where the tub meets the tile was never meant to last the life of the bathroom. The German trade has a name for it, a Wartungsfuge, a maintenance joint, and Wartung means upkeep, the thing you do on a schedule and not when it fails. Every few years the old bead gets cut out with a blade, the gap gets cleaned and dried, and a new bead goes in. Twenty minutes. If you wait until the bead is black and pulling away, water has been going behind it for months, and you are back at number ten.

  

Number sixteen. No cooking with hot tap water.

  

Hot water from the tap has been sitting in the tank, on top of the sediment, and in an older house it has been sitting in pipes with old solder in the joints, and it picks up a little of everything it touches. Cold water has been in the pipe for a moment. Fill the pot cold. Heat it on the stove. It is a slower start and a cleaner pot, and it is the only way a German kitchen fills a pot.

  

Number seventeen. Every tap flushed after a vacation.

  

Come home after two weeks and the water in your pipes is two weeks old. Warm, still, sitting in the same place. Before anyone drinks or showers, open every tap in the house, hot and cold, and let it run until the water goes cold and stays cold. Then the shower, with the head pointed at the wall. Two minutes a tap. Then the house is fresh again.

  

Number eighteen. The water meter read yearly, with a leak sweep.

  

Once a year, on the same date, write down the number on the meter. That is your baseline, and next year you will know whether the house used more water than it should have. And while you are standing there, do the sweep. Close every tap, make sure nothing is running, no washer, no ice maker, no toilet refilling, and look at the meter. There is a small dial or a small triangle on the face that turns with the slightest flow. If it is moving with everything off, something in the house is leaking, and it is usually a toilet. This is the cheapest leak detector ever built, and it came with the house.

  

Number nineteen. Descaling with citric acid, not lye or muriatic.

  

Lime builds in the kettle, on the showerhead, in the aerators, in the coffee machine. The American shelf sells you something strong. Muriatic acid eats the scale and then eats the chrome, and the fumes are not for a bathroom. Lye is for grease and it does nothing to lime. Citric acid is a white powder, sold cheaply in a bag, and a spoon of it in warm water dissolves lime and leaves the metal and the rubber alone. Soak the showerhead overnight in a bowl. Run a solution through the kettle. Rinse. That is the entire German descaling kit, and it has been the same powder for a very long time.

  

Number twenty. Dual flush or 1.28 gallon toilets.

  

An old American toilet uses three and a half gallons a flush, some older ones five. The modern standard is 1.28 gallons, and a dual flush has two buttons, a small flush and a full one. A toilet is flushed thousands of times a year, and every flush is on your water bill and on your sewer bill, which is usually charged off the water meter. The toilet is the biggest water user in the house, and it is the one fixture where using less changes nothing about how it works. It is the same toilet. It just stops throwing water away.

  

So. Twenty rules, and I said there was one thing underneath them. Here it is. In Germany a plumber is liable for his own work for five years. Not the manufacturer, not the homeowner. Him. If the joint weeps in year four, he comes back and he pays for it. Now go back through the list with that in mind. The filter, the reducer, the rubber clamp, the press fitting in the wall, the membrane under the tile. He installs every one of them because in five years the person standing in the wet basement will be calling his number, and he would rather never get the call. That is the difference in the two habits. One trade gets paid for the emergency. The other trade gets paid for the emergency that does not happen, and the second trade builds a quieter house. The rules were never about being German. They were about answering for the work.

  

Three things this week, and no more than three. Read your water meter with every tap closed and watch the small dial for a minute. Go to your main shutoff and turn it closed and open again. Then pull the washing machine away from the wall and look at the hose, and if you cannot remember buying it, you have your answer.

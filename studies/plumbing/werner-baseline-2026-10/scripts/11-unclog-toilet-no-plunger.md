First, before anything goes in, look at the water in the bowl. If it sits a few inches below the rim, you have room to work. If it is close to the brim, it has to come down first, and there is nothing elegant about how. \[matter-of-fact\] Rubber gloves, an old plastic container, a spare bucket, and you bail, one scoop at a time, until the bowl is about half full.

  

Then the valve. Low on the wall behind the toilet, on the pipe that feeds the tank, there is a small handle. Clockwise closes it, and with it closed the tank cannot refill, so a bowl that is already in trouble never gets a second tankful. \[thoughtful\] That little valve is the kind of information nobody hands you with the keys to a house.

  

So. Behind the hole at the bottom of the bowl, inside the porcelain, the toilet has a curved channel called the trapway. It climbs, bends over, and drops toward the drain, and that bend is where a clog sits, usually paper and waste packed into the curve. The plug is soft, but it holds itself together, and it holds on to the walls.

  

The American answer to that is a plunger, which is force. \[slightly amused\] You push, the water slops over the rim, and sometimes the plug moves and sometimes it only packs in tighter. The German kitchen answer is older, duller, and gentler. It changes the plug itself, so that it lets go of the walls on its own. Slippery first, then soft, then a little weight from above, then time.

  

\[quietly\] One honest word, early. Soap and hot water work on the soft clog, paper and waste, which is the usual kind. They do nothing for a toy car or a toothbrush that has dropped in. That is why four more steps stand behind the kitchen ones, each more serious than the last, ending with the same tool a plumber carries through your front door. If the trouble outlasts even that, it is lodged hard in the porcelain or further down the pipe, and I will tell you exactly how to know when to stop.

  

And the water has a number attached, one heat that clears a toilet and another that can crack it, and that number comes right after the soap.

Step one, the soap. Ordinary dish soap, the bottle beside the sink. About a quarter of a cup it wants, a long and generous squeeze, more than any sink of dishes gets. Pour it straight into the bowl. \[matter-of-fact\] Dish soap is a little heavier than water, so it does not float on top. It sinks to the bottom of the bowl and into the mouth of the trapway, right where the plug is. There it does two jobs. It coats the curve so the plug can slide, and it breaks the surface tension, the tight skin of the water that helps hold the whole mass together. Once that skin is gone, the plug stops acting like one solid thing and starts acting like a lot of small soft things, and small soft things travel. Leave it be, and go for the water.

  

Step two, the water, and here is the number. \[slower\] Hot, but not boiling, somewhere between a hundred and twenty and a hundred and forty degrees Fahrenheit. Run the kitchen tap until it is as hot as it gets, and you are usually near the low end of that range. If you warm water on the stove instead, it comes off long before the first bubbles. \[short pause\] Boiling is the mistake, and an easy one, because boiling sounds like the stronger version. Porcelain dislikes a sudden change. Cold porcelain hit with boiling water can crack from the shock of it, thermal shock, it is called, and a cracked bowl means a new toilet. There is a second thing down there you cannot see. Under the toilet, sealing it to the drain, sits a ring of wax, and wax that softens and shifts can start a slow leak you discover later, in the floor. So the number protects two things, the porcelain and the seal. And at that heat, the water does its own work, loosening paper and waste far faster than cold water could.

  

Step three is how it goes in. Fill a bucket with that hot water and carry it carefully, because hot water and a bathroom floor are a poor pairing. From about waist height you pour, steady, aimed at the middle of the bowl. \[matter-of-fact\] The height is the point. Water falling from waist height lands with weight, and that weight is a gentle push on a plug that is already slippery from the soap and softening in the heat. Gentle is plenty, once the plug has stopped holding on. Keep an eye on the level as you pour. If it climbs toward the rim, stop, let it settle for a minute, and pour the rest more slowly.

  

\[slightly amused\] Step four is the one that feels wrong, because it is nothing at all. Fifteen minutes, at the least. Thirty is better. Close the bathroom door, go and make a coffee, and let the soap and the heat work without you. Every minute you wait is a minute you are not reaching for force, and force too early can pack a soft clog tighter. Then come back, and before you touch the handle, look. If the water has dropped on its own, the plug has let go. Lift the lid off the tank first, and flush once. If the water starts to rise instead of falling, press the rubber flapper at the bottom of the tank down with your hand, and the flush stops. A bowl that empties with a proper swirl is finished. Good. Then the valve on the wall goes back on, if you closed it. \[emphatic\] If the water sits exactly where you left it, do not flush. Do the whole thing a second time, soap, hot water, the pour, the wait. Some plugs want two rounds.

  

So. Halfway, and worth one look back. \[thoughtful\] Four steps, and not one of them was force. The soap made the plug slippery, the heat made it soft, the height of the pour gave it a little weight, and time did the work that force usually gets the credit for. All four go with the shape of the toilet, and none of them argues with it. The next four steps do add force, one tool at a time, and each one earns its place only if it treats the porcelain with the same respect. And at the very end, this same idea turns up in rooms that have nothing to do with a toilet.

  

Step five is for water that still sits high after two rounds. An empty two-liter plastic bottle, the soda kind. Cut the bottom off with scissors or a utility knife, and keep the cap screwed on tight. What you are holding now is a tube, open at one end and sealed at the other, which makes it a piston. Gloves on. \[slower\] Hold it by the neck, push the open end down into the hole at the bottom of the bowl until it sits snug, and work it, down and up, in short, firm strokes. With the cap on, the air and water inside have nowhere to go but forward, so every stroke sends a pulse of pressure against the plug. Give it a good run of strokes, then stop and watch the level. If it drops, flush, with the tank lid off and your hand ready at the flapper. \[slightly amused\] Not a handsome tool, a cut bottle. It does not need to be. It was finished being a bottle anyway, and it still had one more job in it.

  

Step six, if the bottle has not moved it. A wire coat hanger. Untwist it and straighten it, leaving a small curve at one end, and wrap that end in several turns of electrical or duct tape, so no bare metal touches the inside of the bowl. \[matter-of-fact\] That tape matters more than it looks, and the reason comes in a few minutes. Gloves on again. Feed the taped end into the drain opening and follow the curve of the trapway, pushing gently and turning as you go, feeling for the plug. When you meet it, work it, a small push and a small twist, until it breaks apart or hooks loose. A hanger will not reach all the way through, but it reaches the first bend, and the first bend is a common place for a plug to sit. Then the same habit as always. Look at the water before you flush.

  

Step seven turns the whole direction around. Every step so far has pushed the plug forward, and a shop vacuum pulls it back, up the way it went down. Only a wet-rated one, though, the kind with wet and dry printed on it, set up for wet pickup the way its manual says. \[emphatic\] Never the vacuum from the hall closet. A household vacuum is made for dust, and water pulled into a machine that is plugged into the wall is how a bad morning becomes an injury. First, use the wet-rated vacuum to pull the standing water out of the bowl. Then push the end of the hose into the drain opening and wrap an old towel around it to seal the gap. Switch it on. The suction pulls on the plug from the side it came in, and a plug that refused to go forward will sometimes come straight back. Clean the canister well, and the vacuum goes back on its shelf, where it lives.

  

Step eight is for a plug that will go neither forward nor back, and it is the real tool. A toilet auger, sometimes called a closet auger. A crank handle, a long tube, and a coiled steel cable inside, with a curved end made to sit in the bowl, and around that end a rubber guard, a sleeve that keeps the steel off the porcelain. \[slower\] Set the curved end in the drain opening with the guard against the bowl, then turn the crank while you push the cable forward. It follows the bend of the trapway by itself, which a hanger never could, and when it meets the plug, it either bores through or snags it so you can draw it back. Crank and push, feel for the resistance, and when the cable runs free, pull it back slowly, still cranking, and see what it brought with it.

  

This is the tool a plumber brings to a stopped toilet. \[matter-of-fact\] The visit for one runs a hundred and fifty to three hundred dollars. The auger, from the hardware store, is twenty-five to forty dollars. The dish soap you started with was about two dollars. \[short pause\] For an ordinary clog, the difference between those numbers is mostly who owns the tool.

  

And now the honest part I promised. If the auger goes as far as it can and the water still sits high, or if the bathtub gurgles and water rises in the shower drain when you flush, the trouble is past the toilet, down in the drain line, or it is something hard lodged where the cable cannot hook it. \[quietly\] Past that point, the job belongs to a plumber, and making that call then is simply sensible.

  

Two things, though, should never go into a stopped toilet, and both of them are sold as the quick way. The first is a chemical drain opener, the bottle from the cleaning aisle. Many of those work by a chemical reaction that gives off heat, and you already know what sudden heat does to porcelain. And if it fails, the bowl is now full of caustic water that whoever comes next, you or a plumber, has to put hands and tools into. \[matter-of-fact\] The second is an ordinary drain snake, the kind meant for a sink or a tub. It has no guard. Its bare steel scrapes the glaze inside the bowl, and here is why the hanger got its tape. Scratched glaze is rough, rough porcelain gives mineral scale a foothold, and scale is exactly where the next clog catches. The toilet auger wears its rubber sleeve for that reason. In German there is one word for both of these mistakes. \[slightly amused\] Pfusch. Botched work, the kind of fix that leaves a bigger job behind it.

  

And if your toilet does this more than once in a while, it is telling you something, usually one of four things. The first is a partial obstruction, something small and hard sitting in the trapway, a toothpaste cap or a little plastic toy, that lets water by but catches paper every single time. The auger can often bring that back up. If it cannot, the toilet has to come off the floor, and that is a job to hand over. The second is a low water level in the tank. Lift the lid. Many tanks have a line marked on the inside wall, and the water should reach it. If it sits well below, every flush is a weak flush, and a weak flush leaves paper in the bend. The fill valve sets that level, and the adjustment is usually a small screw or a clip. \[matter-of-fact\] The third is wipes. Wipes do not break apart the way toilet paper does, including many sold as flushable, so they go in a lidded bin beside the toilet, every time. The fourth is the toilet itself. Some bowls empty by siphon through a narrow trap, and a narrow bend simply catches more than a wide one. If that is yours, use less paper per flush, flush partway through when there is a lot, and keep the dish soap close. \[slightly amused\] Every one of these is a Tuesday afternoon job if you catch it early, and an eleven at night job if you do not.

  

So. Eight steps, two mistakes, four reasons, and looked at all together, they were never separate tricks. \[thoughtful\] They were one idea, and that idea lives in more rooms than the bathroom. Make the stuck thing slippery, make it soft, give it time, and only then bring force, and bring it with something that respects the material. In the kitchen, that is the jar lid that will not turn, held under hot water until the metal gives a little. In the bedroom, it is the wooden drawer that sticks, with a bar of soap rubbed along its runner. In the garage, it is the rusted bolt, oiled and left alone overnight before anyone reaches for a longer wrench. \[warm\] A stopped toilet is the same patience, with a bucket in it.

  

Three small things for this week, then. Find the valve on the wall behind your toilet and put your hand on it once, so you know where it is without looking. Put rubber gloves, a bucket, and a two-liter bottle with the bottom already cut off under the bathroom sink, where you will find them at eleven at night. And lift the lid off the tank and check that the water reaches its line.

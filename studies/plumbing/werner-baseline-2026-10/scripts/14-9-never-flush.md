\[matter-of-fact\] Look at the floor beside your toilet. That little stretch of tile between the bowl and the wall. In most American bathrooms, it is empty. In a German bathroom, usually, a small bin stands there, with a lid. It is the answer to one plain sentence the water utilities in Germany have repeated for years: the toilet is not a trash can.

  

Nine things stay out of the toilet there, and in plenty of American homes, most of them go down it in an ordinary week. One has a word printed on its package that your pipes do not agree with. One is a thread so thin you would never think it could stop anything, and it can stop a pump a long way from your house. And besides what your own body sends there, exactly one thing is allowed in the bowl. \[quietly\] It was built on purpose to do something the nine cannot.

  

I am Werner Finkbeiner. This channel is for people in older houses and apartments who would rather understand the cause than pay for the visit. Today we go through all nine, one at a time, and look at what the German habit does instead.

  

So. We will go the way the water goes. Flush, and follow. Each of the nine stops somewhere, and each one stops a little farther from your house than the one before.

  

Number one never leaves the toilet. Cotton swabs. The little stick with cotton on both ends.

  

\[thoughtful\] Here is the part nobody sees. Inside the porcelain, the water does not fall straight down. It climbs up and over a curve molded into the toilet itself. That curve is called the trapway, and it is what holds water in the bowl and keeps the sewer smell out of your bathroom. Good design. But a stiff stick does not always follow a curve. It can turn sideways and catch across the passage, like a bar across a doorway.

  

One swab, maybe nothing happens. But now there is a bar in the doorway. The next piece of paper catches on it. Then the next. The toilet starts flushing slow, and you decide the toilet is getting old. The toilet is fine. \[slightly amused\] There is a stick in it.

  

If you think one is already in there, the plunger is the wrong tool. It can push the stick deeper into the curve. A toilet auger, the short kind made for toilets, can sometimes hook it out. If it will not come, the toilet has to come up off the floor, and that is a fair place to stop and call someone.

  

\[matter-of-fact\] The German habit costs nothing. The swab goes in the bin beside the toilet. In German the word for this is Ordnung, and people hear it and think of neatness. It is not neatness. It means every thing has a place it goes, and the place for a swab is not a pipe.

  

Number two makes it a little farther, past the bowl and around the bend under the floor. Paper towels, and the tissues you blow your nose into.

  

These look like the paper that does belong in the bowl. Same aisle in the store, same soft white. \[emphatic\] They are not the same paper. A paper towel is made to hold together when it is soaking wet. That is the whole point of it. Nobody wants a towel that comes apart in the hand halfway through wiping a spill. Facial tissues are made the same way, to stay in one piece when they get wet. In the pipe, they do exactly what they were built to do. They stay whole.

  

A wad of paper towel goes around that bend and settles in the low spot, still in one piece. The next flush pushes it a little. Or it does not. In a German home, the used tissue goes in the bin by the toilet, and the paper towel goes in the kitchen trash. Two more steps of walking. That is all this habit asks.

  

\[short pause\] Still ahead is the package with that one word printed on it, and what it turns into under the street has a name of its own.

Number three gets out from under the bathroom and into the drain line inside the walls. Hair. From the brush, from the razor, from the clump you pull out of the shower drain and drop in the toilet because the toilet is right there.

  

Hair does not dissolve. It does not soften. One hair is weak, but a few hundred together are surprisingly strong, and they do something worse than block. \[emphatic\] They catch. A drain line has joints and rough spots and small edges, and hair hooks onto one of them and stays. Then it is a net. Soap catches in the net. Paper catches in the net. A little grease from the kitchen, where the lines join. And the slow drain you blame on old pipes is very often a net of hair somewhere you cannot see.

  

The habit here is almost too small to mention. Hair from the brush goes in the bin. The clump from the shower drain goes in the bin too, folded into a square of toilet paper. \[quietly\] Thirty seconds, and the net never gets its first thread.

  

Number four leaves the house entirely, into the line under your yard that carries everything out to the street. Cat litter.

  

Most of the litter people buy now clumps. That is its job. It meets moisture, it swells, and it binds into a hard lump, so you can scoop it. \[thoughtful\] Now put it in a pipe, which is nothing but moisture. It does the same job there. It swells, it binds, and because it is heavy, it settles in the low spots of the line. Over time it sets up hard, like a patch of cement on the bottom of the pipe. Water still gets over it, at first. Less of it every week.

  

Some litters say they are flushable. In a German household the question hardly comes up. The litter goes in a bag, and the bag goes in the trash. \[matter-of-fact\] I would do the same, whatever the bag of litter says. And that brings us to the item where a printed word matters most.

  

Number five crosses the property line, and it is the one with the word on the package. Wet wipes. Baby wipes, makeup wipes, cleaning wipes, and yes, the ones labeled flushable.

  

Think about what a wipe has to do on the shelf. It sits in a wet package for weeks and comes out in one piece. That is a fine thing in a package. \[emphatic\] In a pipe, it is the whole problem. The ones labeled flushable may come apart more than the others, given enough time and enough tumbling. They do not come apart the way the paper that belongs in the bowl does, and not in the short trip from your toilet to the street.

  

The wipe travels. If there is a root in your yard line, or a joint that has shifted, the wipe hooks on it, and the next wipe hooks on the first. Your house will usually warn you before it backs up. The toilet flushes slow. The tub gurgles when it does. Water comes up in the lowest drain in the house. \[matter-of-fact\] If the trouble is out in the yard line, this is where you stop. That one belongs to someone with a machine.

  

And if the wipe gets past your property line, the trouble only changes owners. It jams the city's pumps, and it binds with grease into the thing sewer crews call a fatberg. One industry estimate in the United States puts what wipes cost the utilities at roughly four hundred forty million dollars a year.

  

The bin with the lid, that is the German habit here. The wipe goes there, whatever the package says.

  

So. Five down, four to go, and we are standing at the edge of your property. \[thoughtful\] Look back up the pipe for a moment. The first five have one thing in common. Every one of them stops, or can stop, on the part of the system that belongs to the house. The bowl. The bend under the floor. The line in the walls. The line under the yard.

  

Here is what that part costs when it fails. A blocked sewer line, cleared, runs somewhere between one hundred fifty and five hundred dollars. A new toilet, installed, starts around three hundred dollars. A line under the yard that has to be dug up and replaced, three thousand to fifteen thousand dollars.

  

The bin with the lid, the one from the start of this video, is under ten dollars.

  

\[short pause\] There is a German word for spending the ten dollars before the bigger number arrives. Vorsorge. Care, taken ahead of time.

  

In Germany, nobody gets to forget who pays. Wastewater is billed by the cubic meter, so every flush shows up on the bill. And a tenant who causes a blockage can be handed that repair bill by the landlord. When the mistake comes back with your name on it, you learn the list quickly. That is why it is common knowledge there.

  

The next four stop past your property line, on the city's side. That sounds like someone else's problem. \[slightly amused\] It is not, quite. The city pays for the pumps and the treatment plant, and that bill comes back to you as well. It only arrives slower, folded into the sewer rate.

  

Number six goes out under the street, into the city's main line. Food scraps, and cooking fat. The grease from the bacon pan, the fat from a roast.

  

Some people pour fat into the toilet instead of the sink, thinking the toilet is the stronger drain. It is not stronger. It is the same pipe a few feet later. Warm, fat pours easily. Then it meets cold pipe and cold water, and it turns back into what it was in the pan once the pan cooled. \[emphatic\] A solid. It coats the inside of the pipe, a little thicker with every pour, and food scraps catch in it. Rice. Coffee grounds. The last of a pot of soup.

  

And here it meets number five. The wipe gives the fat something to grab, and the fat gives the wipe something to stick to. Together, they grow. In some cities, crews have had to climb down and break these masses apart with shovels. That is the fatberg, and now you have met both halves of it.

  

\[slightly amused\] The German habit is boring, which is how you know it works. Fat cools in a can or an old jar, then goes in the trash with the lid on. Scraps go in the food waste bin, or the regular trash. Nothing from the plate goes in the bowl.

  

Number seven goes past the street, to a pump station. It is the thread. Dental floss.

  

It looks like nothing at all. But floss is made from strong fibers, usually nylon or another plastic, and it is designed not to snap while you pull it tight between your teeth. It does not snap in a pipe either. And it does not rot.

  

Where the ground runs uphill, the city cannot count on gravity, so it builds stations full of pumps to lift the sewage along. Inside each pump is a spinning part called an impeller. The floss finds the impeller and winds around it, around and around, and it brings company. Hair. Wipes. \[slower\] Every loop catches a little more, until the pump strains, and stops, and somebody has to go down and cut it loose by hand.

  

It does the same thing closer to home. If a plumber ever runs a snake into your line, floss wraps around the head of that snake like thread on a spool.

  

In a German bathroom, used floss goes in the bin. A piece of thread, into a bin. It seems too small to matter. \[quietly\] It is not.

  

Number eight goes all the way to the treatment plant. Medicine. Old pills, leftover syrup, the bottle at the back of the cabinet that expired some time ago.

  

Here is what most people assume. That the treatment plant cleans everything out. It does a great deal. It takes out the solids, and it breaks down most of what we send it. \[matter-of-fact\] But most plants were never built to pull medicine out of water. A good share of it passes straight through and leaves with the treated water, into the river or the lake.

  

The German guidance is plain. Old medicine goes in the household trash, or back to the pharmacy. In the United States, the same job belongs to take-back programs. Many pharmacies have a drop box, and there are national take-back days as well. \[matter-of-fact\] A few medicines come with their own disposal instructions on the label, and those you follow. Everything else, out of the cabinet, into a bag, and to the drop box.

  

Number nine travels the farthest of them all. Cigarette butts.

  

A cigarette filter looks like cotton. It is not cotton. It is a plastic fiber, and it holds on to what was in the smoke. In water, those chemicals start to leach out. And the filter itself does not break down in a few weeks, or a few months. \[quietly\] It can take years.

  

Somewhere on the route, the plant may screen out the filter. What has already soaked out of it into the water is much harder to catch, and some of it goes on to the river.

  

If you smoke, the German habit is simple. The ashtray empties into the trash, never the toilet and never the sink. Outside, a metal can with some sand in it does the job.

  

So. That is the river, and the end of the route. Now look at where these nine came from. The bathroom, yes. But also the kitchen. The corner where the litter box sits. The back step where somebody smokes. It looks like nine rules for nine different things. It is one rule.

  

\[slower\] Down is not away. When you flush, a thing does not disappear. It starts a trip. And whatever does not come apart in water stays what it is the whole way, until it stops somewhere. In your pipe, in the city's pipe, or in the river.

  

Which brings us to the one thing that belongs in the bowl. Toilet paper. It is allowed for one reason. \[emphatic\] It is the only paper in your house that is engineered to fall apart. It is made with short fibers and very little holding them together, so in moving water it breaks down into pulp. A paper towel is built to survive water. Toilet paper is built to lose to it.

  

That is the whole test, and only one thing in the house passes it.

  

\[warm\] Three small jobs for this week. Put a small bin with a lid on that empty spot beside the toilet. Move the wipes so they live next to the bin, not next to the bowl. And keep one empty jar by the stove, so the next pan of fat has somewhere to cool that is not a pipe.

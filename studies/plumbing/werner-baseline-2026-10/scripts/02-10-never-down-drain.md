Right now, under your kitchen sink, there is a bend in the pipe with water standing in it. The water is a seal. It keeps the sewer out of your house. It is also a net. Whatever went down this week and did not dissolve is still sitting in that bend, waiting for the next thing to arrive and hold onto it. But in a German kitchen, most of what is in that bend would never have gone down at all. And not because of plumbing.

I am Werner Finkbeiner. This is for people who own or rent an older house, an older apartment, something that has started talking back, and who were simply never told what a drain is for. Ten things a German household will not put down one. Americans put most of them down weekly.

Three of them will surprise you. One is a thing somebody you trust told you to pour down there on purpose, to clean it. One goes in as a paste and comes out as a sidewalk, if it comes out at all. And the very first one is stopped by a container you already own and already finished using.

So. Why a German kitchen.

In Germany the sink grinder, the garbage disposal, is rare, and in many towns the local wastewater rules do not permit one at all. There is no machine under the sink whose job is to make food small enough to fit down the pipe. Food goes into a separate bin, the organic bin, and it leaves with the household waste. That is the whole difference, and every habit in this video grows out of it.

When there is no grinder, nobody grows up believing a drain can handle a solid. The hand learns to scrape the plate into the bin before the plate ever reaches the water. The pan gets wiped with kitchen paper while it is still warm. This is Ordnung, which does not mean tidiness. It means the thing has a place and it goes to the place. The tool goes back on the wall. The fat goes in the jar.

And when the drain does not clear, it is a service call. Running a snake down a kitchen line costs a hundred and fifty dollars to three hundred and fifty in most of the country. The jar that would have stopped it costs nothing. You already have it. You are going to throw it away this week.

Number one. Cooking fat and cooking oil.

Your eye tells you the fat is liquid, and liquid goes down a drain. The pipe knows something else. The fat in that pan is liquid because the pan is hot. Four feet down, inside a pipe sitting against a cold wall or a cold slab, it is not hot anymore. It cools. It stops moving. It attaches itself to the top of the pipe, which is the part the running water does not scrub, and it waits there for the next thing to come along.

That is the beginning of what the newspapers call a fatberg. A mass of congealed cooking fat bound to everything else that came through behind it. Cities on both sides of the ocean have pulled them out of sewers by the ton. But the thing does not begin in the sewer. It begins about four feet from where you are standing when you rinse the pan.

The German habit here is unglamorous. Cold fat goes into a jar with a lid, or into the carton the cream came in, and the jar goes in the trash. You buy nothing and you install nothing.

And before the pan reaches the sink at all, it gets wiped. One sheet of kitchen paper, while the pan is still warm and the film comes off easily. What does not go in does not have to come out.

Number two. Food scraps. And inside that, two in particular. Rice and pasta.

Everything else on a plate is an argument. Rice and pasta are not an argument. They are small, they are slippery, they look like they were built to go down a drain, and they are close to the worst thing you can put in one, because they are not finished growing.

Dry rice swells. Cooked rice swells again. Pasta does the same. They go down loose and easy, they arrive at the bend under the sink where the water is standing, and they sit in that water and drink it. A tablespoon becomes a handful. A handful becomes a plug, and the next pour of warm grease seals the plug shut.

There is no grinder in a German kitchen to argue with. The plate gets scraped into the organic bin, standing at the bin, before the plate goes near the tap. The tap is the last step, never the first.

If you do own a disposal, keep it for what falls off the plate by accident, not for what you meant to throw away. A disposal makes food smaller. Smaller food still has to go somewhere.

Number three is the one that will annoy some of you, because the person who told you to do it meant well, and they were not trying to cost you money.

Number three. Coffee grounds.

Somewhere along the way a story got started that coffee grounds clean a drain. That they scrub it on the way past. That they take the smell out. The same story exists in German kitchens, in German, and it is just as wrong there.

Here is what a plumber finds when he opens the trap under a sink where the grounds have been going for a year. A packed black layer, pressed into the grease that was already sitting there, the two of them holding each other in place. Coffee grounds do not dissolve and they do not scrub. They are sand that happens to smell nice, and sand settles.

The test is easy and you can run it tonight. Put a spoonful of grounds into a glass of water and leave it on the counter until morning. Whatever is on the bottom of that glass is what is on the bottom of your pipe, except that your pipe has grease in it and the glass does not.

Grounds go in the organic bin. In a German house that is not a rule anybody argues with, because the bin is already standing there for everything else.

Number four. Leftover medicine.

This one is different from the others and I want to be honest about why. The pills you flush do not hurt your pipe. They go straight through. They arrive at the treatment plant, and the plant does the job it was built to do, which is to take out solids and organic matter and bacteria. It was not built to take out pharmaceuticals, and largely it does not. What goes in comes out the far side and goes into the river.

German guidance on this is plain. Leftover medicine goes into the household trash, because household waste there is incinerated, or it goes back to the pharmacy counter. It does not go in the toilet and it does not go in the sink.

In the United States the clean answer is a take back. Plenty of pharmacies keep a drop box just inside the door, and there are collection days run a few times a year where you hand over a bag and walk out again. Nobody asks you for a form or a reason.

And nobody is asking you to be a saint about this. Only to stop treating the toilet as a wastebasket, which is a thing nobody ever told you not to do.

Number five. Paint, solvent, and thinner.

Latex paint dries. That is the entire product. It is engineered to turn into a solid film the moment it stops being wet, and it does not know the difference between a wall and the inside of a drain pipe. Rinse a roller often enough and the pipe gets a coat of paint, thin and even, and the pipe gets a little narrower every time.

Solvent and thinner are worse in a different direction. They do not build up. They leave fast, and they take the treatment plant apart on the way through, because that plant is running on bacteria and solvent kills bacteria.

In Germany both of these have a fixed address and everybody knows the address. The recycling yard. Cans go in the trunk, you drive over on a Saturday, you hand them across a counter.

Here it is the household hazardous waste day, or a permanent drop off at the county site. Same idea, less signage.

Brush water sits in a bucket overnight instead. The solids fall to the bottom, the clear water gets poured off, and what is left dries out and goes in the trash.

So. Five of them. Stop here for a second and look at what they have in common, because it is not the room they happen in.

Every one of the first five went down looking harmless, and every one of them looked harmless for the same reason. It was moving. Fat moves while it is hot. Rice moves while it is loose. Grounds move while the tap is running. Paint moves while it is wet. Medicine moves because it dissolved.

Water is what makes all of it look like liquid. And water is the one thing in that pipe that does not stay.

When the water leaves, the substance is still there, and now it is cold, or swollen, or set, or packed. Everything you have poured down a drain in your life, you have only ever seen the wet version of.

The second half of this list is the same lesson standing in other rooms.

Number six. Wallpaper paste, tile grout, and the bucket of water you washed the cement out of.

These do not congeal and they do not swell. They cure. Curing is a chemical event, and once it starts it does not care where it is. Grout that sets inside a horizontal run of pipe is no longer a clog. It is now part of the pipe.

A snake will not take it out. Hot water does nothing. Depending on where it set, the answer can be opening a wall or opening the yard, and a main sewer line replacement runs three thousand to fifteen thousand dollars. That is the range for one bucket of tile water somebody tipped into the utility sink because the utility sink was right there.

The German version of this rule is short and it covers every one of these products. Anything that hardens, hardens somewhere you chose. Wallpaper paste goes in the trash inside the bucket it is already in. Grout water settles overnight and the dry solids get thrown out. Cement water goes into a tub and stays there until it is a lump, and the lump goes in the trash.

Number seven. Cat litter.

The bag sometimes says flushable. Set that aside and look at what the product actually is. Clumping litter is bentonite clay, and the entire job of bentonite clay is to meet liquid and lock into a solid mass. That is what you are buying it for. That is why it works in the box.

Now put it into a pipe that is full of liquid by definition. It does exactly the job it was designed to do, in the wrong place, where nobody can reach it.

There is a second part to this one and it is about the water rather than your pipe. Cat waste can carry an organism that treatment plants are not built to remove, and it ends up in coastal water. Water authorities on both coasts have been asking people to stop flushing it for years.

The litter goes in a bag, the bag goes in the trash. There is no German trick here. The trick is that nobody in a German house ever believed the toilet was a second trash can.

Number eight. Wipes. Baby wipes, cleaning wipes, and the ones with the word flushable printed on the front.

Every wastewater association I know of puts these at the top of its complaint list, above grease, above roots, above everything. And the word on the package is not quite a lie. It is answering a smaller question than you think it is. Flushable means the wipe will leave the bowl. Nobody ever promised you it would leave the pipe.

Toilet paper is built to fall apart in water. That is the only thing it is engineered to do and it does it in seconds. A wipe is built to survive being wet, because a wipe that fell apart in your hand would be a bad wipe. So it goes down whole, it reaches the first rough joint or the first bend, and it stops. Then the next one arrives and hooks onto it.

Add grease to that and you have the job that needs a jet truck. Hydro jetting a line that has gone over to grease and wipes runs three hundred and fifty to six hundred dollars, and the line will fill again if the habit does not change.

In a German bathroom there is a small bin beside the toilet, it is not unusual, and nobody is embarrassed by it. That is the whole solution. A bin with a lid, emptied when you empty the others.

Number nine. Hair.

Hair is the one nobody feels guilty about, because hair is not something you decided to put down the drain. It just goes. And on its own it would mostly be fine. Hair is thin and slippery and it would ride the water out.

On its own is the part that never happens. Hair goes down with soap, and with the scum that soap leaves behind in hard water, and that scum binds the strands together the way a rope is made, one catching the next. What comes out of a shower drain after two years is a solid gray cable with a shape to it. You have seen one. Everybody has seen one.

The answer is a flat strainer sitting in the drain, emptied into the trash when you get out. That is the whole repair.

German bathrooms have had these forever and nobody thinks of one as a gadget. It is the same thinking as the jar under the sink. Catch the solid while you can still see it.

Number ten. Motor oil.

This is the one where the number people repeat is hard to believe and the direction is not in dispute. Water authorities have been saying for decades that one liter of used motor oil can spoil a million liters of water. Whether that figure is exact, I cannot tell you. That used oil does not belong in a drain, or on the driveway, or in the yard, nobody argues about.

Used motor oil is also not the oil you bought. It has been through a hot engine and it comes out carrying metal, and what a treatment plant does with that is not much.

In Germany this one is written into law, and it is also easy, because the shop that sold you the oil has to take the old oil back.

In the United States it is nearly the same and hardly anybody knows it. The auto parts store on the corner takes used oil. Most municipal sites take it. You pour it into the jug the new oil came in, you carry it in, and somebody points you at a barrel behind the counter. It takes a few minutes and nobody charges you for it.

So. Ten things. And I will tell you now that they were never ten things.

Look back down the list. Fat, rice, grounds, medicine, paint, grout, litter, wipes, hair, oil. Kitchen, bathroom, workshop, garage. Four rooms, ten substances, and underneath them one question asked ten times in ten places.

Does this thing become water.

That is the whole rule. If it becomes water, it can leave with the water. If it does not become water, then the water is only carrying it, and carrying is temporary. The water goes on. The thing stops. At the first cold wall, at the first bend, at the first rough joint, or at a plant that was never built to handle it.

A drain is a door. Things go through it and keep going, into a pipe you do not own, into water that somebody else eventually drinks. That is all it has ever been. It was never a container, and nobody ever told you so, because the machine under the sink quietly suggested otherwise.

This is what a German house calls Vorsorge, which means looking after a thing before anything is wrong with it. The jar, the bin, the strainer, the Saturday drive to the yard. Small, dull, and early.

Somewhere in your house right now, a window is open about one inch at the top, and it has been open like that since October. You do not think of it as open. It is tilted. It is airing the room. And the heating bill that came last month, the one you blamed on the furnace, on the old house, on the utility company, that bill was partly written by that one inch of window. Germans have a name for what you are doing. They call it heating the street.

  

My name is Werner Finkbeiner, and this is for anyone in the United States paying somewhere between 600 and 1,200 dollars to heat a house through a cold winter and wondering where it all goes. So. Ten rules. Ten habits a German household runs on from November to March, most of them older than the furnace in your basement, and every one of them costs nothing or close to it. Somewhere in the ten is a rule that makes your bedroom colder on purpose. There is a piece of foil that costs 10 dollars and turns an outside wall around. And there is one rule that German law now requires in apartment buildings, which for your furnace is a phone call you have been putting off.

  

We start where the heat leaves. The window.

  

Rule number one. Never heat a room with the window tilted.

  

The tilted window is the great American compromise. The room feels stuffy, so you crack the window, or you tilt it, or you leave the bathroom one inch open all day for the steam. It feels moderate, a little fresh air and a little heat and everybody happy. The physics disagree. A window tilted one inch moves almost no air. What it does move is heat, and it moves it all day long, because the gap sits at the top of the frame exactly where the warmest air in the room collects. That warm air slides out over the top of the sash, cold air drops in underneath, and the radiator under the window works all day replacing what you just gave to the neighborhood, while the room never gets properly fresh, because one inch of gap cannot swap a room's worth of air. And the walls near that window cool down, which matters more than it sounds, and rule two is why.

  

So. A tilted window is a slow leak with a handle on it. Close it, fully. Which raises the obvious question, because the room really was stuffy, and now you have a window you are not allowed to touch. The German answer is that you touch it harder, and for less time.

  

Rule number two. Ventilate by shock, not by trickle.

  

What a German household does instead of the tilt is this. Two or three times a day, the heat under that window gets turned down or off, and the window is opened all the way. Not tilted. Open, wide, and if you can open a window on the far side of the house at the same time, do that too, so the air has a way through. Five minutes in a hard wind, ten minutes on a still day. Then the windows close and the heat goes back on.

  

That does the whole trick. In five to ten minutes you swap the entire volume of air in the room. The stale air goes out, the humid air from cooking and showering and breathing goes out with it, and dry cold air comes in, and dry cold air is cheap to heat. The thing you did not do is cool the walls. Walls, floors, furniture, the bed, all of that holds heat for hours, and five minutes of open window barely touches it, so the room comes back to temperature fast, on stored heat you already paid for. The tilted window does the opposite, never changing the air and cooling the walls slowly all day. Same window. Two habits. One of them is a bill.

  

One caveat, said plainly. In an apartment with a gas water heater or an older gas furnace in the same room, do not switch the appliance off and on to do this. Turn down the thermostat, open the windows, and leave the appliance alone. If you ever smell gas near it, the window is the least of your problems, and you call the gas company.

  

Every rule from here follows the warm air deeper into the house, and rule three is the one where I ask you to make the bedroom colder on purpose, which is also where a small numbered knob costs less than you think.

Rule number three. Every room gets its own temperature, and the bedroom loses.

  

An American house, in my observation, tends to run at one number. The thermostat in the hall says 72, so the hall is 72, the bedroom is 72, and the closet nobody opens is doing its best to be 72. The German house runs by use. Living room, somewhere around 68 degrees, because you sit still in it. Kitchen, a little lower, because the stove does some of the work. Bedroom, 62 to 64, because you are under a blanket and you sleep better cool anyway. Hallways and spare rooms, lower still. Every degree you do not heat is a degree you do not pay for, and the bedroom is the easiest place to find four of them.

  

How you do that depends on the heat you have. With radiators, the German tool is a thermostatic valve, a small numbered knob on the side of each radiator that opens and closes on its own to hold that room at the number you set. They screw onto most modern radiator valves without a plumber. With forced air, a furnace and ducts and registers in the floor, you cannot give each room its own valve. What you can do is close the doors, run a programmable thermostat, and partly close the registers in rooms you do not use. Partly. Closing many registers on a forced-air system builds pressure in the ducts and can hurt the blower, so close a few, never all, and never the one nearest the furnace.

  

So. The bedroom is 63 and you are fine. The trouble is that warm air does not read thermostats, and the moment the living room is warmer than the hall, the living room starts leaking into the hall. Which is rule four, and it is a door.

  

Rule number four. Doors closed between heated and unheated rooms.

  

This one is pure physics, and it is the rule Americans break without noticing, because open doors feel friendly. Warm air is lighter than cold air. It rises, it spreads, and it moves toward cold the way water moves downhill. Leave the living room door open into a 58 degree hallway and the living room becomes a radiator for the hallway, the stairs, and the landing. The thermostat sees the living room dropping and asks the furnace for more, and the furnace obliges, and the money goes up the stairs to warm a space nobody is standing in.

  

The German habit is that the door is closed the moment the room is warm. Every door. Kitchen to hall, living room to hall, bedroom to hall. It feels unfriendly for about a week, and then it is just how the house works. If a door does not latch, or it has a half inch gap at the bottom, that is a repair, not a personality trait. A door sweep costs a few dollars and takes ten minutes. And if the reason you leave doors open is that the heat in one room has to reach another, then something in the system is not delivering, which is rule five, and it is the coldest radiator in the house.

  

Rule number five. Bleed the radiators every fall. Or change the filter every month.

  

Put your hand on a radiator that has been on for an hour. If the bottom is hot and the top third is cool, there is air trapped inside it. Water cannot fill the space the air is holding, so that part of the radiator does nothing except look like a radiator. The boiler works just as hard and you get two thirds of the heat. The fix is a radiator key, which costs about a dollar at the hardware store. With the heat off and the system cool, you open the little valve at the top corner a quarter turn, with a cloth held under it, and air hisses out. When water comes instead of air, you close it. That is the whole repair. Every fall, before the first cold night, each radiator gets bled, top floor first.

  

Two things I say flatly. Do this with the system off and cool, because the water in a hot system is hot enough to burn you, and the cloth is not decoration. And if the water that comes out is brown or black, or the radiator needs bleeding every week, that is not air anymore, that is the system, and you stop here and call someone.

  

For forced air the same rule wears a different coat. A dirty furnace filter chokes the air the same way trapped air chokes a radiator. The furnace runs longer to push the same heat through a clogged screen, and the rooms at the far end of the ducts get less than they should. Change the filter once a month in winter, and mark the arrow on the frame so it points the way the air flows, toward the furnace. It is a 10 dollar part, and it is the cheapest rule in this video to keep.

  

So. Five rules, and I want you to notice what they have in common, because it is not an accident. Every one of them was about air. The air leaving through a tilted window. The air you swap on purpose. The air you heat in a room nobody sits in. The air walking out an open door. The air trapped inside the radiator. You pay for heat once, at the meter, and the first five rules are about not sending it away after you paid. The next five are about walls, glass, the clock, and the machine itself, and the first one is standing right in front of your radiator.

  

Rule number six. Nothing in front of the heat.

  

A sofa against the radiator. A long curtain hanging over the register. A bookshelf pushed into the alcove where the baseboard heater lives. It looks tidy. It also means the radiator heats the back of the sofa, and the sofa, being a sofa, keeps it. A curtain over a floor register catches the warm air on its way up and sends it straight up the window glass, the coldest surface in the room. In a German living room the radiator is naked. Nothing within a hand's width in front of it, and the curtains ending above it, not hanging over it. The warm air needs a clear path up and out into the room, because it moves by rising, and anything in the way is a lid.

  

Two exceptions, and they are the same rule turned around. A shelf a foot above a radiator, with a gap, actually helps, because it pushes the rising air out into the room instead of straight up the wall. And a heavy curtain closed over a window at night, tucked behind the radiator and not in front of it, helps too, and that one is rule eight. First, the wall behind the radiator, and the 10 dollar fix I promised.

  

Rule number seven. Foil behind every radiator on an outside wall.

  

A radiator heats in every direction. Front, top, and back. The front and the top heat your room. The back heats the wall, and if that wall is an outside wall, one brick or one layer of siding away from January, the back of the radiator is heating the sky. In an older American house with thin walls, a fair share of every radiator's output goes straight out the back.

  

The German fix is a sheet of reflective foil, sometimes foil on a thin foam backing, cut to the size of the radiator and fixed to the wall behind it, shiny side facing the radiator. It sends the back heat forward into the room instead of into the brick. It costs about 10 dollars for a roll, it takes an afternoon for the whole house, and it works quietly for years. Attach it with double sided tape or a few small hooks, and leave a gap between the foil and the radiator so air can move. Do not wrap the radiator, do not cover the top, and do not use it on electric baseboard heaters, where anything near the element is a fire question. Radiators only, and outside walls only. Inside walls do not need it, because the room on the other side is yours.

  

So. The wall is turned around. The last hole in the room is the glass, and it is a big one.

  

Rule number eight. Close the shutters and the heavy curtains at night.

  

Window glass is the worst insulated surface in your house. A single pane loses heat something like ten times faster than the wall around it, and even a good double pane is several times worse than a wall. Which means that at night, when it is coldest, the window is where your heat leaves. Germans know this because German houses mostly have exterior roller shutters, metal or plastic, that come down every evening and go up every morning. It is a habit, like locking the door. Down at dusk, up at breakfast. The shutter puts a layer of still air against the glass, and still air is one of the best insulators there is.

  

You probably do not have shutters. What you have, or can have, is a heavy curtain, lined, long enough to reach the sill or the floor, and closed the moment it gets dark. A curtain is not a shutter, but it traps a layer of air against the glass, which is most of what the shutter does. Close them at dusk in every room you heat, and open them in the morning on the sunny side, because daylight through glass is heat you do not pay for. And remember rule six, the curtain goes behind the radiator, never over it. Blinds too, if you have them. Each layer is a little wall.

  

Now the house is closed up for the night and warm, and the question is what the thermostat does while you sleep, which is rule nine, and this one has a government number attached to it.

  

Rule number nine. Set the heat back at night.

  

The German house cools at night on purpose. The heating goes down around bedtime and comes up an hour before anyone gets out of bed. With a modern boiler that is a program on the controller. With radiators it is turning the valves down at bedtime. With forced air it is a programmable thermostat, which is the cheapest thing in this whole video that you may not already own.

  

The number people argue about is whether setting back actually saves anything, since the furnace has to catch up in the morning. It does save. The Department of Energy says that turning the thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees for eight hours a day takes up to 10 percent off the year's heating bill. On a 1,000 dollar winter that is about 100 dollars for pressing a button once in October. The catch-up in the morning costs less than eight hours of holding a house at 70 while everybody is asleep under a blanket, because heat loss follows the difference in temperature. Less difference, less loss. That is the whole argument, and it is the same argument as the bedroom in rule three, applied to the clock instead of the room.

  

So. That will hold. Nine rules, all of them things you do. The tenth is the one thing you pay for, and it is the one that stops the January phone call.

  

Rule number ten. Service the system every year, before the cold, not after.

  

The German word is Wartung, which means maintenance, and it is not optional in Germany the way it is here. For larger buildings German law now requires something called hydraulic balancing, a technician going through the whole heating system and adjusting every radiator so hot water reaches the far end of the building at the same rate it reaches the near end. It sounds fussy, and it saves real fuel, because without it the radiator nearest the boiler cooks and the one at the far end waits.

  

For an American furnace, the same rule is the fall tune-up. A technician comes in, usually for 80 to 150 dollars, cleans the burners, checks the heat exchanger for cracks, checks the flame sensor, tests the blower, and tells you what is wearing out. That is the visit. The other visit is the one on the coldest night in January, when the furnace stops and everything is an emergency rate, and a replacement runs 3,000 to 6,000 dollars. Same furnace. The 100 dollar visit and the 5,000 dollar visit are the same problem, seen early or seen late. This is what the Germans call Vorsorge, and there is no good English word for it, but it means looking ahead, and it means thrift, and the two are the same thing.

  

While the technician is in the basement, or while you are, one more thing. The hot water pipes that run through cold spaces, the basement, the crawl space, the garage wall. Foam pipe insulation, the gray tube with a slit down the side, costs about a dollar a foot, and you push it on by hand. Every foot of bare hot pipe in a cold basement is a radiator in a room nobody uses.

  

So. That is ten. And they are not ten things. It is one thing standing in ten rooms. The heat is paid for once, when the meter turns, and everything after that is you deciding whether to keep it or send it away. The tilted window, the open door, the bare pipe, the bare wall, the naked glass at midnight, the sofa on the radiator, the trapped air, the room nobody sits in, all of them send it away. And the furnace, tuned or not, is only the thing that makes it. Nothing in the ten makes more heat. Every rule keeps it.

  

This week, three things, no more. Close every tilted window in the house and open one wide for ten minutes instead. Put a thermometer in the bedroom and see what number it actually says. And make the phone call for the tune-up now, before the first cold night, while nobody is in a hurry.

How does a drain that a professional just cleared fill up again in a few months? Start at the top. Number one sits just under the cover, at the crossbars.

  

Take the cover off a lot of American shower drains and you will find two thin metal bars crossing the opening, or a small grid. Every hair that goes down has to pass those bars, and a hair is rougher than it looks. \[matter-of-fact\] Under a microscope it is covered in tiny overlapping scales, like the shingles on a roof, so one hair touching a bar catches, and the next hair catches on that one. A few weeks of showers, and there is a net hanging from the bars.

  

Then the net starts catching everything else. Soap scum, the gray, waxy film bar soap leaves behind when it meets the minerals in tap water. Body oil, which coats every hair that leaves your head. \[slower\] And a fourth thing, which nobody mentions on any bottle. Biofilm. That is a colony of bacteria living on the soap and the oil and building itself a slime to live in, and you have seen the edge of it, that gray or pinkish ring around the drain that comes back however often you wipe it.

  

All four mat together on the crossbars, and the loose pieces travel further down and settle where the pipe bends. That is your rope. And because it builds slowly, a shower drain gives plenty of warning before it quits. The water stays around your feet a little longer each week, there is a sour smell from the drain on warm days, and the ring at the edge comes back faster than it used to. \[thoughtful\] The house tells you, in water and in smell, long before it stops.

  

And now there is an argument to settle, because you may have heard that hair has nothing to do with any of this.

  

Number two goes a little deeper, into the rope itself. It is not the hair, people will tell you, it is the slime. And they are right. The biofilm is what turns a few loose hairs into a plug. But it is also the hair, and both being true at the same time decides what you do about it.

  

\[warm\] Think of an old wall made of mud and straw. Mud on its own cracks and washes away in the rain, and straw on its own blows off in the wind. Mix them, and you have a wall that stands for generations. In your drain, the biofilm is the mud, so it is the glue, and the hair is the straw, so it is the mass. The glue alone would lay a thin coat on the inside of the pipe, and water would get past it for a long time. The hair alone would make a loose tangle that water could mostly push through. \[emphatic\] Together they make a felt, and water does not push through felt.

  

So. Two halves, and two answers, because the glue has to be dissolved and the mass has to be stopped. And the order is the part worth remembering. If the hair never reaches the pipe, the glue has nothing to hold, and it stays a thin film that the monthly tablespoon in number six keeps in check. \[slower\] The sieve takes the mass out before the glue has a chance to set. That is the whole thinking behind the German drain. It is also why a sieve on its own cannot rescue a drain that already has a rope in it. In that drain, the glue set long ago.

  

But the rope is only half the trouble. The other half is where it lives.

  

Number three is at the bottom of your reach, where the pipe bends. The trap. Every sink and shower drain in your house has a bend beneath it shaped like a U, usually called a P-trap. It holds a little water at the bottom of the bend all the time, and that water is a seal that keeps the air from the sewer out of your bathroom.

  

\[matter-of-fact\] Under your bathroom sink, you can see that bend. It is held on by two nuts you can often loosen by hand, the bend drops into a bucket, and you clean it out. Unpleasant, but done.

  

Under your shower, the same bend sits below the floor. It is sealed into the drain line beneath the shower pan and the tile, and in an upstairs bathroom it may be hanging just above the ceiling of the room below. There are no nuts on it, and usually no little door to reach it through. \[quietly\] You cannot take it off, and you cannot even see it.

  

So what settles in that bend tends to stay there until something long and metal is pushed through it. That is the job of the plumber's snake. It bores a path through the plug, the water runs, and the plumber packs up. But a snake does not always bring up what is stuck to the walls of the pipe, and the next rope starts on whatever is left. That is your few months, and that is why the visit keeps coming back.

  

For a shower, then, prevention is the whole game. There is a German word for it, Vorsorge, which means care taken in advance. It is plain arithmetic. Care taken in advance costs almost nothing, and care taken afterward costs a visit.

Number four starts back at the top, with the cover. Some lift straight out, some twist, and a lot of them are held by one or two screws. \[slightly amused\] The screws go into a cup the moment they are out, and not onto the shower floor, which is wet, and slopes toward exactly one hole.

  

Gloves on. And one warning before your hands go near that opening. If a chemical drain cleaner went down this drain in the last few days, the water sitting in it may still be carrying some. In that case you want eye protection as well as gloves, and you work slowly, with no splashing.

  

\[quietly\] Now look, before anything else. Very often the start of the rope is right there, wrapped around the crossbars, and you can pull it free with your fingers. Pull slowly and it tends to come out in one long piece. It goes into a plastic bag, and not back down the drain or into the toilet, where a rope of hair can start a second problem.

  

Then the zip tool. It is a strip of plastic about as long as your forearm, with small barbs along both edges. \[matter-of-fact\] Around three dollars it costs, at a hardware store. Feed it down the drain, between the crossbars if they are in the way, as far as it will go without forcing. Give it a little twist, then pull it up slowly. Number five runs the whole length, from the cover down through the bend. A bucket of hot water, as hot as your tap will give, and never boiling water from the kettle, because boiling water is hard on the plastic joints in a drain line. Into the bucket goes a good squirt of dish soap. \[matter-of-fact\] Dish soap is made to lift grease off a frying pan, and in a drain it loosens the oily film that holds the last scraps of the rope to the pipe.

  

Pour the whole bucket in one go, low and steady, so it does not splash. A trickle only wets the bottom of the pipe, but a full bucket fills it, and the weight of all that water pushes through the bend and carries the loose pieces along. Then watch, and if the water drops away quickly, good. That is a drain doing its one job.

  

Now the bottle. Under a lot of sinks there is a drain cleaner made with lye, and you can check, because the back of the bottle will say sodium hydroxide. \[emphatic\] For this drain, it stays on the shelf. In a slow shower drain the chemical sits in the bend instead of washing through, right on the one part you cannot reach and cannot replace without opening the floor, and that is hard on the trap. And if it fails to clear the clog, somebody has to reach down into water with lye in it to finish the job. Usually that somebody is you.

  

The bottle is sold as the fast answer. \[thoughtful\] There is a slower one, older and duller, and it is a spoonful of white powder once a month.

  

Number six works on every inch of the pipe at once, and it works on the glue. Washing soda. You find it in the laundry aisle, in a box, sold for washing clothes. Be careful there, because baking soda is often sold nearby in a box that looks much the same. Washing soda is a good deal stronger, and it is the one you want.

  

Once a month, one tablespoon, stirred into hot water until it disappears, then poured down the drain. That is all it takes. It goes after the biofilm and breaks it down, so the glue has a hard time getting thick enough to hold hair together. \[slightly amused\] One tablespoon, and please do not decide that two must be better.

  

Gloves for this one as well. Washing soda is a strong cleaner and hard on skin, and you keep it away from your eyes. The box stays closed and dry, and out of reach of children and pets.

  

Where that box lives decides whether any of this happens. Put it in the cupboard under the sink and in a month you will have forgotten it exists. Put it on a high shelf in the bathroom, where you see it every morning, and write the date on the lid each time you use it. A box, a spoon, and a date in pencil. That is the whole system for the glue.

Number seven sits at the very top, in the opening, where your fingers are. In a German floor-level shower, the kind with no step, where the tiles slope down to a drain set into the floor, the drain comes with a small insert as standard. In German it is a Haarsieb. \[matter-of-fact\] The American version is a separate purchase. Before you buy, measure across the drain opening, inside edge to inside edge, and buy for that number, so it sits snug and nothing slides past the side.

  

Now the habit, the one I said decides whether any of this works. \[emphatic\] A strainer nobody empties becomes the clog.

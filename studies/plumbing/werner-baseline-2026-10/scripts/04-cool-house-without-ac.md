Right now, the sun is on one side of your house. It has been on that side since about ten this morning. And somewhere inside, a machine is running to undo what the sun did, and you pay that machine by the hour.

  

In Germany, only a few homes out of every hundred have air conditioning. A few out of every hundred, across the whole country. And German summers are not gentle anymore. They get hot enough that you would reach for the switch. So how does a whole country sit through July without the machine?

  

I am Werner Finkbeiner. This channel is for the American homeowner and the American renter, the one whose house has started talking back and who would rather understand it than pay someone to. Today, the German house in summer. Six things it does, in the order the day asks for them, and then five things you can buy in America that do the same job with the same physics. Two of the six cost nothing at all. One of them is the right thing done at the wrong hour, and I will tell you the hour. And one of them you cannot buy, because it is the wall itself.

  

First, the problem, said plainly. The American house cools with a machine. The German house cools with a schedule. That is the whole difference, and everything else today is detail. The machine fights heat after it is inside. The schedule stops the heat before it arrives, and then, once it is dark, throws the doors open and lets the night do the work. Same heat. Same sun. Two solutions to one piece of physics, and the German one is older, duller, and cheaper, which is usually how it goes.

  

So. Thing one. The shutter.

  

Stand outside a German house, any German house, on a hot afternoon, and the windows are not there. What you see instead is a flat grey or white slatted panel where the glass should be, rolled down from a box above the frame. This is the Rollladen, the exterior roller shutter, and it is fitted to nearly every window in the country, new build or old. It is a part of the window the way a sill is a part of the window, and nobody there thinks of it as a luxury.

  

Here is the piece of information nobody gave you. An exterior shutter and an interior blind look like the same idea. They are not the same idea, and the difference is the entire video.

  

Sunlight comes through glass as light. It hits your floor, your sofa, your wall, and the moment it lands on something, it turns into heat. Heat inside the room. A blind on the inside of the glass catches that light after it has already come through. The blind gets warm. The blind is in the room. The room gets warm. You have caught the problem after it walked through the door.

  

An exterior shutter catches the light before the glass. The slats warm up out there, in the air, on the outside of the house, and the air carries that warmth away. It never becomes room heat because it never reaches the room. Shading on the outside blocks most of the solar heat. Shading on the inside stops only a fraction of it, because the fraction it does not stop is already yours.

  

So. Same sun, same window, same afternoon. Outside the glass it is weather. Once it is inside the glass, you pay for it.

  

That is thing one, and it costs whatever a shutter costs, which we get to in the American half. Thing two costs nothing. It is a time of day, and if you get it wrong, thing one stops working.

Thing two. The hour.

  

Ten in the morning. That is the number. In a German house in summer, somewhere between nine and ten, before the sun has any real weight in it, the shutters come down on the sunny side and the windows behind them close. All of them. The house goes dark and quiet, like a shop at lunch, and it stays that way until evening.

  

This is the part the American house gets backwards, and not because anybody is foolish. Nobody taught it. The American instinct on a hot morning is to open the windows and let some air in, and that is exactly the wrong hour, because the air outside at ten in the morning is already warmer than the air inside, and it is getting warmer by the minute. Every open window at that hour is a pipe pouring warm air into a cool room, and the room fills the way a sink fills.

  

So. Ten o'clock, shutters down, windows shut, and then you leave the house alone. It is holding cool air from last night. Your one job is to not let it out. The German word for this whole posture is Vorsorge, which means taking care of a thing before it needs care, and in summer it means closing the house before the house gets hot, not after.

  

Thing three. The night.

  

Here is where the day pays you back. Once the sun is down and the outside air has dropped below the inside air, and in most places that is late evening, sometimes not until ten or eleven at night, the house opens. Not one window. Windows on opposite sides.

  

German windows are tilt and turn. One handle, two positions. Turn it one way and the window swings open like a door. Turn it the other way and the top tilts inward a few inches, so the window is open at the top and locked at the bottom, and you can leave it that way all night with nobody climbing through. On a summer night, one window tilts open on the cool side of the house and one swings open on the far side, and the air moves through the whole floor in a line, pulling the day's heat out with it and leaving the cooler night air behind. That is cross-ventilation, and it works because you gave it a way in and a way out. One open window is a dead end. Two open windows, facing each other, is a corridor.

  

Then, in the morning, before ten, you close it all again and the cycle starts over. Open at night, closed by day. The house breathes once every twenty-four hours, in at night, out in the morning, and never at noon.

  

Now stop for a moment, because there is a pattern in the first three, and I want you to see it before we go on. The shutter, the hour, the night. None of them fights heat. Not one. Every one of them either keeps heat from arriving or lets it leave on its own. The German house does not cool itself. It refuses to warm up, and then it airs out. That is the whole method, and the last three things are just that same idea standing in three more places.

  

Thing four. The wall.

  

You cannot buy this one, and I am telling you about it anyway, because it explains why the first three work as well as they do over there.

  

A German house is heavy. Brick, block, concrete, plaster over masonry. That is simply how the country builds, and it has been for a very long time. And a heavy wall is slow. Sun hits the outside of a masonry wall at noon and the heat does not reach the inside face until evening, if it reaches it at all, because the wall soaks it up the way a stone in the sun soaks it up. The wall takes the heat by day and holds it. And then at night, when the windows are open and the cool air is moving through, the wall gives that heat back to the air, and the air carries it out the window.

  

The house is a thermos. Slow to warm, slow to cool, and if you manage the lid, meaning the shutters and the windows, the inside stays where you put it.

  

An American frame house, wood studs, drywall, insulation in between, is light. It warms fast and it cools fast. That is a different animal, and it means the shutter and the hour matter more for you. A heavy house forgives a window left open until eleven. A light house does not. So when we get to the American half, understand that the blocking has to be better, because the walls are not helping you the way they help a house in Freiburg.

  

Thing five. The awning.

  

The south side of a German house usually has a terrace, and the terrace usually has an awning, a wide cloth roof that cranks out from the wall in the morning and cranks back at dusk. It shades the terrace, yes, but its real job is to shade the wall and the big glass doors behind it. Same principle as the shutter. Stop the sun before it touches the house. A patio umbrella does a little of this. An awning does the whole wall.

  

Thing six. The stopgap.

  

And now the honest one, because I am not going to tell you the German house never gets hot. In a real heatwave, a week of it, the wall fills up. It has soaked all day and the nights are not cool enough to empty it, and by day four the inside is warm no matter what the shutters do. So what does the German household do then? It buys, or borrows, a portable air conditioner. The kind on wheels with a hose to the window. It runs it in one room, the bedroom usually, for the worst week, and then it goes back in the cellar until next year.

  

That is the stopgap, and I say it plainly because it matters. So the German answer includes a machine after all, a small one, in one room, for one bad week, after everything else has been done. The order is the thing. Block, seal, ventilate, and then, last, if the summer wins, a little cooling where you sleep.

  

So. Six things. Now the American half, and the rule for it fits in one sentence. Same order of operations. Block first, ventilate at night, and only then cool. Every item below is a way to do one of the six with what is sold in the United States.

  

One. The exterior roller shutter, because you can buy the real thing. They are sold in the US, and the price runs roughly 300 to 800 dollars per window installed. Less if you buy them as kits and mount them yourself, which is a Saturday with a drill and a level. Start with the windows that face south and west, the afternoon side, because that is where the heat comes in. You do not need them on every window. You need them on the ones the sun hits after lunch.

  

Two. Exterior solar screens, and this is the one I would send you to first. A solar screen is a dark mesh, tighter than an insect screen, mounted on the outside of the window in the same kind of frame. It does not roll up and it does not black out the room. It sits there all summer and it stops most of the heat that an interior blind lets through, because, again, it catches the light outside the glass. Roughly 50 to 150 dollars a window. You can order them cut to size and clip them on yourself. Renters, listen, this is your item, because it goes on and comes off without a mark.

  

Three. Reflective window film. If you cannot put anything on the outside, an apartment with rules, a window on the fourth floor, this is the fallback. A film that sticks to the inside of the glass and bounces a share of the light back out before it lands on your floor. It is not as good as outside shading, because some heat still stops in the glass itself, and the glass is in the room. But it is better than a curtain, it is 30 to 60 dollars a window, and it takes an afternoon and a spray bottle.

  

Four. The whole-house fan, and this is your German night. An American house usually does not have windows that tilt, and it usually does not have the same floor plan on both sides, so cross-ventilation by hand can be clumsy. The whole-house fan solves that. It is a large fan mounted in the ceiling of the top floor, pulling air from the house up into the attic and out through the attic vents. You open a few windows at night, switch it on, and it drags the cool night air in through every open window at once and pushes the day's heat out through the roof. Leave it running a while and the house is flushed. Installed, roughly 1,000 to 2,000 dollars, and it uses a fraction of the electricity of central air, because it only moves air, and moving air is cheap. Morning comes, fan off, windows shut, shutters or screens doing their work. Same cycle as Freiburg, with a motor doing the corridor.

  

Five. The roof, because the sun does not only come in through the windows. It sits on your roof all day, and a dark roof with a sealed attic turns that into an oven above your ceiling. Two fixes. Attic ventilation, meaning a ridge vent or gable vents so the hot air up there has somewhere to go, and a light-colored roof when the roof is next replaced, because a pale surface reflects what a dark one absorbs. Neither is a summer project. Both cut the load from above for every summer after.

  

And now the numbers, so you can see the shape of it. Central air, in a hot summer, runs about 100 to 300 dollars a month to operate, depending on the house and the state. A window unit, 30 to 80 dollars a month. A set of shutters, a set of screens, a whole-house fan, once they are on the house, cost almost nothing to run, because nothing in them makes cold. They only stop heat, or move air. So the real question is how much of the summer you ask the machine to fight, and every one of the five things above shortens that fight.

  

Now, the part I want you to see. There were six things, and then five things, and if you count them you get eleven, but it was one idea standing in eleven places, and the idea is this. Heat is easiest to deal with where it is not yet, and cheapest to remove when it wants to leave on its own. Everything else is a machine arguing with the sun at noon, and that is an expensive argument to have every afternoon.

  

So, this week, three things, no more. Walk outside at three in the afternoon and look at your own house from the street and find the windows that are lit, because those are the ones that need something on the outside. Tonight, when the air outside is cooler than the air inside, open two windows on opposite sides of the house and feel whether anything moves. And tomorrow morning, before ten, close everything, and notice how long the house holds.

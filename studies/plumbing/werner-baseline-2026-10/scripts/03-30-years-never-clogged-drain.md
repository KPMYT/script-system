Under your kitchen sink there is a bend in the pipe, and inside that bend, right now, there is a layer growing. It is about as thick as a fingernail, it is cool to the touch, and it is made entirely out of things you poured down there last week without thinking about it once. It has been thickening since the day you moved in.

On the day it finally closes, you will make a phone call, and somebody will come with a machine, and they will be inside your house for 20 minutes, and the invoice will say somewhere between 150 and 350 dollars. If the line has gone far enough that the machine alone is not enough, that number roughly doubles again.

I have never had a clogged drain. Not in any kitchen, not in any bathroom, not once.

There is no skill in it. There are nine habits, and every single one of them is smaller than the invoice.

One of them costs 6 dollars and sits in a hole you already have in your house. One takes 5 minutes, twice a year, with a bucket on the floor. And one of them is a white powder from the laundry aisle that is not the white powder you are thinking of, and the confusion between those two is the entire reason your baking soda and vinegar did nothing.

Werner Finkbeiner. This is for people living in older houses and older apartments, the ones that have started making noises, who would rather spend 20 minutes than a few hundred dollars. Nine habits, in the order I would start them.

So. Before the first one, the thing nobody explains, which is what a clog actually is.

A clog is not an object that arrives one day. It is a crop. Three materials grow it, and they grow it in a particular order.

Fat is first. Fat leaves your pan warm, and it is still a liquid for the first few feet of pipe, and then the pipe gets cold, because the pipe is under your floor and your floor is not heated. So the fat stops being a liquid. It lines the inside of the pipe the way wax lines a candle mold, a little thicker every week.

Hair is second. Hair on its own clogs nothing. Hair in an open pipe goes straight through and out. Hair only stops when there is something for it to catch on, and what it catches on is the fat.

The third one is alive. A film of bacteria grows on the fat, and that film is where the smell comes from. If your kitchen drain smells but the water still runs, you are smelling that film, and the film is the mortar. It binds the hair and the fat into one solid object.

So. Three materials, one structure. Almost every habit after this stops one of those materials before it is in the pipe at all, and that is the whole method. What is in the pipe is difficult. What is still in your hand is not.

Number one. Nothing that is fat goes into the drain. Not ever, not a small amount, not with hot water chasing it.

I will be specific, because nothing fatty is the kind of instruction a person agrees with and then ignores at the sink. Bacon grease, obviously. But also the film left in the pan after ground beef, the oil in the tuna can, butter in the pot, dressing in the bottom of the salad bowl, cream in the last inch of a coffee cup, the soup nobody finished. All of that is fat, and all of it does the same thing when it reaches the cold part of the line.

The habit has two parts and together they take about 15 seconds.

First, a jar. Any jar with a lid, kept under the sink or beside the stove. Warm grease goes into the jar. When the jar is full, the lid goes on and the jar goes in the bin, and a new one takes its place. You are not saving it for anything. You are only letting it turn solid somewhere that is not your plumbing.

Second, the paper. Before the pan goes anywhere near water, wipe it. A paper towel, an old newspaper, the napkin already sitting on the table. Wipe until the pan is dull instead of shiny, put the paper in the bin, then wash it.

That is the whole habit.

The mistake people make here is hot water. The belief is that hot water and enough soap send the fat away. What really happens is that hot water carries the fat another 6 feet down the line, to a colder stretch of pipe, where it hardens somewhere you cannot reach with anything you own. You did not get rid of it. You moved it to a worse address.

Wipe first, water afterward. That order, always.

Number two. A strainer in every drain in the house. Every sink, every shower, every tub.

In Germany nobody has to be taught this one, because the fitting arrives with it. You buy a sink, there is a basket sitting in the drain. It is simply part of the object. Here they are sold separately, on a hook in an aisle, which means a great many houses never get one. Nobody decided that. It just did not come in the box.

They cost between 6 and 15 dollars. Fine mesh for the kitchen, because the kitchen produces particles. A dome or a hair catcher for the shower, because the shower produces hair, and the dome sits above the opening so the hair wraps around the outside where your fingers can reach it.

Stainless steel if you can. The plastic ones warp after a while, and a warped strainer sits crooked, and things go past it around the edge. A strainer that things get past is a decoration.

Measure the drain opening before you buy, and I know that sounds like fussiness. It is not fussiness. A strainer slightly too small drops down inside the hole at an angle and lets everything in around the rim, and one slightly too large sits on top and gets knocked sideways every time you set a pot down.

So. 6 dollars, one time, into a hole you already have. Of the nine things on this list this one has the best ratio by a wide distance, and it is also the one most houses do not have.

Number three is the one people resist hardest, because it means getting on the floor and taking a piece of your own plumbing apart with your hands, and I want to show you why that is the least frightening thing in this entire video. Before I do, one small thing.

Number three. Twice a year, you open the trap and you clean it out by hand.

The trap is the U shaped bend under your sink. Its job is to hold a small amount of water permanently, and that standing water is a plug that keeps sewer gas out of your kitchen. It is a good piece of engineering. It is also, by design, the lowest point in the line, which means it is the place where everything settles.

In Germany most sinks use a bottle trap. It is a cylinder, and the bottom of that cylinder unscrews by hand. No tool at all. You put a bucket underneath, you turn it counterclockwise, the bottom comes off in your hand, and whatever has been living in there comes out into the bucket.

American sinks use a P trap instead, and people look at it and assume it is a different category of object. It opens the same way. Two slip nuts, one at each end of the bend, and slip nuts are designed to be turned by hand. If they have been sitting a long time they may want pliers for the first quarter turn, and if you use pliers, wrap a rag around the nut first so you do not chew up the plastic.

Bucket underneath before anything else. Always before. There is water in there and it is coming out.

Then loosen both nuts, drop the bend into the bucket, take it to another sink or outside, and clean it with hot water and an old toothbrush or a bottle brush. What comes out is grey and does not look like anything you recognize. That is the three materials from earlier, combined.

Put it back hand tight, plus a very small amount more, and no further than that. Overtightening a slip nut cracks the plastic, and then you have a leak where you did not have one before.

Then run the tap and lay a dry paper towel under both joints for a minute. A dry paper towel is the best leak detector there is, because it shows you a single drop that you would never see on a wet pipe.

5 minutes. Spring and autumn is enough, and I use those two because they are times I am already doing other things, and a habit attached to another habit is a habit that survives.

And here is the part that matters. Do it on that schedule and you will never meet a clog, because you are taking it out while it is still soft. A clog is only frightening when you meet it after it has become solid. You are choosing to meet it early, when it is still just material in a bucket.

Number four. Once a month, one tablespoon of washing soda down each drain, followed by a kettle of hot water.

Now, here I have to be careful with you, because there are two white powders and a great many people reach for the wrong one.

Washing soda is sodium carbonate. It lives in the laundry aisle. In German it is called Soda, only Soda.

Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate. Different chemical, different job. In German that one is called Natron.

So you can see the trap in the naming. A German reads soda on an American shelf and picks up the baking box. An American reads a German recipe that says Soda and buys bicarbonate. Two different substances, one word between them, and a lot of people wondering afterward why nothing happened.

Here is what each one actually does inside a pipe.

Baking soda and vinegar is the famous one. It fizzes, and it fizzes impressively, and the fizz is carbon dioxide, which is a gas, and gas does nothing whatsoever to hardened fat. You have made a small volcano in your sink. The grease has not moved. Then the two chemicals neutralize each other and you are left with salt water sitting in your trap.

Washing soda is a different animal. It is strongly alkaline, and when a strong alkali meets fat, the fat converts into soap. That is not a figure of speech. That is the reaction soap itself has been made with for a few thousand years, and it is called saponification. Soap dissolves in water. Fat does not. So you are not scrubbing grease off a pipe wall you cannot see. You are turning it into something the water carries away on its own.

One tablespoon per drain, then the kettle, and give it 15 minutes before anybody uses that sink.

And the kettle is where the next rule begins, so I will hand it to you now, because it is the mistake that turns a good habit into a repair bill.

Number five. Hot water, yes. Boiling water, no.

The internet is fond of boiling water for drains, and on a cast iron line in a house built in 1910 that was reasonable advice. On what is actually under your sink today, it is not.

Most drain lines in American houses are PVC, the white plastic, or ABS, the black. PVC begins to soften somewhere above 140 degrees Fahrenheit. Boiling water is 212. That is not a narrow margin. That is 70 degrees past the point where the material starts to give, and where it gives first is the joint, because the joint is glued.

A softened joint does not burst in front of you while you watch. That would be easier. It relaxes, and then it seeps, slowly, into the cabinet floor or the ceiling underneath, and you find it a month later as a stain.

Porcelain has the same problem from the other direction. A toilet bowl or a sink basin is cold. Pour boiling water into cold porcelain and the inside face expands while the outside face does not, and porcelain will not stretch. Often nothing happens. Sometimes you get a hairline crack, and a hairline crack in a toilet is a new toilet.

So, hot from the tap. As hot as your tap gives you, which is usually 120 to 140 and sits under the line by design. If you are using a kettle, boil it and let it stand a few minutes, or cut it with cold water.

The heat was never the active ingredient anyway. The washing soda is. Hot water is only the vehicle that carries it down and gives it contact time. It does not need to be violent. It needs to be warm, and there needs to be enough of it.

So. Five of the nine. Stop here for a moment, because the first five have something in common and I want you to see it before we do the last four.

Look at where each one happens.

The jar happens at the stove. The paper happens at the pan. The strainer sits in the drain opening, at the very top of the line. The trap is the first bend, and the washing soda washes that same short stretch once a month. The hot water rule is about not damaging the little piece you can actually reach.

Not one of those five happens deep inside your plumbing.

That is the method rather than a coincidence. Everything past the trap belongs to a professional with a machine, and it should. Everything before the trap belongs to you, and it costs almost nothing. The whole difference between the household that phones and the household that does not is a few feet of pipe and who is paying attention to them.

And the arithmetic, since you should have it in front of you. A drain service call is 150 to 350 dollars. If the line needs hydro jetting, 350 to 600. Against that, a box of washing soda is about 5 dollars and lasts a year, the strainer is bought one time and never again, the jar came out of your recycling, and the paper towel is already in your kitchen. The routine runs under 10 dollars a year.

Four left. Two of them are about things you stop doing, one is 10 seconds long, and the last one is not a drain at all.

Number six. No chemical drain opener. Not the lye kind, not the acid kind, not the gel, not the foaming one.

There are two families in that aisle. Sodium hydroxide, which is lye, and sulfuric acid, which is what the professional strength bottles usually are. They work by chemical attack, which sounds efficient until you ask what exactly is being attacked.

They generate heat, and a serious amount of it. That is the softening problem from a moment ago, except now it is happening inside the pipe, against a glued joint, and it is being held there by the clog itself.

They corrode metal. If any part of your line is old galvanized steel or cast iron, and in an older house some of it will be, you are thinning a pipe that is already thin.

And here is the genuinely dangerous part, the one I want you to remember even if you forget everything else in this video.

When the chemical fails to clear the clog, it does not go anywhere. It sits in the trap. A trap full of lye or acid, held in place, waiting. And then the person gives up on the bottle and decides to open the trap by hand, exactly the way I described in number three, and they fetch a bucket, and they loosen the nut.

Never open a trap that has had chemicals poured into it. If you have already poured, that line is a professional's problem now, and tell them what you used, by name, when they arrive. That is not me being delicate with you. That is a real injury to the eyes and the skin, and the whole point of doing your own work is that you get to choose which jobs are yours.

Here I stop and you should also. The mechanical tools do the identical job without any of that: the plunger, the hand auger, the trap in your hands over a bucket. Slower, duller, and you keep your face.

Number seven. No wipes. Any wipes. Including the ones with flushable printed on the package.

Flushable on that package means it goes down. It means the wipe leaves your toilet successfully. It is a claim about the first 6 seconds and nothing after them.

Toilet paper is engineered to fall apart. Put it in a glass of water, shake the glass, and in under a minute it is pulp. That is the entire design brief of toilet paper. A wipe is built with a fabric structure so that it can be pulled out of a packet wet without tearing, and that structure does not break down, not in your line and not in the pipe under the street.

What happens is the wipe reaches the first place where your line is already narrowed, which is your grease, and it stops there, whole, and the next one catches on it. Municipal crews pull them out in ropes.

Baby wipes, cleaning wipes, cosmetic wipes, disinfecting wipes, all the same. A small bin with a lid beside the toilet solves it permanently and costs nothing after the first week of remembering.

And while we are standing here: dental floss, cotton swabs, the paper wrapper off a straw, and coffee grounds. Coffee grounds especially. People pour them down because they are small and they look like sand, and sand is exactly the problem. They settle in the bend and they pack together, and packed grounds are one of the things that come out of a trap most often.

Number eight. Hair comes out of the strainer weekly. Not when you happen to notice it. Weekly.

This is the smallest habit on the list and it is the one people skip, and they skip it because it is slightly unpleasant.

So make it less unpleasant. A square of toilet paper, pick the hair up through the paper, into the bin. 10 seconds. Attach it to something you already do every week so you never have to remember it as a task of its own. When the towels go in the wash. When the bathroom bin gets emptied. A habit standing on its own is a habit that dies.

And understand why weekly is the word, because it is not fussiness either.

Hair sitting on top of a strainer is a nuisance. Hair that has gone below the strainer is a structure. The moment it is past the opening it lies down in the bend and it starts collecting, and every shower after that adds soap scum, which is the calcium in your water bound up with soap, and soap scum on hair makes a mat. Once it is a mat it will not come out with water at any temperature. It comes out with a hand auger, or with the trap in a bucket, or with a plumber.

The strainer only works while it is empty. A full strainer catches nothing. It is a lid.

Number nine, and this one is not a drain.

Twice a year you find your main water shutoff valve, and you turn it all the way off, and then all the way back on.

I put it last because it belongs to the same idea as everything before it, and because out of the nine things on this list it is the one that prevents the largest single number.

Here is why a valve needs exercising. A valve that never moves seizes. If it is an older gate valve, the type with a round wheel, the stem corrodes in position and the packing dries out, and the valve quietly becomes an ornament on a pipe. The failure mode is not a leak. It is that on the day you need it, the thing will not turn, or it turns and shears off in your hand.

And the day you need it is the day a washing machine hose splits, or a supply line under a sink lets go, or a water heater opens up at the seam. Those do not drip. They deliver water at full pressure, continuously, until somebody closes that valve. The distance between a wet floor and a destroyed ceiling gets measured in how many minutes it takes to shut it off, and most of those minutes get spent looking for it.

So find it now, while nothing is wrong. Basement, crawl space, utility room, usually near where the line comes into the house, often close to the water meter. In warm climates it may be outside at ground level in a box.

Turn it off. Go to a tap and confirm the water actually stops, because sometimes what you found was not the main. Turn it back on slowly, because opening it fast sends a pressure surge through the whole house. If it will not turn by hand, or if it weeps at the stem when you move it, that is a plumber, and it is a cheap visit set against the one you are preventing.

Then tell whoever else lives in the house where it is. A valve that only one person can find is half a valve.

So. Nine habits.

And now the thing I actually wanted to tell you, which is that they were never nine.

Look at them again. A jar beside the stove. A basket in a hole. A bend of pipe in a bucket in the spring. A spoonful of powder once a month. Water that is hot instead of boiling. A bottle you do not buy. A bin next to the toilet. 10 seconds in the shower on a Sunday. A valve that gets turned so that it can still turn.

Different rooms, different objects, one idea.

The idea has a name in German, and the name is Vorsorge. Care taken in advance. Not repair, and duller than caution. It means the work happens on a schedule instead of in an emergency, and the reason it happens on a schedule is that the schedule is cheap and the emergency is not.

That is the piece that does not travel across an ocean well, because it is not knowledge. Every one of these nine is obvious the moment somebody says it out loud. None of it is technical. It is only that somebody has to tell you the check exists, and put it in your year, and then it becomes something the house simply gets, rather than something you decide about at 11 at night with water spreading across a floor.

The invoice was never for the work. The work is 5 minutes and a bucket. The invoice was for the fact that nobody ever put it in your calendar.

So. Three things this week, not nine.

One. Put a jar under the sink tonight. Tonight, not Saturday.

Two. Measure your kitchen drain and your shower drain, and order the strainers. It is the cheapest decision in this video and the one that does the most work.

Three. Go and find your main shutoff, and turn it. Once. If it turns easily, you have learned something good. If it does not, you have learned something much more useful than that.

The trap and the washing soda can wait for the weekend. Those two are a calendar entry rather than a task.

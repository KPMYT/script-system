Go and look at your kettle. Not the outside. Tip it toward the window and look at the floor of it. That white, rough skin, the one you decided some time ago is simply what the inside of a kettle looks like. That is not the kettle. That is your water, left behind one boil at a time, and it now sits between the heating plate and the water you are trying to warm. So. Every morning your kettle heats a layer of rock first, and you second. And the kettle is the small one. The same rock is in your showerhead, which is why three of the holes spray sideways. It is under the rim of your toilet. It is inside your dishwasher, and it is inside a water heater on your wall that costs fifteen hundred dollars to replace when the rock finally wins.

  

In a German kitchen, this rock has a short name. Kalk. And the thing that takes Kalk off is a white powder in a paper bag, on the bottom shelf, beside the baking soda, for about the price of a coffee. It goes into ten different places in the house, and in every one of them it goes in the same way. Today I show you all ten. The showerhead, which spends the night in a plastic bag. The toilet, where there is one warning you need before you pour. And the water heater, which is the one where I will tell you to stop.

  

I am Werner Finkbeiner. This channel is for the American house that has started to talk back, and for the German household habit that would have kept it quiet for almost nothing. Ten places, one powder, and what each one costs if you leave the rock where it is.

  

The powder first, because everything else follows from it. It is citric acid. Food-grade citric acid, the same acid that is in a lemon, only dry, white, and concentrated. In Germany it sits in every supermarket and every drugstore, two to three euros for a bag of two hundred fifty to five hundred grams, and nobody thinks of it as a cleaning product any more than they think of salt as one. In the United States it is sold as food-grade citric acid, in the canning aisle where the pickling jars are, or online, eight to ten dollars for a pound. A pound is a lot of powder. A tablespoon does a kettle. So the arithmetic comes to pennies per use, and a year of the habit is about eight dollars for the whole house. Write that number down, because we will set it beside some other numbers later and the gap will speak for itself.

  

So. Why this and not vinegar, which your grandmother used and which you can smell from the hallway. Kalk is calcium carbonate. It is the same material as a seashell, as chalk, as the stone in a cave. Vinegar dissolves it, slowly, and leaves the kitchen smelling like a salad for a day. Citric acid dissolves calcium carbonate several times faster, and it turns the rock into calcium citrate, which dissolves in water, so it rinses away instead of settling back down as a paste. It works at room temperature. It works faster in warm water. And when you are finished there is no smell at all, which matters when the object is a coffee machine.

  

One rule with the warm water, and it is the German rule, so learn it now. Warm. Not boiling. A very hot, strong solution can leave a thin white citrate film behind on the metal, and then you are cleaning a second time to remove the cleaner. Warm to the touch, the temperature of a bath. Good. That is the whole rule.

  

And here I stop and say the cautions, because they belong at the front and not at the end where you have already poured. Gloves when the solution is strong. It is a food acid, but strong solutions and dry hands do not enjoy each other. Never mix it with chlorine bleach, not in the same bucket, not in the same toilet bowl one after the other. Never let it touch marble, travertine, or any natural stone counter or tile, because those are calcium carbonate too, and the acid does not know the difference between the rock you want gone and the rock you paid for. It will etch the polish off, and that does not come back. And on chrome and on enamel, it goes on, it does its work, and it comes off with a rinse. It does not sit for hours. Twenty minutes to overnight depending on the object, then water. That is all the caution there is. Now the ten.

  

Number one. The kettle. Fill it to the line with water. Two tablespoons of the powder for an ordinary kettle, three if the floor of it looks like a riverbed. Switch it on and stop it before it boils, or boil it and let it stand five minutes first so it comes down to warm. Then leave it. Twenty minutes. You will hear it, a small fizzing, that is the rock going into solution. After twenty minutes, pour it out, and look. Where the crust was thick, a little may remain; a second round takes it. Rinse twice, fill with clean water, boil that once, pour it out, and the kettle is finished. Bare metal. It should have been bare metal the whole time. And your water will heat faster, because the plate is touching water now instead of touching stone.

  

That is the entire method. You have just seen it. Powder, warm water, wait, rinse. What changes for the other nine is only the dose and the container. So. Nine to go. One of them is a fifteen hundred dollar appliance, and the powder goes in exactly as it went into the kettle, but there is a reason the professionals charge two hundred dollars to do it, and I will show you what that reason is when we get there.

Number two. The coffee machine. Here the powder is the same, but the rule is the machine's. Every coffee maker has a descaling procedure in its manual, and here is a thing worth knowing: most of the German machine makers sell a descaler of their own, and the main ingredient on the back of that bottle is citric acid. So the powder in your paper bag is what they are selling you in a branded bottle. Follow the manual's steps for quantity and for the rinse cycles, use the powder in warm water as the solution, and run the rinse cycles it asks for, all of them, because the taste of citric acid in a coffee is small but it is there. What you get back is water that comes through at the right temperature again and a machine that stops making that groaning sound at the start of the pour. That sound was the pump working against a narrowed pipe. Now it is quiet.

  

Number three. The showerhead and the tap aerators. These are the small screw-on screens at the tip of your kitchen and bathroom taps, and they collect Kalk the way a drain collects hair. A tablespoon of powder in a cup of warm water. Put the solution in a plastic bag, put the showerhead into the bag so the face is under the water, and tie the bag around the neck of the shower arm with a rubber band. Leave it overnight. In the morning, take the bag off, run the shower hot for a minute, and count the holes that spray straight now. All of them. For the aerators, unscrew them by hand, or with a cloth and pliers if they are tight, drop them in a cup of the same solution overnight, rinse, and screw them back on. A new showerhead is twenty to fifty dollars, and the one you have was never broken. It was only wearing a coat.

  

Number four. The toilet. Two places, and one warning. Under the rim, where the water enters the bowl, there is a ring of small holes and they scale up exactly like the showerhead did, which is why your flush has become lazy on one side. A cup of solution, one tablespoon in warm water, poured slowly around the rim so it runs down through the holes, and left overnight with the lid down. Flush in the morning. The second place is the tank, and here is the warning. The tank has a rubber flapper at the bottom and rubber seals at the fill valve, and rubber does not want to sit in acid for a night. So in the tank the soak is short. Turn off the supply, flush so the tank is mostly empty, pour in a cup of solution, wait thirty minutes, no more, then turn the supply back on and flush twice. Short, and done. If you have ever paid a plumber to come and look at a weak flush, this was what he found, and he charged you for the drive.

  

Number five. The dishwasher. Empty it. Two to three tablespoons of powder straight into the bottom of the machine, not in the detergent cup, and run the hottest, longest cycle it has with nothing inside. The heating element, the spray arms, and the filter come out of it clear. Your glasses stop coming out cloudy, because the cloud on the glass was never the glass. It was a fine layer of the same rock the machine was spraying on with the water.

  

Halfway. Stop a moment and see what the five have in common, because it is not an accident. Kettle, coffee machine, showerhead, toilet, dishwasher. Different rooms, different shapes, one procedure. Warm water, a measured spoon of powder, a wait, a rinse. Not one of these needed a tool. Not one needed a part. The only thing that changed between them was how long you waited and how much powder went in. This is the German idea of Wartung, which is maintenance, and it means a small thing done on a schedule instead of a large thing done in a panic. Nobody in that kitchen is descaling the kettle because it broke. They descale it because it is the first of the month. So. Five more, and now they get bigger, and the numbers on the invoice get bigger with them.

  

Number six. The washing machine. Same as the dishwasher, one step different. Two to three tablespoons of powder into the empty drum, run the hottest cycle it has, empty. If your machine has a service wash or a drum-clean cycle, use that one. What this does you cannot see, because the element in a washing machine sits inside the drum housing, and it has been growing a stone coat for years, so each wash heats slower and costs more. When it fails, the repair is a service visit and a part, and the machine is often old enough that the visit talks you into a new machine instead. Eight dollars a year of powder against that. Once a month for a hard-water house, once every two or three months elsewhere.

  

Number seven. Tiles, glass, and taps. Here the powder becomes a spray. One tablespoon in five hundred milliliters of warm water, which is a little over two cups, into a plastic spray bottle. Label the bottle. Spray it onto the white haze on the shower glass, the crust around the base of the tap, the dull ring on the chrome, wait five to ten minutes, wipe with a cloth, rinse with clean water. Rinse, always, on chrome. And read the caution again before you spray a wall. Ceramic tile, glass, chrome, stainless, enamel, fine. Marble, travertine, limestone, granite that has been sealed with something you do not know: leave it. That is not a stain on the stone. That is the stone, and the acid will eat the polish with the same appetite it ate the Kalk. Here you stop.

  

Number eight. The iron. If your steam iron has begun to spit brown flakes onto a white shirt, that is Kalk breaking off the inside of the steam chamber. Check the manual, since some irons ask for nothing but distilled water and say so. For the ones that allow it, a weak solution, a teaspoon in a cup of warm water, into the reservoir, let the iron heat, steam it out over the sink until the reservoir is empty, then fill with clean water and steam that out too. Twice. The shirt stops getting speckled. The iron did not need replacing. It needed to be emptied.

  

Number nine. The humidifier. The white dust on the furniture near it, the crust on the element or the wick housing, all Kalk, because the machine is taking your tap water and leaving the minerals behind on itself. Unplug it, empty it, a tablespoon in warm water in the base, twenty to thirty minutes, a soft brush on the stubborn places, rinse well, dry. Do this every couple of weeks in the season you use it and it lasts for years. The manual will tell you the same and call it descaling, and now you know the word on the back of the descaler bottle.

  

And number ten. The tankless water heater. The one on the wall. This is where the stakes change, so listen. Inside a tankless heater the water runs through a narrow heat exchanger, and Kalk builds inside that exchanger exactly as it built on the floor of your kettle, only you cannot tip it toward the window and look. When the passages narrow, the heater works harder, the hot water gets weaker, and eventually the unit fails. A replacement is fifteen hundred dollars and often more, before anyone installs it. The professionals descale it, and the solution they pump through is, again, citric acid in water, circulated through the exchanger for about forty-five minutes with a small pump and two hoses connected to the isolation valves under the unit. They charge one hundred fifty to two hundred fifty dollars for the visit. The powder in their bucket is the same powder in your bag. What you are paying for is the pump, the hoses, and knowing which valve.

  

So here is my plain word on it. If your heater has isolation valves with the service ports already fitted, and you own or borrow a descaling pump kit, and you have read your unit's manual and it describes this procedure, then it is the kettle again, only with a pump. Warm solution, forty-five minutes of circulation, flush with clean water, done. If any one of those three things is missing, if there are no service valves, if you are not certain what the manual asks for, if the unit is gas and you are not comfortable near it, then this one is not for you and I have said why. Book the visit. Two hundred dollars once a year is the sensible price for a fifteen hundred dollar machine you are not sure of. What you do not do is nothing, because nothing is the most expensive option on the list.

  

So. Ten places. And now look back along the row, kettle to heater, and you will see that there were never ten things here. There was one thing. Kalk is the same rock in every room. The kettle floor, the sideways spray, the lazy flush, the cloudy glass, the spitting iron, the weak hot water, all one material, one cause, one powder. And the two households you saw today were not one clever and one foolish. They were two answers to the same water. The American answer replaces the showerhead, buys the branded descaler, calls the man with the pump. The German answer buys a bag of powder for the price of a coffee, puts it on the bottom shelf, and marks a date. Older, duller, cheaper. It was never taught to you. Now it has been.

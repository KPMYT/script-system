\[quietly\] There is a small movement you make in your bathroom without noticing it anymore. You flush, you turn to leave, the tank is still hissing, and you reach back and give the handle a little shake. Sometimes two. The hissing stops, and you get on with your evening. Your hand worked that out on its own, and it is right more often than you would guess. But a toilet that needs your hand every night is trying to tell you something, and the shake only answers part of it.

  

A toilet runs for one of three reasons. One is a crust of stone thinner than a fingernail, where you would never think to look, and it dissolves in something likely already in your kitchen. \[slightly amused\] One is a screw you have never touched, which the toilet has been obeying faithfully all along. And the third is the reason your hand learned that shake. The work takes about ten minutes of your hands, and usually no new parts. Sometimes a part truly is worn out, and I will show you how to tell, and what it costs when it is.

  

\[warm\] I am Werner Finkbeiner, and here the American house gets the German answer to its everyday troubles. Today, the toilet that will not stop running, made quiet again with a few things from around the house and a little patience.

  

One thing said plainly first. Water on the floor around the base of your toilet, or a crack anywhere in the porcelain, means a different job, and not a ten-minute one, so this is not the video for it. If your toilet runs, hisses, or refills itself with nobody near it, stay.

  

Refilling with nobody near it is the part worth worrying about. A seal that leaks a little lets a thin film of water slide into the bowl, too thin to see moving, and every so often the tank quietly tops itself up. At night you may hear it, a short hiss from an empty bathroom. People call that a ghost flush. \[matter-of-fact\] A toilet leaking past its seal can lose up to two hundred gallons in a day. On many American water bills, where you pay for the water coming in and again for the sewer taking it away, that comes to fifty to a hundred dollars a month, for water no one ever used.

  

So. Watch this through once, then do it in order, with your phone propped where you can see it.

  

The lid comes off the tank first. It is porcelain, heavier than it looks, and it breaks like a dinner plate, so it goes flat on a folded towel on the floor, never across the seat. In German there is a word for this kind of care, Ordnung. \[slightly amused\] It sounds stern, but here it only means the lid has a safe place, and it goes there before anything interesting happens.

  

Inside, three things matter. On one side stands the fill valve, a plastic tower with a float, either a cup on the tower or a ball on an arm, and the float tells the valve when to stop. In the middle stands an open pipe, the overflow tube, a safety drain into the bowl. At the bottom lies the flapper, a round rubber flap resting on a ring and tied to the handle by a short chain. You flush, the chain lifts it, the water rushes out, and it drops back onto its ring.

  

\[slower\] Three looks, then, and the order matters.

  

First, the top of the overflow tube, once the tank has filled. If water keeps slipping over that rim after the filling should have stopped, you have found one cause already. A thin hose clipped to the tube trickles in during each refill, and that belongs there, but water still running in after the tank is full does not.

  

Second, the chain. With the flapper down, is it pulled straight and tight, or knotted, or is a loose end lying where it could slip underneath?

  

Third, the color. A few drops of food coloring in the tank water, a color your bowl is not already, then no flushing for fifteen minutes. If color creeps into the bowl, water is getting past the seal. \[emphatic\] This test only tells the truth if the first look came out clean, because water spilling into the overflow carries the color into the bowl even past a perfect seal. If the tube is spilling, then, the color waits until the float is set right.

  

While the color sits, look through the water at the ring the flapper rests on. In plenty of tanks it has gone chalky or rough, with a pale crust along its edge. That crust is limescale, the same white stone that grows inside a kettle, left behind by hard water one tankful at a time.

  

\[thoughtful\] A great deal of Germany has hard water, and in a German house, when a toilet runs, the first suspect is not a broken part. It is that crust, or a grain of grit stuck on the ring, holding the seal open by less than the width of a hair. Rubber resting on stone stops being a seal.

  

\[short pause\] Here the two habits part ways. In many American homes, a running toilet means a new flapper from the hardware store. In many German homes, it means taking the old seal out and dissolving the stone, and that works more often. A brand-new flapper, set down on the same crusted ring, leaks too. The stone was on the seat all along.

  

Getting that stone off takes something from your kitchen and twenty minutes, and those twenty minutes are a hard limit, for a reason that has to do with the rubber itself.

Fifteen minutes on, the bowl has its answer. Color in the water, even a faint tint at the back, means the seal is leaking. A clear bowl means it is holding for now, and the trouble is the float or the chain. Either way, the seal gets cleaned today, because the tank will be open anyway and the stone keeps growing. Flush the color away while it is fresh.

  

Now the water goes off. Behind the toilet, low on the wall or the floor, a small valve sits on the pipe that feeds the tank. Turn it clockwise, the way you close a tap, until it stops, and some need only a quarter turn. \[matter-of-fact\] If it will not move by hand, do not lean on it with pliers. Old valves snap when they are bullied, so stop there and have that valve seen to first.

  

Flush and hold the handle down so the tank drains as far as it can, then sponge the last water from the bottom into a bucket. Rubber gloves for the rest of this.

  

If a cleaning tablet sits in the tank, on the bottom or in a little cage, lift it out now and set it outside the bathroom. \[slower\] An acid is about to come in here, and the chlorine in many of those tablets meets acid badly, as a gas you do not want to breathe in a small room. Why that tablet never goes back, you will hear later.

  

If your bowl took the color, the seal was your leak, and here it is. Unhook the chain from the handle arm. A typical flapper hangs from the base of the overflow tube by two rubber ears hooked over two pegs, and it comes away with a gentle pull outward. Some slide over the tube on a ring instead. Nothing in a toilet tank wants force.

  

Turn it over and hold it to the light. A pale film on the underside, a gritty rim, sometimes a crust you can catch with a thumbnail.

  

Into a bowl it goes. White vinegar straight from the bottle works, and so does citric acid, the white powder a lot of German kitchens keep for this kind of job, a few spoonfuls stirred into lukewarm water. The whole sealing face goes under.

  

Now a timer for twenty minutes, a real one, and here is the reason I promised you. The acid that softens the stone works on rubber too, only more slowly. \[emphatic\] At twenty minutes the stone has gone soft and the rubber has not. Leave it an hour and you begin to soften the very part you came to save. Those twenty minutes belong to the acid, and you have other work to do inside them.

  

The ring gets the same bath while the clock runs. Soak a cloth in the vinegar or citric acid, fold it, and lay it on the ring so it stays put. After a few minutes, scrub the ring with an old toothbrush or the soft side of a sponge. Leave the steel wool in the cupboard, because a scratch in that ring is a new leak you made yourself. \[quietly\] Then run a fingertip all the way around. It should feel like the rim of a clean drinking glass. Any grain or ridge gets more acid and more brushing, and when the ring is smooth everywhere, most of the job is done.

  

Twelve minutes or so left on the timer, plenty for the float. If you saw water slipping over the rim of the overflow tube, this one is yours, but look either way.

  

Then the inside wall of the tank. \[thoughtful\] In hard water, a tank keeps a record. The water stands at the same height day after day, and the minerals leave a faint line, like a ring in a bathtub. If that line sits level with the top of the overflow tube, or above it, your tank has been filling until it spills, and the spill has run into the bowl the whole time.

  

That is the screw from the beginning. The float lets the valve keep filling until it rises to a set height, and at some point that height got set too high. \[slightly amused\] The toilet simply did as it was told.

  

So you lower it. On most modern valves the float is a cup on the tower, set by a long screw on top or a metal clip on a thin rod, which you pinch and slide down. On an older toilet with a ball on an arm, look for a small screw where the arm meets the valve. Which way lowers it is often marked on the valve, and if not, a few turns and the water will soon show you. The target is about an inch below the top of the overflow tube, and a water line marked inside the tank, if yours has one, wins.

  

So. Eight minutes left, and time to put the first two side by side. A seal that could not close because of stone you could barely see, and a valve that kept filling because one screw was set too high. Neither part was broken, and you replaced nothing. Both were doing their jobs while conditions shifted quietly around them. \[thoughtful\] Before you ask what broke, ask what changed.

  

When the timer sounds, the flapper comes out of its bath for a rinse under the tap. Feel it. A healthy one is soft, bends easily, springs back when you let go, and its sealing face is smooth from edge to edge. That feel decides whether you are finished or shopping.

  

If it passes, it goes back the way it came off and drops flat onto the ring, sitting square. If it fails, set it aside, and I will come to it shortly. \[warm\] Now the third cause, and if your hand knows that little shake, this one is about you.

  

Hook the chain back onto the handle arm so that, with the flapper lying flat, about two links hang slack. Pulled tight, it lets the arm hold the flapper up by a hair, and water slips out under it. If it is too long, the spare links can drift under the rubber and prop it open from below. And a chain that has twisted or knotted on itself is a short chain now, so straighten it link by link.

  

That is what your hand was doing every evening. The shake knocked a tangled chain loose, or settled a flapper the chain would not let fall. \[quietly\] Your hand found the fix a long time ago. It never found the cause.

  

Open the valve slowly until it is fully open, let the tank fill, and watch where the water stops. About an inch below the top of the overflow tube, or at the line, is right. If it climbs toward the rim, lower the float another turn or two, and if it stops far too low, give a little back. Flush a few times, watch the flapper drop and settle, and listen. Once the tank is full, the hiss should stop and stay stopped.

  

During one of those refills, lift the float gently with your hand, and the water should stop. If it does, the fill valve is healthy. \[matter-of-fact\] If it keeps hissing with the float held up, the valve has worn out inside, and that is one of the two times a part is really needed.

  

Then the color once more, fifteen minutes, no flushing. A clear bowl means a sealed tank, and the lid leaves its towel and goes back where it belongs.

  

So, the two parts. The first is a flapper that is finished, one that comes out of its bath stiff, or warped so an edge no longer lies flat, or cracked, or soft and swollen. \[matter-of-fact\] No cleaning brings that back. A new flapper costs five to ten dollars. Take the old one along to match it.

  

The second is the fill valve that keeps hissing with the float held up. A new one costs ten to fifteen dollars, and with the water off it comes out once you undo the supply line and one large nut under the tank. Each of these is about a ten-minute job.

  

Set those beside the bill. \[quietly\] A plumber's visit for a running toilet costs a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty dollars, and inside that bill, very often, is the same five-dollar piece of rubber.

  

And the tablet you lifted out at the start. Many of those work with chlorine, and chlorine, sitting in a tank day and night, is hard on rubber. Slowly it hardens, swells, or warps the flapper until it no longer sits flat. When a flapper warps long before its time, the tablet is most often why. \[emphatic\] The big toilet and fill valve makers say in their own warranty terms that chlorine in the tank voids the coverage. The tablet goes in the trash, then, and a brush keeps the bowl clean without it.

  

\[thoughtful\] In Germany, most toilets have no tank you can see, only a flush plate on the wall, and behind it sits the same rubber seal on the same kind of ring, in water just as hard. It comes out through the opening behind the plate, gets the same citric acid bath, and goes back. Descaling is an ordinary chore in a German home, for the kettle, the coffee machine, the shower head, and, when a toilet runs, that seal. \[warm\] The plain word for chores like this is Wartung, maintenance on a schedule, done before any emergency.

  

Now look at the three together. Stone on the ring kept the flapper from settling, and a float set too high kept the valve from ever switching off. The chain, the one your hand kept fixing, held the flapper up so it could not fall. Three culprits in three corners of the tank, every one failing the same way. \[slower\] Something in there was not allowed to come to rest. A running toilet is never more than that, a small machine that cannot finish its job and go still.

  

Three small jobs for the coming week, then. Run the color test on every toilet in the house, the quiet ones too, since a leak that makes no sound is the one nobody checks. Put a bag of citric acid under the bathroom sink. And mark twenty minutes on the calendar for the same week every spring, flapper in its bath, cloth on the ring, one look at the line on the wall.

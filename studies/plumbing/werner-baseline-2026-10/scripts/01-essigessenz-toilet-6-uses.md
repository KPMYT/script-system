The bottle is called Essigessenz. Vinegar essence. Every supermarket in Germany carries it, down beside the cooking vinegar, plain plastic, 400 ml, about one euro. About a dollar. What is inside is acetic acid at 25%.

Now look at the white vinegar on your own shelf. That is 5%. Five times weaker. And that one number is the whole video, because at 5% you are wiping a surface, and at 25% you are taking the surface apart.

In America the grocery store will not help you here. The hardware store will. Ask for cleaning vinegar at 30%, sold by the gallon, $15 to $20. A gallon is a great many cups, and the cup you are going to pour tonight works out to well under a dollar.

You do not need a test kit to know whether this applies to you, either. If your soap will not lather properly, if the glass in the shower spots every single time it dries, if there is a white fur on the inside of your kettle, your water is hard and it has been depositing on every surface it touches since the day you moved in.

And that deposit is not only an appearance problem. Those little holes under the rim are jets. The flush is supposed to come out of them sideways and go around the bowl with some force. Fill them with stone and the water dribbles instead, which is the real reason a lot of toilets stop clearing properly and get called weak.

Which is worth sitting with for a second. Having a scaled toilet professionally descaled runs $100 to $150. A bowl left long enough that nobody can save it gets replaced instead, $300 to $600 installed. You did not pay for the crust. You paid for not knowing what dissolves it.

Before you open anything, though, I stop and you should also.

Acid at 25% is corrosive. Gloves, every time. A window open or the fan running. Nothing near your eyes, and if it does reach them, water for a long time and then a doctor.

Never mix it with chlorine bleach. Vinegar and bleach together release chlorine gas, and a bathroom with the door shut is a very small room to do that in. Never mix it with hydrogen peroxide either. That combination makes peracetic acid, which is harsher than either thing you started with. If you have already poured something else into that bowl today, flush it through and wait. One product at a time, always.

Keep it off marble, off natural stone, off unsealed grout. Acid eats calcium, and calcium is what those are made of. Keep long soaks away from rubber. And on chrome, short contact only, then rinse it.

One more, on storage, because this is where it goes wrong in a house with children. When you mix a dilution, mix it in a spray bottle that was never a drinks bottle, write on it in marker what is inside, and put it up high with the cleaning things. It looks exactly like water. It is not water.

Number one. The bowl and the rim.

What you felt under that lip is limescale, calcium carbonate, the same white stone that forms inside a kettle. In a toilet it comes mixed with urine scale, which is where the yellow and the brown come from, and together they harden into a crust that a brush simply rides over. You can scrub that ring for ten years. The bristles are softer than the stone.

And I know what has already been tried, because everybody tries the same three things. Bleach, which whitens the crust beautifully and removes not one particle of it, so the stain comes back in a fortnight looking like it never left. The blue tablet in the tank, which sits on your flapper all day long doing nothing for the ring at all. And then the pumice stone, scraped down the porcelain in frustration, which does take the crust off and takes the glaze with it, and a bowl with no glaze left scales twice as fast forever after.

So here is the whole method instead. Gloves on, window open. Pour one cup of the essence straight into the bowl. Not diluted. You want it at full strength against the waterline where the ring sits.

For the underside of the rim, where nothing ever stays long enough to work, tear off a good length of toilet paper, soak it in the essence, and press it up under the lip so it holds there, wet, against the crust. Then go to bed.

In the morning you will see the answer before you touch anything. The crust will be fizzing where the acid has found it, and the edges of it will have lifted away from the porcelain in little plates. That is the calcium carbonate coming apart and giving up its gas. Now take the brush. It will not fight you. The ring comes away in sheets and goes down with the flush.

If it is an old crust, thick and layered, one night will loosen the top of it and no more. Do not go and find the pumice stone. Do it again the next night. The second soak reaches what the first one opened up, and that is the whole trick with acid, patience rather than pressure.

Whatever you do, keep it out of the tank. Down inside that tank is a rubber flapper, and rubber sitting in acid overnight is how you buy yourself a running toilet on top of the problem you already had. The bowl only.

So. Now it is white. And listen to the next flush, because that is the part nobody expects. The jets are open again and the water goes around the bowl the way the factory intended. It should have been doing that the whole time.

Number two. The kettle and the coffee maker.

Same mineral, different machine. That white shell on your heating element works like a jacket. All the heat has to push through it, so the machine runs longer and hotter to do the same job it did in its first year, and in a coffee maker the water reaches the grounds at the wrong temperature and the cup tastes like a bean you did not buy.

You already know if yours needs it. The kettle takes noticeably longer than it used to. It rattles and roars on the way up instead of humming. There is grit in the bottom, and sometimes a white flake floating in your tea.

This one you dilute. Roughly one part essence to ten parts water. Fill the kettle with that, bring it up warm, switch it off and let it stand while the white lets go of the metal. Pour it out, run plain water through it twice, throw both of those away. A coffee maker, same idea, run the dilution through and then two clean water cycles after it. Every few months in hard water, once or twice a year in soft.

But read the manual first, and I mean that. Many German manufacturers will tell you to use citric acid instead of vinegar in their machines, particularly anywhere there is aluminum inside, and aluminum with acid on it is a conversation you cannot have in a machine you are not able to open. The label knows things about that machine that you do not.

Number three. The showerhead, and the little screen in the end of your kitchen faucet.

You know the spray. One jet going sideways, hitting the wall or your shoulder, the pressure gone soft and disappointing. The head is blocked. Nothing inside it has broken, and nothing needs replacing.

Mix that same one to ten dilution into a sandwich bag. Bring the bag up over the head so the whole face of it sits submerged, rubber band around the neck, and leave it overnight. Nothing gets unscrewed, nothing gets taken apart, and nobody comes to the house.

In the morning, off comes the bag, and run the hot water through for a minute to push the loosened scale out through the holes. An old toothbrush across the face of it takes care of whatever is left sitting in the rubber nozzles. Then rinse the outside of it properly, because chrome wants short contact with acid and a good rinse afterward.

The aerator in your faucet is the same problem in a smaller part. Unscrew it by hand, and as it comes apart, lay the pieces down on the counter in the order they came out, because there is a screen and a washer in there and they go back the way they came. Drop the lot in a cup of the dilution overnight, brush the screen clean in the morning, reassemble, screw it back on. Water pressure at that sink that you had assumed was the building's fault.

Number four. The washing machine, and there are two separate uses inside the one machine.

First the machine itself. Scale forms on the heating element in there exactly the way it does in your kettle, and detergent residue builds up in the drum and in the folds of the rubber door seal, which is where that smell comes from in a machine that is supposedly clean. So, one cup of the essence into an empty drum. No clothes, no detergent. The hottest cycle the machine has, and let it run all the way through. Every few months is plenty.

While it is running, pull back the door seal with a gloved hand and wipe into the fold with a cloth, because that is where the black speckling lives and no cycle reaches it. And from now on, leave that door standing open between washes so the thing can dry out.

Now the second use, the one I promised you. The rinse compartment, where the fabric softener goes. A splash of diluted essence goes in there instead, and plenty of German households have run their laundry that way for years.

The reasoning is not complicated. Softener works by coating the fibers, which is also why towels that have had years of it go slick and stop actually drying you. The acid takes that leftover coating off during the rinse instead of adding another layer of it. Towels come out able to take water again. And the smell does not survive the spin, so do not let that stop you.

A bottle of softener is a thing you buy, and buy again, and go on buying forever. This is a splash out of a bottle that costs about a dollar.

Number five. The shower glass and the taps.

Same one to ten dilution, this time in a spray bottle, labeled, as we discussed. The cloud on your glass door and the white spotting on your chrome is the same mineral again, only thinner, left behind by drops that dried where they landed.

Spray it on, give it two or three minutes and no longer, wipe it down, then rinse the glass with water and dry it off with a cloth. On the taps the same, short contact, then rinse. This is where people get careless, because chrome looks tough and takes its time about showing you that it was not.

And in that room especially, watch where you are spraying. A marble sill or a natural stone floor will etch, and etched stone does not come back. With acid there is no being roughly careful.

Then the habit that stops it returning. A cheap squeegee hung inside the shower, one pass down the glass while you are still standing there wet. Ten seconds. The mineral cannot dry onto glass that has no water left on it, and you will not need the spray bottle again for months.

Number six. The toilet brush, and the pot it stands in.

Nobody thinks about this one. That holder has an inch of old water sitting in the bottom of it that has been there since the day you bought it, and the brush you use to clean the toilet is now the dirtiest object in the room.

Pour a finger of essence into the bottom of the holder. Stand the brush down in it. Leave it while you do something else, then tip the whole lot into the toilet and rinse it out. From then on, a splash in the bottom of the holder each time you use the brush, and the brush drips it into the bowl the next time, which is exactly where you wanted it anyway.

It costs almost nothing and it takes away the smell that nobody can locate in a bathroom that is otherwise spotless.

Now, where you stop, because a bottle of acid is not the answer to everything in that room.

If two nights of soaking has moved nothing at all, the crust is not the whole story and something is feeding it, so stop soaking and have it looked at. If the bowl has been attacked with a pumice stone in the past and the porcelain is dull and rough to the hand, no acid restores glaze, and that bowl will keep scaling no matter what you do. If there is water at the base of the toilet, or a stain on a ceiling below a bathroom, that is not a cleaning question and this bottle has nothing to say about it. And if a fixture is old brass, or coated in something you cannot identify, test on the underside where nobody looks before you go anywhere near the front of it.

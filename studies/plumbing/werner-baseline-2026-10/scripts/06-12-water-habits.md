Your toilet charges you for the same flush whether the job was big or small. Above a German toilet, set into the wall, there are two buttons, and the small one uses about 3 liters. That button is one of twelve habits that keep a person in a German house to about 125 to 130 liters of water a day. The EPA puts an American at 82 gallons a day at home, which is 310 liters. But before you picture cold showers and a dead lawn, not one of the twelve asks you to live worse.

  

For a family of four, the EPA's number is more than 300 gallons a day, just at home. The German figure is less than half of that, and the habits behind it are smaller than you would expect. Among them is a part the size of a coin that screws onto a faucet and costs about $3. Another is a tablet you drop into your toilet tank to find a leak that makes no sound at all. And the last is a little dial, down in your basement or under a lid near the street, that can tell you in two hours whether your house is leaking.

  

I am Werner Finkbeiner, and this channel is for people in American houses who would rather understand a thing than pay someone to look at it. Today we walk through your house room by room, with the German habit for each one and how to copy it this week.

  

The reason a German house learned all of this is mostly the bill. Water and wastewater together in Germany commonly cost the equivalent of $15 to $25 for every thousand gallons. In a typical American city, the two together come to roughly $10 to $12. When water costs that much more, a household starts noticing where every liter goes, and these twelve habits are what it noticed. They were seldom taught in America, because for a long time the bill did not demand it. It is starting to. The average American household now pays around $1,000 a year for water and sewer.

  

So. We begin at the two buttons. Number one is the dual flush itself. The big button on a German toilet sends about 6 liters, the small one about 3, and the small job is the one that happens several times a day. An American toilet solved the same problem with one flush big enough for anything. It always works, and it always costs the full amount. The German toilet asks you to pick, every time you flush.

  

You do not need a new toilet to copy it. A dual-flush conversion kit for an American toilet runs around $20 at the hardware store, and the common ones swap the flapper and the handle for a valve with two settings, a short flush and a full one. It is an afternoon job, and most kits need nothing beyond your hands and a sponge to empty the tank.

  

Two cautions first. These kits fit many toilets, not every one, so read the box against what you see inside your own tank. And if the short flush will not clear the bowl once the kit is in, that toilet is not a candidate, so take the kit back and keep the full flush.

  

Number two stays at that toilet, because it is about the day you replace it. Newer German toilets are often set to about 4.5 liters for a full flush. In America, a toilet that carries the EPA's WaterSense label uses 1.28 gallons or less, and that lands in nearly the same place. So the German habit is already sitting on the shelf at the store, if you read the box.

  

To see where your toilet stands today, look at the china just behind the seat, or at the back wall inside the tank. The flush size is often stamped right there, in gallons, in liters, or both. If your number is higher than 1.28, you now know which toilet in the house goes first, whenever one of them has to go. When that day comes, read the stamp first, because you pay for that flush every day it sits there.

  

But this toilet is not finished with us. It has one more habit, and this one makes no noise at all. It can run all day and all night with nobody home, and we come back for it before we leave the bathroom.

Number three is at the tip of the bathroom faucet. Unscrew that little cap and you will find a screen and a plastic insert, the aerator, which mixes air into the stream so a smaller amount of water still feels full on your hands. Every tap in a German home wears one, and one of the biggest makers of these inserts in the world started out in Germany.

  

An American faucet almost always has one already, so the question is which one. Many are marked on the side with their flow. The most a new American faucet may run is 2.2 gallons a minute, and a bathroom aerator with the WaterSense label runs 1.5 or less. And some are so crusted with mineral that the stream sprays sideways. A new one costs about $3. Take the old one to the store with you, because they come in a few sizes, and some thread onto the outside of the faucet while others thread inside it.

  

Screw the new one on by hand. If it weeps at the thread, a little more turn is all it wants, and if you must put pliers on a chrome finish, wrap the jaws in a cloth first. Two minutes, this job takes, and it keeps working every time the tap opens.

  

The aerator shrinks every minute the tap runs. Number four, at that same sink, gets rid of most of those minutes, and it costs nothing at all. The tap is off while the toothbrush works, and off again while the hands are soaped. It sounds too small to bother with, so work it through once. A bathroom tap at 1.5 gallons a minute, left running through two minutes of brushing, morning and night, is about 6 gallons a day for one person, and every drop of it went down the drain without touching the brush.

  

In a German bathroom the tap opens to wet the brush and opens again to rinse, and between those two moments the sink is quiet. Washing hands goes like this. Wet them, close the tap, soap for as long as you like, then open it to rinse. It costs you a turn of the wrist and nothing else.

  

Turn around to the shower, where that pattern runs bigger, and that is number five. A German shower usually has a hand shower on a hose, and some of them have a button on the handle that stops the water while you soap and brings it back when you press it again. The routine is wet, pause, soap, rinse. The water only runs for the parts of a shower that need water, and that alone makes the shower shorter. The hot water is still hot. It stops while nobody is standing under it.

  

A handheld shower head with a pause button fits the standard shower arm in most American bathrooms. It goes on with a wrench, a cloth to protect the finish, and a few wraps of thread tape on the arm.

  

One thing you should know before you use it. On some older shower valves, the first second of water after a pause can come out hotter than you left it. So when you press the button again, point the head at the wall, test the water with your hand, and then step back under it.

  

With a fixed head, the habit works at the valve too, wet, off, soap, on, and it saves just as much.

  

And now back to the toilet, for the habit it keeps quiet. Number six. A toilet can leak for months without making a sound. At the bottom of the tank sits a rubber flap, the flapper, and as it ages it stiffens and stops sealing, so water slips past it into the bowl around the clock. Nothing overflows and the floor stays dry. The EPA says a leaking toilet can waste up to 200 gallons a day, and on a typical American bill that can add $50 to $100 a month, for a piece of rubber you could hold in one hand. A German household tends to catch this early, because a bill like theirs gets read closely.

  

Finding it costs almost nothing. Lift the tank lid and drop a dye tablet into the tank water, or a few drops of food coloring, which work just as well. Put the lid back, do not flush, and wait 15 minutes. If color shows up in the bowl, water is getting past the flapper. Flush when you are finished so the color does not stain the tank.

  

If the bowl stays clear, check the other quiet way a toilet leaks. There is an open pipe standing in the middle of the tank, the overflow tube. If the water sits at the very top of that tube and trickles into it, the fill valve is set too high, and a turn of its adjustment brings the level down.

  

A new flapper goes on without tools on most toilets. Close the little shutoff valve behind the toilet, flush to empty the tank, and bring the old flapper to the store to match it. And if that shutoff valve will not turn, stop right there. An old valve that gets forced can crack, and then you have water on the floor.

  

So. Six habits, and every one of them happened in the bathroom. What they share is a single moment, the moment after the job is done. The flush bigger than what is in the bowl. The tap still running while the brush works. The shower running on while you soap. The flapper still passing water at three in the morning. Each time the job was finished, and the water kept going. The other six leave this room for the kitchen, the laundry, and the yard, and they end at the one spot in your house that knows about every drop.

  

Out of the bathroom, then, and into the kitchen, where the first thing to listen for is the faucet. Number seven. A drip gets fixed the week it starts. The EPA figures a faucet dripping once a second wastes more than 3,000 gallons a year, and a drip does not heal on its own. The washer or cartridge that let it start keeps wearing, so the drip only grows. In a German kitchen a dripping tap is treated like a light left burning in an empty room, and it is dealt with that week.

  

On an older faucet with two handles, the culprit is usually a rubber washer inside one handle that has gone hard. On a single-lever faucet, look for a worn cartridge. Either way, the first steps do not change. Close the two shutoff valves under the sink, open the faucet to let the pressure out, and put the stopper in the drain so no small part disappears down it. Photograph each piece as it comes out, and carry the worn part with you when you buy the new one, so it matches exactly.

  

Here the job stops if the valves under the sink will not close, or if the faucet body itself is cracked. Those are a different job, and not one for a Saturday morning.

  

Number eight is right beside that sink, and it sounds backward. German dishes wait in the dishwasher until it is full, and the sink stays mostly empty. A machine feels like the wasteful choice. But a modern dishwasher uses only a few gallons of water for a whole load, and a kitchen tap left running passes that much in about two minutes. So the machine wins, and not narrowly.

  

The habit has two halves. Scrape the plates into the bin and skip the rinse under the tap, because a modern machine is built to handle what is left. Then run it full, because on most machines a half-empty load uses nearly the same water as a full one.

  

If you grew up being told that dishwashers waste water, the people who told you were remembering older machines, and those did use more.

  

The laundry room follows that arithmetic, only with more water at stake. Number nine. A German washing machine loads from the front, and it runs full. A front-loader tumbles the clothes through a shallow pool of water instead of filling a whole tub to float them, and it uses roughly a third of the water an old top-loader does.

  

You do not throw out a working machine to copy this. A top-loader that still spins is not garbage, and the day it dies is the day to choose the front-loader. Until then, wait for a full basket, and if the machine has a load-size setting, set it honestly, because a small load on the large setting fills the tub for nothing.

  

Stay at the washer and look behind it, because number ten is the hose. Behind a German washing machine there is usually one hose, for cold water only. The machine heats the water itself, inside the drum, and only as much as the cycle asks for. Behind most American machines there are two hoses, hot and cold, and a warm wash pulls hot water out of your water heater, which then burns gas or electricity heating fresh water to replace it.

  

So this habit lives mostly on your other bill, the one for energy. It still belongs on this list, because hot water is paid for twice, once for the water and once for the heat. To copy it, wash ordinary loads on cold, with a detergent that says on the bottle it works in cold water, and save the hot setting for the loads that need it.

  

And while you are down by the laundry, notice where the water pipe comes into the house. In a lot of houses with a basement, the meter sits close to that spot, and that meter is where this walk ends.

  

But the meter comes last. First, out the back door to the yard, because in an American summer that is where the bill climbs. Number eleven. A German yard usually has no sprinkler system buried under the lawn. What water the flower beds get tends to come from a barrel standing under the downspout, filled with rain that ran off the roof.

  

An automatic sprinkler system is a part of the house that runs while nobody watches, so if you have one, go and look at the controller. If it runs on a fixed schedule, it waters the morning after a storm as faithfully as it waters in a dry week. Many controllers have a rain setting, and a rain sensor can be added to many more. And a cracked head or a broken line underground leaks every time the timer runs.

  

A rain barrel under one downspout is the German half of this habit, with two things to watch. Some states and cities have rules about how much rain you may collect, so check yours first. And give the barrel a lid and a fine screen, because standing water draws mosquitoes. Use that water on the flower beds only.

  

And last, the place where all of this water comes into the house. Number twelve. Once a year, read your water meter with everything in the house turned off.

  

German water bills usually come once a year, from a meter reading, so German households tend to know where their meter is and what it says. If you have never looked at yours, today is a good day to find it. In a house with a basement it is usually near where the main pipe comes through the wall. In warmer places it sits outside, under a metal or plastic lid near the street. You can lift that lid to read the meter, but leave any valve in that box alone, because in many towns it belongs to the water company.

  

Before you read it, make sure nothing is using water. The washer, the dishwasher, and the sprinklers are off, nobody is in the shower, and the ice maker and anything else on a timer, like a water softener, are switched off too. Then look for the leak indicator. On many meters it is a small triangle, a star, or a little wheel, and it turns with even a trickle. If it is turning now, water is moving through a house where nothing is supposed to be using any.

  

If your meter has no indicator, write the number down, wait two hours without using a drop, and read it again. That is the EPA's own test. If the number changed, the house is leaking somewhere, and you hunt in order. The toilets first, with the dye. Then the faucets and the tub spout. Then the outside taps, which can leak inside the wall where you never see it. And if every one of those passes and the dial still moves, the leak may be underground, between the meter and the house. That one is not yours to dig. Call the water company first, because some of them will help you find it.

  

So think about what a still dial means. When nobody in the house needs water, none is moving, and every habit on this list was that same rule, carried into a different room. The small button sends a small flush for a small job. The tap goes quiet while the brush works. The dishwasher fills once, where the sink would have run and run. The rain barrel waters the beds with rain nobody paid for. And the dye finds the toilet that has been turning that dial while you slept. All of it together is what a German means by Sparsamkeit. A dictionary turns that word into thrift, and thrift sounds like going without. In a German house it is plainer than that. The water runs while it is doing a job, and you refuse to pay for any that is not.

  

So the week looks like this. Tonight, put color in every toilet tank and look at the bowls a quarter of an hour later. This weekend, on a morning when nobody needs a shower, do the two-hour test at the meter. And the next time you pass a hardware store, buy one aerator for the tap you use most.

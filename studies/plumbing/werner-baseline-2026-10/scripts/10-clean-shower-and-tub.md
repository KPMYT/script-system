\[thoughtful\] The first job happens every time, and to see why it works, start with what is actually in your water. Tap water carries minerals, mostly calcium, picked up from the ground on the way to your house. As long as the water stays liquid, you never see them. When a drop dries, the water leaves and the minerals cannot, so each drop leaves a tiny ring, and the next shower adds another ring on top. That white crust on your shower door is dried drops, stacked, months of them. Leave it long enough and the glass underneath goes cloudy for good, and a replacement shower door is five hundred dollars or more. \[matter-of-fact\] The squeegee is still five.

Mold works from the same water in a different way. It needs moisture and something to eat, and a shower gives it both, a damp corner and a thin film of soap and skin. Take the water away and it has nothing to start in. So. Scale cannot begin on a dry surface, and neither can mold. That one fact is the German routine in a single sentence, and the squeegee is how you act on it.

\[slower\] Top to bottom the blade goes, glass first. When you turn off the water, before you reach for the towel, pull the squeegee down the door in straight strokes, each one overlapping the last a little, and shake the water off the blade into the tub as you go. Then the tile walls you can reach, the same way. Then one slow pass along the bottom edge of the door, where water likes to sit. Ten seconds, maybe fifteen the first week, while your hands learn it.

The squeegee lives on a hook inside the shower, at the height of your hand, and never under the sink. Germans have a word for this, Ordnung, which means order in the plain sense that every tool has its place, and the place for this tool is where the water is. A tool you have to go and fetch is a tool you skip. \[slightly amused\] Nobody hands you a squeegee with the keys to a new apartment, so the habit never made the trip over here. The American answer became a strong spray and a long Saturday. The German answer is ten seconds and nothing to buy after the first day. Same minerals, same physics, two different answers.

\[thoughtful\] The squeegee keeps clean glass clean. What it cannot do is lift the crust that is already there, or the gray ring, or that black line, and getting those off without ruining what is underneath is where the next seven jobs come in, including the sponge that must never touch an acrylic tub.

\[matter-of-fact\] Once a week comes the second job, and it is for fresh scale, the thin white haze the squeegee missed. Scale is mostly calcium carbonate, and a mild acid takes it apart. On a German cleaning shelf, the biggest section is vinegar cleaner, plain sprays built on vinegar, and for this job that is exactly the right thing. Some households buy vinegar essence instead, which is a concentrate, and that one always gets diluted with water first, with gloves on, because at full strength it is hard on skin. In an American kitchen, white vinegar cut with water in a spray bottle is the same idea.

Spray the glass, the taps, and the tiles, and then leave it alone for a few minutes, long enough to make a coffee and not long enough to drink it. Rinse everything with clean water, and squeegee it dry.

\[emphatic\] Two rules travel with the vinegar, and they matter more than the vinegar does. The first one is chrome and enamel. Shiny taps, and the glassy coating on an old steel or cast iron tub, can take an acid for a few minutes, but they should never sit under one for hours, so the rinse is part of the job and never optional.

The second rule is stone. If your shower is marble or travertine or any other natural stone, no acid goes on it at all, not the vinegar and not the citric acid coming next. So. The reason is close to funny. Marble and travertine are made of the same mineral as the scale. \[short pause\] The acid cannot tell the crust from the wall, it dissolves both, and on stone it leaves a dull etched patch where the shine used to be. Stone gets a cleaner sold for stone, and a little more faith in the squeegee.

Vinegar handles what is new. Scale that has been building for months, the crust you can catch with a fingernail, waits for the third job, and that one you do only when the bathroom asks. Its tool is citric acid, a white powder sold in the canning aisle. \[slightly amused\] It is a stronger acid than vinegar, it does not make the bathroom smell like a salad, and old scale gives way to it. Stir a couple of spoonfuls into a cup or two of warm water, warm the way you would wash your hands, never boiling.

Now the part that makes it work. A liquid sprayed on a vertical door runs straight to the drain and does almost nothing on the way, so the acid has to be held against the scale. Soak a cloth and wrap it around the tap. Press soaked paper towels flat against the glass door, where they will cling on their own. Lay more of them along the crusty line of the tub. \[matter-of-fact\] Then leave all of it for twenty to thirty minutes and go do something else.

When you come back, peel everything off, wipe the softened scale away with the cloth, and rinse it all with plenty of clean water before you dry it. On chrome and enamel, that rinse is what keeps half an hour from turning into an overnight soak. Refinishing a tub that scale has ruined runs three hundred to five hundred dollars. A bag of citric acid is about eight.

\[slightly amused\] The fourth job you find with your feet. When the tub feels slick under them, or the tile has a dull gray film you could write your name in, that is soap scum. Soap meets the minerals in hard water, and together they make a waxy layer that water alone will not rinse away. For this one, the German answer is a cream cleanser in a squeeze bottle, about three dollars, or a paste of washing soda and a little water. Washing soda is sold in the laundry aisle, and it is strong enough that gloves are a good idea. Rub it on with a soft cloth, give it a minute, rinse, and the gray film goes with it. \[thoughtful\] On a glossy acrylic tub, keep to a soft cloth and a light hand, and if the cleanser bottle says it is not for acrylic, believe the bottle.

And now the sponge from the beginning. The white foam block sold as an eraser is made of melamine, and it works because it is an extremely fine abrasive, closer to sandpaper than to a sponge. On tile, for a stubborn mark, it does good work. On a glossy acrylic tub it does exactly the same thing, and that is the trouble. It sands. The shine it takes off is the shine the tub left the factory with, and no sponge will ever put it back. Tile, yes. \[emphatic\] Acrylic, never.

That is four of the eight, and it is worth stopping here to notice what the four have in common. None of them asked you to scrub hard. The squeegee takes ten seconds, the vinegar sits while you make coffee, the citric acid sits on its towels for half an hour, and the one tool that truly scrubs, the melamine, is the one kept away from the tub. \[quietly\] Time is doing the work your arm used to do. And all four were about what water leaves behind. The other four are about what water feeds, and where it goes.

Color tells you when the fifth job is due. When the grout, the lines between the tiles, has gone darker than the day the tiles were set, it is time. \[matter-of-fact\] Grout is a kind of cement, and it holds water the way a sponge does, slowly, for hours after the shower is over. That is why the dark usually shows up there first. The tool is a soft brush, and soft matters. A stiff wire brush scratches the grout open, and opened grout drinks even more water the next time. The cleaner is oxygen bleach, a white powder you dissolve in warm water. It fizzes, it lifts the stain, and it is kind to colored grout. Brush it into the lines, give it a while to work, brush once more, and rinse well.

Why not chlorine bleach, the bottle a lot of American bathrooms keep under the sink? Because of everything else in this routine. \[slower\] Chlorine bleach and an acid, vinegar or citric, used in the same session, can give off chlorine gas, and a bathroom with the door shut is a small room. So chlorine does not come into this routine at all, and no two products ever get mixed in one bottle or one bucket. That is the pair I promised you at the start.

The silicone is the sixth job, and it runs on two clocks. The soft white line where the tub meets the wall, and where the glass meets the tile, gets wiped dry with a cloth after every shower, right after the squeegee, because that corner is where water sits the longest. \[sighs\] And still, one day, it goes black. Look closely and you will see the black sits inside the silicone itself, because mold has grown down into the material or behind it, where no spray can reach.

German trade rules have a name for that line, Wartungsfuge, a maintenance joint. Silicone in a wet room is classed as a part meant to be renewed, the way a filter is meant to be changed, so when it turns black it is simply due. That is the silicone I said German rules expect you to replace, and it is an afternoon's work. One honest limit first. \[slower\] If the wall right above the joint feels soft when you press on it, water has already gotten behind the tile, and that belongs to a professional before any new silicone goes on.

Otherwise, cut along both edges of the old silicone with a sharp utility blade, always cutting away from your other hand, then pull the strip out and scrape off what is left. On an acrylic tub, finish with a plastic scraper, never metal. Clean the joint, and then comes the part nobody likes, letting it dry completely, a full day if you can, with the window open. \[matter-of-fact\] New silicone over a damp joint is a waste of the tube. Run painter's tape along both sides of the gap. If it is a tub, fill it with water first, so it sits at its heaviest while the new silicone sets. Lay one steady bead of bathroom silicone, the kind sold for kitchens and baths, smooth it with a wet fingertip, pull the tape, and do not shower until the tube says it has cured.

Thirty seconds a week is all the seventh job takes. Lift the strainer out of the drain and pull the hair off it, into the trash. Loose hair tangles, and once it picks up the soap scum from the fourth job it binds into a mat, and that mat is what turns a fast drain slow. \[thoughtful\] A shower that drains a little slower than it used to is the house telling you early. If your drain has no strainer at all, that is worth fixing this week.

And the last job brings you back to the moment this video started with, the water going off. The squeegee dried the walls, but the air in that room is still carrying the shower, and warm wet air puts its water down on the coldest thing it can find, the mirror, the window, the corner of an outside wall, the ceiling. So for twenty minutes after every shower, the window is open or the fan is running. \[slightly amused\] You do not have to stand there with it. The fan manages fine without you.

Now look at where the eight jobs actually happened, on the glass, the tub, the grout, the silicone, the drain, and in the air. Six places, and the same idea standing in every one of them. \[short pause\] Water may pass through a shower. It may not stay.

This week, three things. Hang a squeegee on a hook inside the shower, where the water is. Put a bag of citric acid and a pair of gloves under the sink. And look closely at the silicone along your tub, because now you know what the black inside it is telling you.

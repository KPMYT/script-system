A spotless house gets mold too. What mold needs is a surface that stays damp, and the number to remember is about eighty percent. \[slower\] When the air right against a wall sits above about eighty percent relative humidity for days at a time, that wall is where mold grows. The middle of your bedroom can feel perfectly comfortable while the corner behind the dresser is quietly damp, because the same air holds its water differently depending on how warm it is.

  

Warm air carries water you cannot see. When it touches something cold, it cools down, and cooler air cannot hold as much water, so the extra settles onto the cold surface. You know this from a cold glass of beer on a summer table, or the bathroom mirror after a shower. On a wall you mostly do not see it. The paint just stays a little damp, night after night, and for mold that is plenty.

  

So. Mold is what happens when warm, wet air meets a cold surface and is allowed to stay. \[short pause\] Scrubbing changes neither the temperature of the wall nor the water in the air, which is why the same spots come back to the same places every winter.

In Germany the cracked window has a cousin, the tilt window, hinged at the bottom so the top leans into the room a few inches. Plenty of Germans leave it like that for hours and believe they are airing the room. \[slightly amused\] They are, very slowly. A small gap moves almost no air, so the wet air stays where it was, but the cold comes in all day long, right where it does the most harm, along the window frame and down the wall beside it. After a day of good intentions you have the same water in the room and a colder wall to put it on, and you have been heating the street the whole time. The American inch of open window, left like that overnight because the bedroom feels stuffy, is the same physics with a different window.

  

\[matter-of-fact\] The German answer goes the other way, short and wide. There is a single German word for it, and in English it comes out as shock ventilation, which sounds far more dramatic than it is. It is the habit those rental contracts are talking about, and somehow it never made it across the ocean.

  

First the heat goes down. If you have radiators, turn the valves down, and if you have a furnace, turn the thermostat down for these few minutes so it is not running against an open window. Then every window opens all the way, not tilted, not cracked, wide. If you can open windows on opposite sides of the house, do it, because then the air crosses straight through like water through a pipe. \[emphatic\] Five minutes it needs with a draft like that, and ten with windows on one side only. Then you close everything and turn the heat back up, and that is the whole habit. Two or three times a day, in the morning when you get up, because a night of breathing leaves water in a bedroom, again after the cooking, and once more before bed if you can manage it.

  

The first time you do this in January, it will feel a little crazy, every window wide open and frost on the lawn. Do it anyway, and when you close up, put your hand flat on the wall. \[quietly\] It is still warm.

  

So why does five minutes of cold air work when a whole day of it does not? Because of where the heat in a room actually lives. Very little of it is in the air. Most of it is stored in the walls, the floor, and the furniture, the way heat stays in a cast iron pan long after the burner is off, and ten minutes of cold air barely touches it. The air, which carries the water but almost none of the heat, simply gets swapped. The new air comes in cold and holding very little water, warms up against those warm walls, and now it has room to take water on instead of dropping it. The room dries out and the walls stay warm, and that is what those five minutes buy you.

  

\[short pause\] It is a dull habit, and dull is good. A spray works until it dries. A habit works as long as you keep doing it, which is the only honest meaning of forever I can give you.

  

But the habit is only half of it. The other half is seven small house rules, and the first one costs about eight dollars.

  

Rule one. Buy a hygrometer, a little humidity meter, for about eight dollars, and hang it on the wall in the room that worries you most, away from the stove and the shower so the steam does not fool it. What you want to see is somewhere between forty and sixty percent. When it reads above sixty, the bursts that day come sooner and last longer. Watch it once during a burst, too. You should see the number drop while the windows are open, and that drop is water leaving your house. \[thoughtful\] That is all the meter does, and it is enough, because from now on you are not guessing. You read the house the way you read the weather before choosing a coat.

  

Rule two. No cold rooms. Every room in the house stays at sixty to sixty-two degrees Fahrenheit at the very least, and that includes the guest room nobody sleeps in. Closing the vent and shutting the door on a spare room feels like thrift, but it turns that room into the coldest place in the house, and the warm, wet air from everywhere else drifts under the door looking for exactly that. It finds the cold walls and leaves its water there. \[slightly amused\] Sixty-two degrees is not cozy, and it does not have to be. It only has to keep that wall from being the coldest thing the air can find.

  

Rule three is the dresser from the beginning. A big piece of furniture against an outside wall makes a pocket of air behind it that never moves and never warms up, so the wall back there is the coldest spot in the room, and nobody ever looks at it. \[matter-of-fact\] Pull the dresser, the bookshelf, and the headboard two to four inches away from every outside wall, about the width of your hand. Air moves behind them again, the wall warms a little, and the gray strip has nothing left to stand on. The closet on the outside wall that smells like a basement is the same story, coats and boxes packed tight against a cold wall where no air has moved since October. Thin it out, leave that same hand's width at the back, and open the closet door now and then.

  

So. \[slower\] Stop here a moment and look at what we have so far. The burst, the warm spare room, and the gap behind the dresser all keep your walls warm enough that the water has nowhere to land, and the meter tells you when to do more. That was the first half. The second half is the water itself, and how much of it you put into the air without noticing, which is more than you would guess.

  

Rule four is in the kitchen. A pot of boiling water without a lid is a small steam machine aimed at your coldest window, so the lid goes on, and the hood goes on before the water boils rather than after. \[matter-of-fact\] One honest thing about that hood, my friend. If yours only recirculates, pulling the air through a filter and blowing it back into the kitchen, it catches grease but not water, and then the open window after cooking is your real hood.

  

Rule five is the laundry rack. One wet load, hung up to dry inside, gives up to two liters of water to the room, which is a little more than half a gallon, and every drop of it has to land somewhere. If you dry clothes indoors, do it in a room with a window you will actually open, and keep that door shut so the water stays where you can send it out. And if your dryer vents to the outside, this rule is already done for you.

  

\[slightly amused\] Rule six is the bathroom, and here the American house is actually ahead, at least on paper, because you already have the fan. The trouble is that it gets switched off on the way out the door, while the wet tiles, the wet towel, and the shower curtain keep handing water to the room for a long time after. The German rule is the window open or the fan running for twenty minutes after every shower, and the glass and the tiles squeegeed, because every drop the squeegee takes is a drop that never goes into the air. In an American bathroom you do it with a timer switch on the fan, about twenty dollars, so it keeps running for those twenty minutes after you have left. \[warm\] Twenty dollars, twenty minutes, and nothing to remember.

  

Rule seven is the water that comes from outside, and this is the line I promised you at the start. A basement is cool by nature, because its walls sit in the ground, so down there a dehumidifier holding the air under sixty percent does the work the windows cannot. Then, after the next rain, walk once around the outside of your house. If a gutter spills against the wall, or a downspout empties right at the foundation, or the ground slopes toward the house instead of away from it, that wall is getting wet from the outside, and no window in the world dries it from the inside. \[emphatic\] That is a gutter and grading job, and it comes first.

  

And if the spots are already there? Then the size decides, and the German guidance is plain about it. A patch smaller than about half a square meter, roughly five square feet for you, is a job you can do yourself. Put on gloves and a mask and open the window. On hard surfaces like tile, glass, or a vinyl window frame, use seventy percent isopropyl alcohol or hydrogen peroxide on a cloth, one product at a time and never mixed with anything else. \[matter-of-fact\] Alcohol burns, so keep any flame, a candle or a cigarette, out of that room while you work.

  

Bigger than five square feet, the cloth goes back in the drawer and you call a mold remediation company. That is real work, and it runs from about five hundred dollars to six thousand. Six thousand dollars on one side, and on the other an eight-dollar meter and a twenty-dollar timer. \[quietly\] I will just leave those numbers there.

  

Now the bleach under your sink. On porous drywall, chlorine bleach is the wrong bottle. It is mostly water, and drywall drinks it, so the stain you can see turns pale while the water goes into the board behind it, and the wall you were trying to dry ends up wetter than when you started. Whatever you clean with, the cleaning comes after the habit, never before, because a clean wall that stays cold is only a fresh start for the same spots.

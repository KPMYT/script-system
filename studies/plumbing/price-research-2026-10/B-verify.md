## Safety facts: checked against official guidance

### 120°F setting
- CPSC 5098 (read directly): "urges all users to lower their water heaters to 120 degrees Fahrenheit." DOE Energy Saver Guide 2022 (read directly): "Set your water heater temperature to 120ºF, a temperature considered safe for most of the population." PNNL/BASC: EPA recommends 120°F. **Matches.**
- **Easy for a viewer to dispute:**
  1. **Legionella.** A 2025 CPSC child-burn meeting log (search summary) notes that 140°F is recommended by some to control Legionella. DOE's own standards documents mention higher temperatures in the same breath as sediment and Legionella.
  2. **Dishwashers.** CPSC's bathtub study notes 120°F may not work with dishwashers that need hotter water. DOE says 120°F is fine if the dishwasher has its own booster heater.

  Suggested line: "Most homes should use 120. If a dishwasher manual or a health condition calls for hotter water, a plumber can fit a mixing valve so the tank runs hotter but the taps stay safe."

### Scald times
- CPSC (exact, for **adults**, third-degree burns): 150°F two seconds; 140°F **six** seconds; 130°F thirty seconds; 120°F five minutes.
- The ABA chart (2007) says 5 seconds at 140°F.
- **Fix:** the researcher's script line "At 140, ... about five seconds" does not match CPSC. Use "about six seconds" (CPSC), or "five to six seconds" if both are cited.
- CPSC also says most victims are "the elderly and children under the age of five." That is relevant to a 50+ audience: their skin burns faster than the adult figures suggest.

### Savings per 10°F
- No DOE page with "3-5% per 10°F" was found by the researcher or by me. PNNL (a DOE national lab) says "about 5%." Cornell and UF extensions say 3-5%.
- **Fix:** don't say "according to the Department of Energy" with 3-5%. The DOE dollar figure ($36-$61 a year for turning down to 120°F) is the safer DOE-attributed claim.

### Gas leak steps
Checked against PG&E, AGA and SoCalGas (read), and National Grid, Con Edison, Atmos and Piedmont (search summaries). The researcher's four steps are right. Suggested tightening:
1. "Get everyone out now. If you're outside, move away, upwind" (PG&E).
2. "Don't touch light switches, appliances, doorbells, phones or flashlights, don't light anything, and don't start a car" (AGA, PG&E, National Grid: National Grid adds doorbells).
3. "Once you're well away, call 911 and your gas company" (PG&E: 911 first, then the utility; AGA: 911 *or* the utility). Add "don't assume someone else called" (National Grid).
4. "Don't go back in until the gas company or fire department says it's safe, and **let the gas company turn the gas back on and relight your pilots**." PG&E's service reps do this. The researcher's "or a pro" is looser than the utility guidance.
5. Optional, relevant for 50+: "Your nose isn't always reliable. Some people can't smell the gas odor, and it can fade" (SoCalGas). Hissing sounds and dead vegetation over a line are other signs (SoCalGas, Atmos).

Not found in any source checked: "open the windows" and "shut off the gas at the meter yourself." Leave both out.

## Other flags

- **Out of date (pre-2024) in the researcher's file:**
  - Bob Vila repair guide (2023-01-27): #5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 17
  - Bob Vila tankless (2023-10-31)
  - Bob Vila water filtration (2022-08-16)
  - Bob Vila leaking heater (2020)
  - the ABA chart (2007)
  - CPSC 5098 (undated, old). It is still CPSC's current publication, so it is fine to cite.

  None of these is the only support for a figure, but none should be the source named in a script.
- **Today's Homeowner** is Angi data. Don't count it as independent if the researcher adds it later.
- **Tax credits (not checked, from my own knowledge):** a source seen in search says the federal 25C credit (30%, up to $600 for a gas tankless or $2,000 for a heat-pump water heater) ended for equipment placed in service after 2025. If any script mentions a federal tax credit for water heaters, verify it on irs.gov first. A viewer will dispute an expired credit.
- **#5 frequency:** DOE's 2022 guide says "Drain a quart of water from your water tank every 3 months." Manufacturers and Fixr say flush yearly. These are compatible (a quick drain versus a full flush), but a script shouldn't present a yearly flush as the only official advice.
- **#23 method:** Navien allows "a descaling solution or white vinegar." The Rinnai search summary says Rinnai warns against chemical solutions. Script: "use white vinegar or whatever descaler your manufacturer approves; check the manual."
- **#4:** Thumbtack's "around 10 years" for tankless is the outlier against DOE, ENERGY STAR and all four manufacturers. "15 to 20" covers Rheem's lower figure and won't be challenged.
- **#20:** the researcher's table ($150-$400) and script line ($150-$300) disagree with each other. Thumbtack's data ($243-$823) supports the table, not the script line.

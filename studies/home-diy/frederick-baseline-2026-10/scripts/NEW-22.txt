Number four. The e-bike battery. Or the scooter, or the hoverboard somebody got for Christmas, charging in the hallway, in the bedroom, or right by the front door where it got wheeled in. A lithium pack is what lets these things go twenty miles on a charge, and for years it’s fine. \[matter-of-fact\] But a lithium pack does not go quietly when it goes. One cell gets hot enough to set off the cell beside it, that one sets off the next, and the whole pack starts feeding itself. The fire service has a name for it, thermal runaway, and in plain language it means the battery is making its own heat from the inside, so a bucket of water on the outside buys you very little. There’s a jet of flame, a thick white smoke that fills a room in seconds, and it happens faster than anyone expects. \[slower\] Two facts make this a bedroom problem. The failures that hurt people tend to happen on the charger, and they tend to happen at night, hours after the pack was already full. And in the fires that turn deadly, the bike is very often parked across the only path to the door. So the rule is strict. It charges on a hard surface, concrete or tile, never carpet and never a bed, and never in the room where anyone sleeps. \[emphatic\] It charges away from the exit, so the way out is never the thing that’s burning. It charges while you’re awake, and it comes off the charger when it’s full. And it charges on the charger that came with it, not the cheap one off the internet, because a mismatched charger is the most common way a good pack gets pushed into a bad night. The tell is a battery that’s swollen, that hisses, that gives off a sweet chemical smell, or that took a hard fall. That one doesn’t go back on the charger. Number one. The space heater. \[quietly\] I’ll say the hard part first. Heating equipment is one of the leading causes of home fires in this country, and of the people who die in those heating fires, space heaters are behind most of them. Not the furnace or the fireplace, the little box from the hardware store. Ask any fire marshal and you’ll get the same answer. A space heater running in a room with you awake in it is a space heater. Here’s the part that matters. The leading reason these go wrong is distance. Something that burns ends up too close, a bedspread that slides off the bed, a curtain, a pile of laundry, the dog’s bed, a stack of magazines. \[emphatic\] Three feet. That’s the rule. Three feet of clear floor on every side, and I mean hold your arm out once so you know what it looks like, because a foot and a half feels like plenty until you see it. The second reason is the strip, the one I promised to come back to. A heater pulls more current than almost anything in the house, about as much as a toaster running flat out, except it runs for hours, and a power strip was never built to carry that.

\[matter-of-fact\] Ask ten people where house fires start, and nine will tell you the kitchen. They’re right. But ask the people who’ve walked through a house the morning after, and you’ll hear a different question answered: where fires turn deadly. That answer moves down the hall. \[quietly\] It’s the bedroom. Not because the bedroom has the most ways to start a fire, but because it’s the one room where everybody is unconscious when it happens. A fire in the kitchen gets noticed. A fire in the bedroom gets a head start.



I’m Frederick Barnes, and I walk houses with the people responsible for them: young families, grown kids keeping an eye on a parent’s place, first-time buyers living with choices somebody else made. Tonight is one room and ten things that have no business in it, each with the reason it made the list and the swap that takes its place. And the last one on the list was never plugged in and never lit, so stay with me for that one.



Number one. The space heater within arm’s reach of the bed.



\[warm\] Now, a space heater on its own is not a villain. A newer unit with a tip-over switch, sitting on hard floor with open space around it, taking the edge off a cold room while you’re up reading, that’s the machine doing its job. The problem is the distance. Close to the bed means close to bedding, and bedding moves. A comforter slides. A sleeve of the duvet drifts off the edge at two in the morning, and now fabric is resting against a grille built to push out heat.



\[emphatic\] Here’s the enemy for the whole night, and I’ll name it once: heat with nowhere to go. A heater warms the air, and moving air carries that heat away. Lay fabric against it and the air stops moving. The heat stays. It builds into whatever it’s touching, and it keeps building while you sleep.



The fix is free. Three feet of open space on every side. Pace it off, and move the bed or move the heater until you have it. Floor only, never a dresser. And the plug goes straight into the wall, because a heater’s draw is more than any strip should be asked to carry. Keep that thought warm, because strips come back later tonight. If the plug or the cord ever feels hot in your palm, the heater has served its time. Replace it.



A heater warms the room so the bed feels right. The next item skips the room and goes straight for the bed.



Number two. The electric blanket past its prime.



A newer blanket, lying flat, warming the sheets before you climb in, that’s fine. It’s what the thing was built for. \[thoughtful\] The trouble is age and wear. Inside that fabric runs a long line of fine wire, and every wash, every fold, every night of being kicked to the foot of the bed flexes it. Blankets crease in the same spot for years, and the wire creases with them. Insulation thins. A strand breaks partway through. And a damaged spot in a wire runs hotter than the rest of it every time current passes, which is small heat, wrapped in fabric, with nowhere to go.



\[slower\] So tonight, lay yours flat and run your hand slowly across it. You’re feeling for lumps, kinks, anything that doesn’t match the rest. Look at the cord where it meets the controller and where it enters the blanket. A scorch mark, a whiff of hot plastic off the controller, a crease worn shiny, any of those and that blanket has finished its career. And whatever its condition, about ten years is a full working life for one. Keep that ten-year number in your pocket. It comes back at the end of this list, on something that matters even more.



\[short pause\] And the blanket has a smaller cousin in most houses that causes more grief, for a reason most people have exactly backwards.

Number three. The heating pad you fall asleep on.



Twenty minutes on a sore shoulder, sitting up with the lights on, that’s the tool used as designed. \[matter-of-fact\] The backwards part is this: people figure the pad is safer than the blanket because it’s smaller, and it’s the opposite. A pad concentrates the same kind of heat into one patch of fabric, and when you fall asleep on it, your own weight presses it into the mattress. Trapped on both sides. Heat with nowhere to go, for six or seven hours instead of twenty minutes. Every tired-wire failure the blanket can have, the pad has too, only folded tighter and pressed harder.



So the rule is simple. A heating pad works on you, never under you, and never while you sleep. Use it before bed, use the timer if it has one, and if yours is old enough that it can’t shut itself off, that’s your sign. Check the cord, check the controller, and the moment the cover shows a scorch or the plastic smells cooked, its service is over. The relief it gives you is real. Collect it awake.



A heating pad spends hours putting out gentle heat. The next item is its opposite: a box that stores a full day’s worth of energy and, on its worst day, gives it all back in about a minute.



Number four. The e-bike battery charging in the bedroom. Same seat at the table for the scooter and the hoverboard.



The battery itself is a marvel, and a healthy one, on the charger it was sold with, is generally well-mannered. \[matter-of-fact\] But a lithium battery doesn’t fail the way a toaster fails. When one cell inside overheats, from a drop, a cheap replacement charger, damage you can’t see, it heats its neighbors, and they heat theirs, and the thing starts feeding on itself. That is not a smolder you wake up to. That is fast fire and a great deal of ugly smoke, from an object that’s very often parked between your bed and your door.



\[emphatic\] So the bedroom is off the list for charging, full stop. Charge it on a hard surface, somewhere you’re awake and nearby, away from the way out, and only ever on its own maker’s charger. And learn its tells: a case that swells, a hiss, a sweet chemical smell, warmth when it’s been sitting idle. Any one of those and that battery is finished. It goes to a battery drop-off, not the kitchen trash.



Of course, that battery was plugged into something. And in most bedrooms, the something is our next item.



Number five. The power strip behind the headboard, or under the bed.



The strip itself is honest. It carries what it’s asked to carry, and most of what we ask, a lamp, a clock, a phone, it carries easily. The trouble is where it lives and what gets added to it. Behind a headboard, a strip disappears. Dust settles over it like a quilt. The dust ruffle leans against it, air stops moving around it, and nobody has laid eyes on it since the bed was assembled. \[slightly amused\] I’ve pulled strips out of spots like that and had to guess what color they’d started out. Then somebody plugs a heater or a hair dryer into it, and now real current is crossing connections nobody can see, under a blanket of dust, with nowhere for the warmth to leave.



Tonight, pull yours into the open. Wipe it down, look at every socket, and set it where air and eyes can reach it. Anything that makes heat goes straight to the wall, and it’s one strip per outlet, always, because a strip plugged into a strip is the same gamble with more seats. If the wall outlet behind it feels warm, or holds a plug loosely, stop right there. Finding that is your job. Opening that wall belongs to a licensed electrician.



And when the strip’s cord won’t reach the outlet, people solve it with the next item. Then they hide the solution under a rug.



Number six. The extension cord under the rug.



An extension cord in the open, the right thickness for the job, for a weekend, that’s a tool doing temporary work. \[thoughtful\] The word that matters is temporary. Under a rug, a cord becomes permanent, and it picks up two problems at once. First, the rug is a blanket for the floor: whatever small working warmth that cord makes has no air to hand it to. Second, feet. Every step across that stripe grinds the insulation a little flatter, and a wire damaged where no one can see it runs hot where no one can feel it.



The free fix happens tonight. Pull it out from under there and route it along the wall, in the open, where a problem has to show itself. The tells are a warm stripe in the rug, or a jacket gone flat and shiny from being walked on, and either one retires the cord. And if the room simply can’t run without an extension cord, the answer isn’t a longer cord. It’s a licensed electrician and one more outlet.



Everything so far has been hiding its heat. Number seven doesn’t hide a thing.



Number seven. The lit candle on the nightstand.



Candles are old company, and I understand the pull: the light, the scent, the end of the day. In a dining room, with people awake around it, a candle on a plate behaves itself. The bedroom is different for one reason. The bedroom is where people drift off, and a flame doesn’t need anything to go wrong to start a fire. It already is one, politely waiting for reach. A curtain drifts on the warmth of its own rising air. A paperback gets set down too close. \[slightly amused\] A cat decides the nightstand is a runway.



\[warm\] The swap here is the easiest on the whole list: flameless candles. An LED flame, a warm flicker, most with a timer that puts itself to bed, and about fifteen dollars for a set. The scent is the only thing you give up, and scent has plenty of ways into a room that don’t involve fire. Keep the real ones for the dinner table, with one house rule attached: if a flame is burning, someone awake is in the room with it.



From a flame you lit on purpose, to a bulb that ran nearly as hot. And for a stretch of years, almost nobody realized it.



Number eight. The halogen torchiere, the tall floor lamp with the open dish that throws light at the ceiling.



\[thoughtful\] If you furnished a place in the nineties, you probably owned one. They were cheap, they were bright, and they were everywhere. The catch sat up in that dish: a tube-shaped halogen bulb running hundreds of watts and far hotter than the bulbs everyone was used to. Hot enough that a curtain drifting into the bowl, or a shirt tossed over the top, didn’t need long. Enough bedrooms burned that the safety standard itself got rewritten. Guards went over the bulb, the allowed wattage came down, and college dorms started turning the old ones away at the door.



If one of these is still standing in your bedroom, it has outlived its era. Retire it with honors. The swap is an LED torchiere, twenty-five to forty dollars, the same wash of light up the ceiling, and a housing that stays cool enough to rest your palm near. That’s the bedroom test for any lamp, really: after an hour on, if you can’t comfortably hold a hand close to it, it’s the wrong lamp for a sleeping room.



A hot bulb has to wait for fuel to find it. The next item carries fire and fuel in the same hand, and it is the oldest entry on any list like this one.



Number nine. The ashtray on the nightstand, and everything it stands for.



\[quietly\] I’m not here to talk anyone out of a habit. What happens in your home is yours. But ask anyone in the fire service for the oldest rule they know, and you’ll hear some version of the same sentence: nobody smokes where they sleep. Here’s why it has held for generations. \[slower\] A dropped ember doesn’t blaze. It burrows into a mattress or a fold of bedding and smolders, a slow burn with no flame to see, running for a long time and making smoke the whole while, doing its work on a sleeping person before anything in the room looks like fire. It begins at exactly the moment nobody is awake to catch it.



So the rule is about location, not judgment. Smoking happens where you’re upright, in another room, or better, outside. The ashtray is deep, it lives off the nightstand, and nothing leaves it for a trash can until it has been drowned in water. Every time, including the nights you’re certain it was out.



And every ember, every hot grille, every cooking bulb on this list has been waiting on one thing: something to land on. Which brings up the last item, the one I promised you, never plugged in and never lit.



\[short pause\] Number ten. The pile.



Every bedroom has one. The chair that collects clothes, the laundry that didn’t make the hamper, the boxes that moved in for a week two years ago. On its own, a pile is just mess, and mess never burned a house down. Location is what promotes it. A pile against a baseboard heater, against the back of the space heater from number one, against the base of a floor lamp, that’s stored fuel hand-delivered to a heat source. It’s the enemy from the start of tonight finally meeting its other half: heat with nowhere to go, handed something to spend itself on.



And there’s a second spot a pile has no business being: the path. Bed to door, bed to window. In a fire you’ll be moving through dark and smoke on a budget of seconds, and a knee-high pile in that line takes seconds you don’t have. \[emphatic\] So, tonight, three feet of nothing around anything that makes heat, the same rule from number one closing out the list, and a clear, empty lane from the bed to both ways out.



\[warm\] That’s what leaves the room. Here’s what stays, four things that belong within arm’s reach of anyone asleep. A smoke alarm on the ceiling, tested recently enough that you remember the sound, and here’s the ten-year number I asked you to keep: it applies here hardest of all, because a detector past ten years old is furniture, and it gets replaced, not trusted. A flashlight in the nightstand drawer, because fires take the lights with them. Your phone on the nightstand, not across the room and not under your pillow, since it’s your light and your call for help in the same hand. And shoes beside the bed, because the floor after a fire starts is no place for bare feet, and getting out fast beats getting out carefully. \[slower\] An alarm, a light, a phone, and shoes: four quiet things, and any one of them is worth more at three in the morning than everything this list just carried out.

Number seven. The house at the edge of the trees.



\[matter-of-fact\] Let me give you the ordinary case first. Living with woods or grass or dry brush at your back fence is one of the nicer ways to live, and the house itself is probably built just fine. The danger isn’t the wall of flame you see on the news. Ask anyone who’s walked a neighborhood the morning after a wildfire and they’ll tell you the houses mostly went from the small stuff. Embers. Little coals riding the wind a mile or more ahead of the fire, landing in a gutter full of dry leaves, blowing into an attic vent, tucking into the gap under a wooden deck, settling into the bark mulch piled right up against the siding. \[emphatic\] The fire doesn’t have to reach your house. Your house just has to be ready to catch.



\[warm\] So here’s what to look at tonight, and you can do most of it from the back window. Where does the brush stop and your yard start? The first few feet out from the walls want to be bare dirt, gravel, stone, concrete, or green and watered, not dry mulch and not a stack of firewood leaning on the siding. Gutters cleaned out. Vents screened with fine metal mesh so an ember can’t get through. Anything under the deck that burns, pulled out. None of that costs much and most of it costs nothing. And if you live in one of those neighborhoods, your smoke alarms matter more than almost anybody’s, because by the time you smell it, the fire may already be inside the attic. \[short pause\] Hold onto that. I’m going to keep coming back to it.



Number six is for the family who moved out where it’s quiet.



\[warm\] I understand the appeal. Long driveway, no neighbors close enough to hear you, the kind of dark at night that people in town forget exists. The house may be new, the wiring may be perfect, and you still carry more risk than a house in the suburbs for one plain reason. \[slower\] Distance. A house fire doesn’t grow in a straight line. It feeds on itself, every minute hotter than the last, and the difference between a crew arriving in six minutes and a crew arriving in twenty is usually the difference between a damaged room and a lost house. Rural departments do an honest, hard job, and a lot of them are volunteers driving from home to the station before they ever drive to you. The clock starts when somebody calls, and if everybody in the house is asleep, nobody calls until a neighbor sees the glow.



\[matter-of-fact\] Which means out there, early detection is the whole game. The alarm is doing the job those twenty extra minutes would have done. The ones that talk to each other, so a detector going off in the garage wakes you in the bedroom, are worth the little extra, and even a plain one is twenty-some dollars at the hardware store. Check the driveway tonight too, and I mean that literally. Can a big truck get in and turn around? Is your house number readable from the road in the dark? Any fire chief in a rural county will tell you the same thing, and it still gets ignored every year. \[quietly\] A crew can’t save what it can’t find.



Number five. The house that runs on a space heater, or a wood stove.



\[warm\] A heater isn’t the enemy, and I’ll say that plainly because I know how many of you run one every night. The enemy is heat with nowhere to go. A space heater throws real warmth a few feet in every direction, and whatever sits inside those few feet is slowly cooking. A curtain that drifts. A comforter hanging off the bed. A basket of laundry on the floor. Nobody sets out to run it till morning. You get warm, and the next thing you know it’s three in the morning and the quilt that was two feet away at ten o’clock is touching the grille. If heaters carry most of your heat through the winter, you have more of those nights, and more of those nights is the risk.



\[matter-of-fact\] Three feet of clear floor around it, every direction, every time. Plugged straight into the wall, never a power strip and never an extension cord, because a heater pulls more current than almost anything else in the house and a thin cord turns into a second heater under the rug. Off when you leave the room, off when you sleep, and if it’s older and the cord has gone stiff or the plug gets hot, it’s done its service. Retire it.



\[thoughtful\] Wood stoves are a different animal, and the danger lives in the pipe. Burn wood that isn’t fully dry, or let a fire smolder low all night, and a tarry crust called creosote builds up on the inside of the chimney. It builds and builds. One hot fire on the wrong day and that crust lights, and now you have a fire inside the wall of your house burning at a temperature no stove was built for. The fix is a chimney sweep once a year, dry seasoned wood, and a hot clean burn instead of a long choking one. \[quietly\] And the ash. Ash looks dead for days and can hold a live coal for a week. Metal bucket, metal lid, outside, away from the house, never a paper bag on the porch.



Those first three live out in the open where you can see them, and the last four are different, because those live in the walls, in the panel, in the year the house was built, and that’s where number one, the one the insurance companies walk away from, is waiting.

Number four is for anybody who has never once opened their breaker panel.



\[matter-of-fact\] Walk to it, the gray metal door in the basement or the garage or the hallway closet, and look at it the way you’d look at a smoke alarm. Every breaker in there has exactly one job. When a circuit carries more current than it should, the breaker trips and cuts the power before the wire in the wall gets hot enough to matter. That’s the whole bargain. You trust that little switch to fail safe, every time, for decades, and most of them do. The problem is the panel that’s been in service since before your kids were born, or before you were, because a breaker is a mechanical thing, and some older designs have a record of not tripping when they should. \[slightly amused\] I’m not going to stand here and name brands, and you don’t need me to. Any electrician who works in older houses knows which panels make them slow down and look closer.



\[warm\] What you can do tonight is simple. Open the door, keep your hands off everything inside, and just look and listen. A breaker that feels warm to the back of your hand. Scorch marks or a brown smudge around one. A buzz or a crackle that wasn’t there last year. The smell of hot plastic. A breaker that trips over and over, or the one that has never tripped once in fifteen years on a circuit you know you’ve overloaded. \[sighs\] I’ve seen a breaker get reset so many times the family stopped counting, and nobody ever asked it what it was trying to say. Any of that, and the panel is telling you something. If the panel is thirty or forty years old and nobody has ever had it inspected, that alone is worth the call. A licensed electrician will look it over for a couple hundred dollars, give or take, and that’s money against the one piece of equipment that’s supposed to stop everything else on this list.



\[thoughtful\] Here’s the enemy for the rest of the way down, and I want you to carry it with you. The warm connection. A connection that’s a little loose runs a little hotter every time the load comes on, and it never fails all at once. It fails over years, and the only warning it gives is a few degrees you’d never notice unless you went looking. Keep that in your pocket. Number three. The manufactured home built before the summer of nineteen seventy-six.



\[matter-of-fact\] Plenty of people live in manufactured homes and live in them well, and a newer one is built to a federal standard that covers the wiring, the heating, the smoke alarms, and the way out. That standard took effect in June of nineteen seventy-six. Before it, what everybody called a mobile home was built to whatever the factory decided, and the record on fire deaths in those older units is worse, and nobody in the fire service argues the point. Smaller rooms, thinner walls, more of the house made of material that burns, and sometimes one door at the far end of a hallway that’s the only way out.



\[warm\] Tonight, find out which one you’re standing in. A home built under the standard carries a red metal certification label on the outside, usually near a corner of the back wall, and a paper data plate inside, often on the inside of a kitchen cabinet door, in a closet, or near the panel, with the date it was built. No label, no plate, or a date before seventy-six, and you treat the place the way you’d treat an old house with old wiring. Alarms in every sleeping room, a second way out of every bedroom, which usually means a window that actually opens, and anything electrical that feels warm or smells wrong goes straight to a licensed electrician who works on these homes. If it’s your mother’s place, this is the visit to do it on.



Number two is for everybody in a house from the late sixties or early seventies.



\[matter-of-fact\] Between about nineteen sixty-five and nineteen seventy-three, copper got expensive, and a lot of builders ran the branch wiring, the cable that goes to your outlets and switches and lights, in aluminum instead. Aluminum carries current just fine. The trouble is at the ends. Aluminum expands and shrinks with heat more than copper does, so every time a circuit warms up and cools down, the wire under that screw terminal in the outlet box moves a little, and over years it creeps loose. Loose means resistance, resistance means heat, heat means it creeps looser. \[emphatic\] Remember the warm connection I told you to carry. This is where it lives. The Consumer Product Safety Commission put out a warning on these homes decades ago, that the connections in them are far more likely to reach fire conditions than copper ones, and that warning has never been taken back.



How you check tonight. Open the panel door again, flashlight on, hands off, and look at the cables coming in. Copper is copper colored. Aluminum is silver, and the plastic jacket on the cable usually has the letters A L printed along it, or the word aluminum spelled out. You can sometimes see it in the attic or the basement where cable runs exposed, too. Around the house, the tells are the ones you’d expect. Outlet covers warm to the palm, lights that flicker when nothing has changed, a faint smell of hot plastic near an outlet, an outlet that quit working for no reason. \[thoughtful\] I’ve laid a hand on an outlet cover that had no business being warm, in a living room that looked perfectly fine from the couch. If you find aluminum, don’t panic and don’t touch a thing. There are approved repairs for every single connection in the house, and all of them are a licensed electrician’s job. Your job ends at finding it. The box stays closed until somebody with a license opens it.



Number one. The house wired before about nineteen forty.



\[slower\] This is the one I teased at the top, and I’ll say it plainly. If your house still has its original wiring from the twenties or thirties, a lot of insurance companies will decline to cover it, and the ones that will may want it gone first. That’s not them being difficult. It’s called knob and tube, and it was good work for its day, done by hand, and its day is over. Picture two separate wires, one hot and one neutral, run apart from each other through the joists, held off the wood on little white porcelain knobs and passed through the framing inside little porcelain tubes. The wires are copper wrapped in rubber and cloth, and that rubber is now closing in on a hundred years old. It dries, it cracks, it flakes off in your hand. There is no ground wire anywhere in the system, which is why those houses have the two-prong outlets, and the whole thing was designed to shed its heat into open air.



\[emphatic\] And here’s the part that matters. Somewhere in the last century, somebody insulated that attic. Blew in cellulose or laid down batts right over the top of wiring that was built to breathe, and now the heat has nowhere to go. Trapped heat again, sitting on insulation older than anybody in the house. Add the load a modern family puts on it, and the warm connection is everywhere at once.



\[matter-of-fact\] Tonight, take the flashlight up to the attic hatch and down to the basement ceiling and look along the joists. White porcelain knobs with a wire running through them. Porcelain tubes where a wire passes through a beam. Old cloth-covered wire that looks like a shoelace. If you see any of it, don’t touch it and don’t assume it’s dead, because it very often isn’t, and some of it may be carrying the lights you turned on tonight. Call a licensed electrician, tell them what you found, and ask them to tell you honestly how much is still live and what it would take to replace it. Nobody with a screwdriver and a free Saturday should touch it. That’s a license and a permit.

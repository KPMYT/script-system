The smoke alarm on your ceiling has one job, and with any luck you've never heard it do it. So how would you know if it still can? The National Fire Protection Association looked at years of home fires where an alarm was right there on the ceiling and never made a sound. Over and over, the reason was the battery. Dead, missing, or pulled because it kept going off over a frying pan. But a ten-minute round, once a month, catches that kind of failure on an ordinary Saturday, long before a real night tests it.



I'm Frederick Barnes, and if you're the one in your house who notices the dripping faucet and the flickering bulb, this round is going to be yours. By the end, you'll know it by heart, and you'll know exactly where every alarm in your house stands instead of just hoping.



Think about what that alarm actually is. It's a plastic disc about the size of a saucer, screwed to the ceiling the week somebody moved in, and it has hung up there through every holiday, every heat wave, and every birthday cake with too many candles on it. It never asks you for anything. It doesn't hum like the refrigerator or blink at you like the internet router, so it slides right out of your mind.



And that's the trouble with it. A smoke alarm that can't do its job looks exactly like one that can. I call that dead air, an alarm that's up there, looks ready, and would give your family nothing on the one night it's needed.



There's an idea I lean on a lot on this channel called the Hazard Chain. A house fire is almost never a freak accident. It's usually three things lining up in plain sight, a piece of faulty equipment, an unsafe habit, and a warning sign nobody acted on. Your smoke alarm is supposed to be the warning when that chain lines up somewhere else in the house. But a neglected alarm can carry all three links on its own.



The faulty equipment is the alarm itself, getting older up there, its sensor wearing out while dust, cobwebs, and the odd coat of ceiling paint clog the little slots smoke has to get through. The unsafe habit is one just about every household has been tempted by. Dinner's smoking, the alarm is shrieking, somebody climbs up on a kitchen chair, and the battery comes out, and everybody means to put it back. And the ignored warning sign is that single chirp, every half a minute or so, that starts when a battery runs low. Plenty of families live with it for a week, and some pull the battery just to make it stop.



Which leads to something a little backwards. The alarm in your house that's made the most noise over the years is often the one most likely to be silent tonight. Keep the one nearest your kitchen in mind, because we're going to pay it a visit.



Now, maybe you're one of the good ones, and you press the test button every so often. Keep doing that. But it's worth knowing exactly what that beep proves, because it's less than it sounds like. When you press that button, the alarm runs a quick check on itself and sounds its horn. If it screams at you, you've learned two things, that there's power in it and that the horn works.



What the button can't tell you is how old the sensor inside is. It can't tell you whether the alarm is hanging somewhere smoke will actually reach it, or whether the people asleep down the hall, behind closed doors, would hear it at all. An alarm can pass that button every month and still let you down in any one of those three ways.



So the round goes past the button. And on the back of every one of those alarms is a date you've probably never read, and before we're done, you'll see why it can matter more than the battery.

The round is five moves, each one a plain verb, and a sixth that the other five exist for. You'll count, press, listen, look, and read, and then you'll finish at a door. Pick a day you won't forget, like the first Saturday of the month or the day the rent goes out, and set a repeating reminder on your phone. In an average house the round takes about ten minutes, and the first one takes longer, because the first round is where you learn your house.



Count every alarm.



Walk the whole house, top to bottom, and count your alarms, then count the places that ought to have one. Fire safety guidance is plain on this. You want one inside every bedroom, one in the hallway outside the bedrooms, and at least one on every level of the house, basement included. They belong on the ceiling or high on a wall close to it, and away from windows, doors, and the vents that push air around, because a draft can carry smoke right past them.



The first time through, plenty of houses come up short somewhere. A bedroom that was a home office when you moved in. A basement nobody thinks of as living space. A finished attic that quietly turned into somebody's room. Write down every gap, because each one is a room where smoke could build for a while before anything says a word, and every gap on that list gets an alarm before next month's round.



Count the batteries along with the alarms, because from the floor, an empty alarm and a loaded one look the same. Start with the one nearest the kitchen, the one I asked you to keep in mind. It went off over a skillet one night, somebody climbed up and pulled the battery, and that battery has been rolling around the junk drawer ever since. That's dead air with a perfectly good excuse.



I understand why it happens. But that alarm going off over dinner was the alarm working. It smelled smoke and said so. The real trouble is usually where it's hanging, because an alarm too close to the stove will complain about every seared steak, and that's why the standard advice is to keep smoke alarms at least ten feet from a cooking appliance.



So the fix is never the battery. When dinner sets it off, open a window, run the fan over the stove, wave a dish towel under the alarm, or press the hush button if yours has one, which quiets it for a few minutes and then rearms it by itself. If it keeps happening, move the alarm instead of silencing it. On a battery model, that's a step stool and a couple of screws. If it's wired into the house, moving it is a job for a licensed electrician.



Counting tells you they're up there. Whether a single one of them will make a sound is a different question, and that's the next move.



Press and hold each test button.



Every alarm, every month, not just the one in the hallway you can reach without a stool. Press the test button and hold it until the alarm runs through its full pattern, three loud beeps, a pause, then three more. A quick tap sometimes gets you a single chirp, and a single chirp doesn't tell you much, so hold it longer than feels polite.



If you can't reach, the end of a broom handle works on most test buttons. If you'd rather be up close, use a sturdy step stool, or a ladder with somebody holding it, and stay off the desk chair. A rolling chair has its own ideas about where you're headed.



If your alarms are linked, either wired together or connected wirelessly, pressing one should set off every one of them, and that's a test in itself. Stand in the hallway and count the voices. If one stays quiet while the rest go off, that one has a problem, and if they're wired into the house, a licensed electrician should find out why.



A loud, steady pattern is a pass. A weak horn, a slow or crackly one, or no sound at all is a fail. A little blinking light only tells you there's some power in there, not that there's enough to push that horn, so don't let a blinking light talk you into a pass.



On an alarm with a battery you can change, put in a fresh one, the kind the label on the alarm asks for, and test again. If it still won't scream, retire that alarm. On a sealed alarm, the kind with a battery built in to last about ten years, there's nothing to swap, so a failed test means the whole alarm gets replaced. And even when they pass, give every alarm with a changeable battery a fresh one at least once a year, on a weekend that comes around anyway, like the one when the clocks change in the fall.



A loud horn in the hallway is a pass for the alarm. It isn't a pass for the bedrooms yet.



Listen from the bedrooms.



This is the move that gets skipped, and it matters most to the people you're doing all of this for. Once a month, have somebody press the hallway alarm while you stand inside each bedroom with the door shut, then trade places. If someone sleeps with a fan going or a white noise machine on, turn those on too, so the room sounds the way it really does in the middle of the night.



A closed bedroom door is one of the best things you can put between your family and a fire, because it slows down smoke and heat. But a solid door also swallows sound. An alarm that's ear-splitting in the hallway can come through a shut door as something easy to sleep through, and from where you're lying, an alarm you can't hear is just more dead air.



And fire safety researchers keep finding that children can sleep right through a smoke alarm, and so can some older adults and anybody who sleeps heavy.



The fix starts with an alarm inside every bedroom, which you already counted for. Then it's alarms that talk to each other, so when one goes off, they all do. If your house isn't set up that way, there are alarms that link up wirelessly without any new wiring. If you'd rather have them wired into the house, that's a licensed electrician's job from start to finish. And if anyone in your home, or in your parents' home, is hard of hearing, there are alarms built for that, with a bright strobe light and a pad that shakes the bed. That one's worth bringing along on your next visit.



With kids, teach them the sound ahead of time. Press the button with them standing right there, tell them what it means, and tell them exactly what you want them to do when they hear it.



While you're listening, learn your alarm's two voices. The big one, beeps in threes with a pause between, means smoke, and it means get out. The small one, a single short chirp every half a minute or so, means the alarm needs something, a fresh battery, or on a sealed alarm, retirement.



That chirp has a habit of starting in the middle of the night. I've stood on a step stool in the dark, half asleep, trying to work out which alarm was doing it, and I don't recommend it. There's a plain reason for the timing. A house is coolest in the small hours, and a battery that's already running low has a harder time pushing out power when it's cold. It slips under the line, and the alarm speaks up. Then the house warms back up by breakfast, the chirping stops, and it's easy to decide the problem fixed itself. It didn't.



A chirp that quits by morning still means a battery on its way out, so change it that same day, before the next cold night gets to it. And never pull the battery to stop the noise and leave it out, because that's the unsafe habit all over again, and that's how dead air ends up over somebody's bed.



Now, an alarm can sound perfect and still have its doorway half blocked, which brings you to the next move.



Look closely at each alarm.



Get your eyes right up to the little openings around the edge. Smoke has to drift in through those to reach the sensor inside, so anything that clogs them can slow the alarm down.



Dust and cobwebs are the usual ones. Then there's paint, from the time the ceiling got a fresh coat and somebody rolled right over the alarm instead of around it. There's tape, there are stickers, and there's the plastic bag or shower cap somebody pulled over it during a renovation and never took back off. An alarm wearing a shower cap is just a decoration.



For dust, run the soft brush attachment on your vacuum gently over the openings, as often as the alarm's instructions say. Don't spray cleaner into it, and never paint it. If paint has already sealed it, no amount of scrubbing will open it back up, so replace it.



Clean openings and a loud horn still can't tell you how old the sensor is, and that's the date I promised you.



Read the date on the back.



Twist each alarm off its mounting base, just this once, and turn it over. Somewhere on the back is the date it was made, sometimes printed, sometimes stamped into the plastic, sometimes on a little sticker. A wired one will usually stay hanging by its short plug while you read it. Go by that date, the day it was made, rather than the day somebody put it up.



Count ten years from it. This is one rule fire safety groups don't argue about. A smoke alarm gets replaced at ten years, even if it still beeps beautifully on the button, because the parts inside wear out, and a passing beep won't always tell you.



If you can't find a date at all, or you can't make it out, treat that alarm as past due. And if you just bought the house, don't assume a thing about the alarms. They came with the place, same as the wiring, and you don't know their story.



There's a trick so you only take them down once. With a marker, write the replace-by year on the side of the alarm, big enough to see from the top of the step stool, and from then on, reading the date is just a glance during the round.



When that year comes, replace the whole alarm, not just the battery. A fresh battery in a worn-out alarm is a new coat of paint on a rotten fence post. And if it's wired into the house, have a licensed electrician do the swap.



Every move so far has been about the alarm. But an alarm only hands you a warning, and what your family does with that warning is the part no alarm can do for you. That's why the round ends at a door.



Leave the house on purpose.



Finish the round by standing in each bedroom and finding two ways out. The door is one. The second is usually a window, and it has to be one that actually opens, isn't painted shut, and isn't blocked by a dresser somebody moved last spring. If that window is upstairs, think hard about how a person gets down from there, and if you're not sure, call your local fire department and ask.



Two ways out of every room is about as old as fire safety advice gets. The National Fire Protection Association once built an entire Fire Prevention Week around it, under the line every second counts.



Then choose a meeting spot outside, something that doesn't move, like the mailbox, a neighbor's porch light, or one particular tree out front. Everybody goes there and stays there, so you can count heads without anybody running back inside to look for someone who's already out.



Decide now who helps whom. A baby can't get out alone, and neither can a parent who needs a walker or who sleeps without their hearing aids in. Put a name next to each of them, so on the real night nobody stops in the hallway to work out who's got the toddler.



Close doors behind you on the way out if you can, because a shut door slows the fire down for anyone still inside. And call nine-one-one from the meeting spot, not from the hallway.



Twice a year, turn the round into a practice run. Set the alarm off with the test button, let everyone follow the plan from their own room, and have each person at least open their second way out so you know it works, while you stand by the mailbox and watch who comes out where. The first practice is usually a little messy, and that's exactly why you want it on a Saturday afternoon. Then, if that alarm ever does its one job for real, the only thing anybody in your house has to do is the thing they've already practiced.

The answer is the first five feet.



Now, here's what an ember actually is, because I think most people picture it wrong.



An ember isn't fire arriving. It's fire mailing itself ahead. A piece of burning bark or a chunk of wood breaks off, the wind takes it, and it lands somewhere the fire hasn't reached yet. CAL FIRE puts it plainly: most homes that ignite in a wildfire ignite from embers landing on or around the house, not from flames touching it. The figure fire agencies keep repeating is right around 9 out of 10.



And these things travel. Not across the yard. A mile or more out ahead of the fire front, in wind that in the test chamber runs between 30 and 55 miles an hour.



This next piece is the one the whole video runs on.



Embers don't fall like rain. They behave like snow.



Snow doesn't land evenly. Snow piles against anything that blocks the wind. Fence lines, doorways, the low side of a parked car. Embers do the exact same thing, and I want you to have a word for it, because we're coming back to it all night. Call it the drift.



The drift finds the base of your walls. It finds the inside corner where two walls meet. It finds the step at your front door. It finds the space under your deck. It finds your gutters.



And it sits there, and it glows, and it waits for something to burn.



Which is why the fire agencies and the research people land in the same place: the first five feet out from the house. Some call it Zone Zero. Some call it the ember resistant zone. Same five feet. The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety has tested it in the lab and gone back through real burned neighborhoods afterward, and the conclusion doesn't wobble. Taking everything that burns out of that first five feet is the most effective thing a homeowner can do.



Not the trees 100 feet out. Those matter too. But this is the one.



So let's go stand in it. Walk out your front door and take two normal steps. That's about five feet. Everything inside that line is what we're dealing with.



And the first thing most of you are standing on is bark mulch.



I know why it's there. It's cheap, it holds moisture, it keeps the weeds down, and it makes a bed look finished. Nobody ever laid mulch against a foundation while thinking about fire.



But look at what mulch physically is. Wood, dried out, shredded into small pieces, spread thin with air running all through it. If somebody handed you a sack of tinder and asked you to arrange it for the easiest possible ignition, you would build a mulch bed.



In that chamber, the fire ran through the wood mulch and stopped dead the moment it hit the rock. That's the whole finding. Fire went until it ran out of things to burn.



So the fix. Nothing that burns in the first five feet. Gravel, crushed rock, decomposed granite, pavers, concrete, or plain dirt. Any of those. Pull the bark back to the five foot line and put something down that won't light.



And if this isn't the month you're buying gravel, here's the free version, and it works tonight. Rake it out. The bark, the leaves, the needles, the dead grass, all of it out of that first five feet and into a bag. Bare ground doesn't carry fire.



The tell is about as simple as it gets. If you can rake it, an ember can light it.

The thing standing in your five feet that you aren't counting is your fence.



It doesn't feel like part of the house. It's yard. It's the property line. It came with the place. But follow it with your eyes and watch where it ends, because on most houses a wood fence doesn't stop short. It runs right up and bolts into the siding.



At that point it isn't a fence. It's a wooden ramp with one end attached to your wall.



The institute tested exactly that. A combustible fence section attached to the home, put in front of flame, could carry fire straight into the structure. And a fence is a gift to the drift, because it's a long low wall standing sideways in the wind, catching embers along its entire length.



The fix is only the last five feet. You don't have to replace the fence. The final stretch before it meets the house needs to be something that won't burn. A metal gate. A masonry section. Or a break, where the wood stops short and something noncombustible bridges the gap.



Go out and walk your fence line to the house, and put your hand on the last board before the wall. If that's wood, you found it.



And where a section is bolted into the wall, that's work for a fence contractor, not something to improvise on a weekend.



Around to the deck.



The deck itself isn't the problem. Decks are fine.



The problem is underneath it. Think back to the drift. Anywhere the wind slows down is somewhere embers collect, and the space under a deck is a wind shadow with a roof over it. Embers go in there and they stay.



In the testing, embers lit wood mulch under a deck and melted the vinyl lattice, and even then the fire didn't spread underneath. The reason was plain. That space had been kept clear. There was nothing under there to take.



So it all comes out. The storage tubs. The firewood. The leftover lumber from the project you'll get to. The bags of potting soil. The pool noodles. The patio cushions you shoved under there last October. Out, and either into the garage or 30 feet from the house.



Then look at the deck surface. Every deck has gaps between the boards, and every fall those gaps pack with needles and leaves that never quite blow away. An ember lands on the boards, rolls into the gap, and lands right on top of the needles in the gap. Sweep them out. A shop vac does it in ten minutes.



If you have to crouch down to see into a space, that's exactly the sort of space the drift likes.



Now straighten up and look at what's actually touching your walls.



This is the part that gets people, because none of it looks like fuel. It looks like a house being lived in.



The doormat. The patio cushions stacked by the back door. The recycling bin. The broom leaning in the corner. The firewood stacked against the siding, because that's the convenient place for it. The plastic trash can. The propane tank for the grill.



Every one of those either burns or melts, and every one of them is parked in the exact spot the drift piles up.



In the testing, a wood front door ignited from embers accumulating against it. Nothing dramatic came first. Embers just built up on the step, the way they always do, and eventually the door took.



So here's the rule, and it's short. Nothing that burns touches the wall. Cushions come inside or go into a bin with a lid. The doormat comes up when there's a watch on. Firewood goes out to 30 feet, not against the siding. The grill and the propane come off the wall.



Same for the plants. Nothing touching the structure. No branches hanging over the roof. Nothing dead inside the five feet. That dried out shrub by the front window you keep meaning to deal with is as good an ember catcher as anything on your property, and pulling it out is free.



The five feet is the ground game, and it's the most important thing you'll do all week. But embers don't only land on the ground. They also look for a way in.



Ask anyone who's walked a neighborhood after a wildfire and they'll point at the same three spots. Vents, gutters, and the roof.



Vents first.



Your vents are supposed to be open. That is their entire job. An attic that can't breathe gets moisture and rot, so nothing about this is about sealing your house up.



But a vent moves air, and embers ride air. One goes through the screen, lands on dust, on the paper backing of a batt of insulation, on a cardboard box of Christmas decorations, and now there's a fire in your attic while the outside of your house is still untouched. That's the inside out fire people describe afterward, and there's nothing mysterious about it. It came through a vent.



The fix is mesh. Corrosion resistant metal mesh, one eighth of an inch openings, over every vent you've got. Attic, gable, soffit, crawl space, foundation. California's wildfire building code sets one eighth as the limit in wildland urban interface areas, and the Wildfire Prepared Home standard goes tighter, at one sixteenth or a vent tested and listed for ember intrusion.



Two things people get wrong here. Quarter inch mesh, which is what a lot of older houses came with, is too coarse. Embers go straight through it. And never fiberglass or plastic screen, because it melts, and the second it melts you've got an open hole.



One caution, and it's a real one. Some of the openings on your house aren't ventilation, they're exhaust. A furnace flue. A dryer vent. A radon system. You don't screen those. If you aren't certain which is which, that's a phone call, not a guess.



Gutters next.



A gutter is a long open trough running the whole perimeter of your roof, and by September it's holding an entire season of dry leaves and pine needles. Embers land in it. The debris catches. And now the flame is sitting at the roof edge, which is the worst place for it, because from there the fire gets up under the roof covering and into the underlayment.



Clean them out. Then clean the valleys, where two roof planes meet and the debris collects in a line. That's a drift spot too.



And the roof itself. Most roofs in this country are already Class A rated, which is the good news in all this. The weak point on a modern roof usually isn't the roofing material. It's what's lying on top of it and what's packed into the gaps. Needles in the valley. Debris under the first row of tiles. A branch reaching out over the whole thing.



If climbing on your roof isn't something you do safely, don't. That's a call to a gutter company.

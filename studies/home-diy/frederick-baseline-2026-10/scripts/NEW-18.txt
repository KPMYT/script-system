Every year, on the first real cold night, the space heater comes out of the back of the closet, gets plugged into the wall, and pushes that first wave of warm air across your feet. It’s one of the small comforts of winter. It’s also the start of heating season, and home heating fires peak from December right through February. Space heaters are behind only about a third of those fires. \[quietly\] But they’re tied to the vast majority of the deaths. \[matter-of-fact\] A space heater is built nothing like your furnace. The furnace sits in the basement or a closet down the hall and sends its heat out through the house, a long way from anything you’d sleep under. A space heater packs a lot of heat into one small box, sets it on the floor at the height of your bedspread and your couch cushions, and puts it in the same room you’re in, very often the room you’re about to fall asleep in.



That heat leaves the box two ways. Some of it warms the air, and the warm air rises. The rest travels straight out as radiant heat, the same kind you feel on your face from a campfire, and radiant heat warms whatever it lands on, whether that’s the bedspread, the curtain, or the arm of the recliner. The fabric doesn’t have to touch the heater to get into trouble. It soaks up that heat, and if it takes heat in faster than it can let it go, the temperature keeps climbing. First it scorches. \[slower\] Then it starts to smolder, a slow, low burn that can fill a room with smoke before there’s a flame anybody would notice.



Every fire needs three things: heat, fuel, and oxygen. In a bedroom with a space heater running, all three are in there with you. The heater is the heat, and that’s why you bought it. The air is the oxygen, and you’re breathing it. That leaves the fuel, the blankets, the curtains, the couch, the laundry basket by the door. \[thoughtful\] While that heater’s running, the one side of the triangle you really control is the fuel, and how close it gets.



That’s why the deadly ones catch careful people off guard. The heater doesn’t have to be broken, or cheap, or old. A brand-new one, doing exactly what the box promised, can start the fire simply by sitting a foot from the wrong thing. Fire safety groups have tracked heating fires for years, and when one turns deadly, the most common reason comes down to that one detail. \[quietly\] Something that could burn was too close.



Even when you set it up right, the room doesn’t hold still. You leave a good wide gap at nine o’clock. Then the comforter slides off the side of the bed. The curtain sways every time the furnace kicks on. The dog drags its blanket across the floor and flops down wherever it’s warmest. \[thoughtful\] I call that the drift, and it does its work while you’re asleep.



A heater fire almost never comes out of nowhere. There’s usually something off about the equipment, a habit that feels harmless, and a warning sign nobody acted on, all lined up in plain sight for weeks. The fire is just the last link, and pulling out any link before it breaks the chain.

First, the heater itself, because what it’s made of matters more than the name on the box. Pull it out and look through the front grille. If you can see bare wire coils back there, the kind that glow orange once it warms up, that’s the open-coil style, and plenty of them have been riding out winters in closets for a long time. \[warm\] I understand the attachment. It’s kept somebody warm for years, and it still comes on every time you ask. But that coil gets hot enough to glow, and the only thing between it and a stray sock is a wire grille.



Newer designs keep the hot parts out of reach. A ceramic heater tucks its element deep inside the housing, so the outside of the case stays far cooler than a bare coil ever could. An oil-filled radiator looks like an old-fashioned radiator on little wheels. Electricity warms oil that’s sealed inside the fins, the oil holds onto that heat, and the fins give it off slowly, with no glowing element anywhere you can reach. Those fins still get hot, so everything about distance still applies. It’s just a calmer kind of heat.



So here’s the heater I’d retire. \[emphatic\] If yours is the old open-coil kind, let it go. It’s earned its rest. The same goes for any heater with a cord that’s cracked, stiff, or frayed, a plug that’s turned brown or lost its shape, or scorch marks anywhere on the housing. Before yours runs for the first time this season, set it somewhere with good light, unplugged, vacuum the dust out of the grille, and run your fingers down the whole length of the cord, because cords get kinked and pinched while they sit in a closet all summer. A little dusty smell the first time you run it each fall is normal, and it burns off in a few minutes. \[thoughtful\] A smell that hangs around, or any whiff of hot plastic, is the heater asking to be retired. Replacing it does cost money, unlike most of tonight’s fixes, but a sealed heater with modern safety features built in isn’t a big purchase.



Whether it’s the heater you already own or one still in the box at the store, look for three things. The first is a mark from a testing lab, usually UL or ETL, which tells you the design was tested against a safety standard. The second is a tip-over switch, which cuts the power if the heater gets knocked over. The third is overheat protection, which shuts the heater down if its insides get too hot. Every heater in your house should have all three, and it’s worth knowing what they can’t do. A tip-over switch knows when the heater falls. \[slightly amused\] It has no idea when the blanket does. Overheat protection is watching the inside of the heater, and it can’t see the curtain eight inches away. Those switches are your backup. The space around the heater is the plan.



One more kind belongs in this conversation, and that’s any heater that burns fuel, like kerosene, propane, or gas, without a vent to the outside. Anything that burns fuel indoors makes carbon monoxide, a gas you can’t see or smell. Those heaters need the fresh air their maker calls for, a carbon monoxide alarm nearby, and refueling done outdoors once they’ve cooled down. Some states restrict them. \[quietly\] And none of them belongs in a room where somebody’s sleeping.



Look down next, at what the heater is standing on. A rug feels warmer under your feet, and that’s exactly how heaters end up on them. But a soft or uneven surface makes a heater easier to tip, and on some models a deep rug crowds the vents along the bottom. Up off the floor is worse. A heater on a chair, a table, or the foot of the bed is one bump away from landing in the fuel. \[slightly amused\] A space heater on an ottoman is just a short fall waiting for a reason. Set it on bare, hard, level floor, like tile, wood, or vinyl, then give it a little nudge with your foot. If it rocks, find it a better spot. That fix is free, and it takes about ten seconds.



From there, follow the cord to the wall. The power strip. Power strips are fine for a lamp and a phone charger, but a space heater asks for far more. \[matter-of-fact\] On high, a typical plug-in heater pulls close to everything an ordinary household circuit is built to carry, all by itself, for hours at a stretch. A power strip or a light extension cord adds extra connections and often thinner wire, and every connection is a spot where resistance turns electricity into heat. That thin brown extension cord from the junk drawer was made for a string of holiday lights, not a heater.



The rule itself is simple. Plug the heater straight into a wall outlet, and give it that outlet to itself, with nothing else heavy sharing it and never a strip or a cord in between. \[emphatic\] If the cord won’t reach the wall from a safe spot, the heater is in the wrong spot. And keep in mind that the outlet is only the end of a longer run of wire. The outlet your heater uses may share its circuit with the lamp, the TV, and an outlet or two in the next room. Run a hair dryer or a vacuum on that same circuit while the heater’s going, and you’ve stacked two heavy loads on wire meant for one.



Your hands and your nose come next. After the heater has been running for a while, rest your palm on the plug and on the faceplate around it. A plug can feel a little warm after an hour of hard work. \[matter-of-fact\] Hot is different. A hot plug, a warm faceplate, brown marks around the slots, a plug that sags loose in the outlet, or the smell of hot plastic all mean you stop using that outlet tonight. The same goes if the lights in the room dim every time the heater clicks on, or the breaker keeps tripping, because that circuit is working harder than it should. You can spot every one of those signs from the outside. What’s behind the faceplate belongs to a licensed electrician.



Then step back and look at the floor around the heater, all the way around. \[slower\] Three feet. The front is the side everybody checks, because that’s where the warm air blows. But heat doesn’t stay where the fan points. The sides and back of a heater get warm too, and on a lot of models the back is where the air gets pulled in, which can tug a curtain right up against the grille. \[thoughtful\] Trouble likes to come from the direction you weren’t watching, the curtain behind it, the couch beside it, the stack of mail under the desk where it’s been tucked out of sight. The national fire safety groups put three feet of clear space at the top of their heating advice, winter after winter.



Three feet is about one big step for a grown adult, or the length of a yardstick. Stand at the heater and take one big step in every direction. Nothing that can burn belongs inside that circle. That means bedding, the edge of the mattress, curtains, upholstered chairs, clothes, paper, cardboard boxes, the dog’s bed, and come December, the wrapping paper and the Christmas tree.



And then there’s the wet stuff. It makes perfect sense on a snowy morning to line the gloves and boots up in front of the heater, or drape the socks right over the top. \[chuckles\] I’ve caught myself nudging a heater closer with my heel on a cold night, so I’m not judging anybody here. But anything draped over a heater traps the heat it’s trying to get rid of, and that trapped heat ends up in the fabric. Dry things on a rack across the room. Nothing ever goes on top of a heater, not even for a minute.



Clear the circle once tonight, then glance at it every time you walk past. It takes two seconds, and it breaks the link that shows up most often when these fires turn deadly.



Next, think about who walks through that circle. \[warm\] A small child sees a glowing heater about the way you’d see a campfire, something warm and bright to get close to. A cat will curl up as near to the heat as she can get. A dog’s tail, or the bed he drags around behind him, can end up pressed against the grille without anybody noticing. Make those three feet a no-kid, no-pet zone, and set the heater where a running child or a sliding dog can’t knock into it. When you leave the room, the heater goes off. That’s every time, even if you’re only stepping out for a few minutes, and especially if the dog is staying behind.



And if your mom or dad lives on their own, take a look at their heater on your next visit. A lot of parents pull the heater right up to the recliner or the side of the bed because they’re cold, and they’d never think of it as a hazard. \[thoughtful\] That’s the drift, just done on purpose. Slide it back, show them the one-big-step test, check that the plug goes straight into the wall, and make sure there’s a working smoke alarm in the hallway outside their bedroom.



Which brings us to the last place that heater can reach, the bed you’re about to climb into. Heating fires are least likely to start in the small hours of the night. \[quietly\] But the ones that do start then account for a far bigger share of the deaths, because by then nearly everyone in the house is asleep.



And remember the drift. Even if the heater stays perfectly still all night, the bedroom doesn’t. Sheets slide, blankets fall, and the gap you measured before bed closes up while you sleep. So the heater doesn’t run while you do.



Now for the promise from the start. On a lot of heaters, the knob you turn at night is a thermostat, and turning it down only tells the heater to wait for a colder room. When the room cools off around three in the morning, it can click the element right back on with nobody awake to see it. Even a real off switch can get bumped by a foot, a pet, or a blanket sliding across it. \[emphatic\] A plug lying on the floor can’t do any of that, and you can see it from the doorway on your way to bed.



While you’re standing in that doorway, look up at the smoke alarm in the hallway and press its test button.

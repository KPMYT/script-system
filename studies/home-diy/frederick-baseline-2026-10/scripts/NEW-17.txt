\[quietly\] It’s a few minutes past midnight, and you’re the last one awake in the house. You check the back door, you kill the kitchen light, and somewhere between that switch and your pillow, you make about ten small decisions without ever noticing you made them. \[thoughtful\] Most nights, every one of them is free. Tonight I want to show you the ten that aren’t. Ten things that could kill you in your sleep, and they split right down the middle: half are hazards sitting out in plain sight, and half are protections that are supposed to be standing guard and simply aren’t. I’m Frederick Barnes, and this walk is for anyone who locks up over a houseful of sleeping people — kids down the hall, a parent in a house across town, or the home you just bought with decisions in its walls nobody explained. Stay with me to the end, because the number one spot doesn’t go to anything in your house; it goes to something missing from it.



One more thing before we count. Everything on this list shares a single advantage, and it’s worth naming: the head start. \[matter-of-fact\] Nothing tonight has to beat you. It only has to beat you to waking up. Keep that in your pocket, because we’re coming back to it.



Number ten. A space heater, still running, a few feet from your bed.



I know how it gets there. The bedroom runs cold, the heater runs warm, and nobody plans to drift off mid-cozy; it just happens. \[matter-of-fact\] The heater itself usually isn’t the problem. The distance is. These things work by pouring heat into whatever sits in front of them, and a bedspread, a curtain, or a dropped sweatshirt can only take so much before taking heat turns into making smoke. \[short pause\] Here’s the part that matters. Give it three feet of open air on every side, keep it on the floor, and plug it straight into the wall, because a heater on an extension cord or a power strip is asking thin wire to do heavy work, and thin wire answers by heating up inside its jacket where you can’t see it. Better yet, make the heater a while-you’re-awake machine: warm the room, then switch it off before your eyes close, or get one with a timer and let it shut itself down. And run your palm along that cord tonight. If any stretch of it feels warm, or the prongs have gone brown, that heater has finished its service. Replace it.



Number nine is quieter. It doesn’t even hum.



Number nine. A flame you lit on purpose, still burning after you’ve stopped watching it.



\[warm\] A candle at dinner, an oil lamp when the power blinks. That’s not recklessness, that’s atmosphere, and I understand the appeal. The danger starts the moment the flame outlasts your attention. A candle burned to the bottom turns its own glass into the hottest object in the room, and glass that hot cracks. An oil lamp tipped by a cat or a sleeve doesn’t spill water. And a window cracked an inch is enough breeze to walk a curtain into a flame while you’re two rooms away brushing your teeth. \[matter-of-fact\] The fix costs nothing but a breath. The last one up puts out every flame in the house, every night, no exceptions; make it part of locking up, same as the door. If you love the glow, a battery candle will flicker all night without asking a thing of you. And read the glass on anything you do burn: black soot climbing the sides means the flame has been running too big, and a wick drowning in a deep well of wax is a candle telling you it’s done.



Number eight starts smaller than either of those. Small enough to hide for hours.



Number eight. A cigarette, finished on the couch or in bed.



If someone in your house smokes, this isn’t a lecture about quitting. It’s about where the last one of the night happens. Ask anyone who has spent a career around house fires and you’ll hear the same hard thing: smoking materials start the fires people don’t get out of. Here’s why. An ember that slips between couch cushions doesn’t flame up. It smolders, buried in foam and fabric, sometimes for hours, while everyone heads to bed believing the evening is over. \[quietly\] The room fills with smoke before there’s ever a fire to see, and at night, smoke does the killing long before flame does. The fix is a rule, not a product. The last cigarette of the night gets smoked outside, standing up, and it dies in water or a deep metal can with sand in it — never a wastebasket, never flicked into the mulch. And read your own furniture while you’re at it. A small dark-rimmed scorch on a cushion or a mattress isn’t a stain. It’s a record of the night this almost happened.

And hold the number one spot in the back of your mind while we go, because it decides whether anything else on this list ever gets the chance to wake you.

Number seven. An e-bike or a scooter, charging overnight, parked by the front door.



The bike isn’t the villain, and neither is charging. It’s the combination: a big lithium battery, a cheap replacement charger or a pack that has taken a hit, all of it running unwatched in the one spot your whole family funnels through to get out. \[matter-of-fact\] Lithium fails differently than anything else in your house. One cell overheats, cooks its neighbor, and in seconds the pack stops being a battery and becomes its own blowtorch — fast, violent, and parked across your exit. So here’s the rule. Charge while you’re awake and home, on hard flooring, away from the door and the hallway, and unplug when it’s full. Use the charger the bike came with, nothing borrowed, nothing bargain-bin. And retire without negotiating: a pack that’s swollen, a pack that’s been dropped hard or soaked, a pack that runs hot on a charge that never used to warm it. That battery’s service is over, and it goes to a battery recycler, not the kitchen trash.



Number six doesn’t burn at all. That’s exactly what makes it worse.



Number six. An engine running in an attached garage. A generator during an outage, or a car warming up on a cold morning.



Nobody does this to be careless. They do it because it’s raining, or freezing, and the garage feels like shelter. \[quietly\] But an engine makes carbon monoxide every second it runs, and carbon monoxide is the perfect intruder: no color, no smell, no taste, nothing to notice. Even with the big door wide open, a garage still fills, and the gas doesn’t stay put. It slides under the door to the house, through the shared wall, up into the bedrooms, and the people breathing it just get sleepier. There’s no halfway on the fix. A generator runs outside, a good twenty feet from the house, exhaust pointed away from every window and door — not in the garage, not in the doorway, not under the eaves. A car gets backed out before it idles, every single time, even for a minute. If tonight is the night the power fails, cold and inconvenienced beats warm and unconscious.



Which leads straight into number five, because this same gas has quieter ways in, and most houses have left that door unguarded.



Number five. No carbon monoxide alarm in a house with gas appliances, a fireplace, or an attached garage.



This is the first missing protection of the night. Your furnace, your water heater, the gas stove, the fireplace, that garage we just left — on a good day, all of them behave. \[quietly\] The trouble is the day one of them doesn’t, when a cracked vent or a blocked flue sends that same invisible gas drifting through the house, and your nose never files a report. All your body gets is a headache and a heaviness that feels exactly like being tired, and at night, in bed, you’d never know the difference. \[matter-of-fact\] The fix plugs into the wall. A carbon monoxide alarm runs twenty-five to forty dollars, and it belongs in the hallway outside the bedrooms on every sleeping floor, close enough to drag you out of a dream. If one is already plugged in somewhere, flip it over tonight. These age out like anything with a sensor inside, and the date printed on the back tells you whether yours is still on duty or just decorating the outlet.



It’s getting on toward two now. Doors are closed, or about to be. That’s number four.



Number four. Sleeping with your bedroom door open.



\[thoughtful\] I know exactly why the door stays open. You’re listening — for the baby, for the kid who gets up, for the old dog on the stairs. But a few years back, fire researchers burned identical rooms side by side, one door open, one door shut, and the pictures tell it better than numbers ever could. The open-door room came out black and melted. The closed-door room looked almost slept-in. \[short pause\] A plain bedroom door holds back heat and smoke, starves a fire of fresh air, and keeps the air on your side of it breathable through the minutes that decide everything. So the fix is six inches of effort: close the door, every night, as the last move before bed. If you need your ears on someone down the hall, close their door too and let a monitor do the listening. It was built for the job. And hold onto this closed door, because it comes back at number two, and it changes what you need on your ceiling.



Number three is about the moment everything goes right, and the house still won’t let you leave.



Number three. A way out that doesn’t actually open.



Every bedroom is supposed to have two exits: the door, and almost always, the window. The door you use daily. The window is the one that quietly stops being an exit — painted shut one repaint at a time, swollen into its frame, or covered by security bars bolted on with no quick-release inside. Add a hallway that has turned into storage, the laundry basket, the donation boxes, the bike against the wall, and your escape route is an obstacle course you’re expecting to clear in the dark, in smoke, crouched low, possibly carrying a child. \[matter-of-fact\] Tonight’s fix is a test, two minutes a room. Walk in and open the window all the way with your own hands. If it fights you, it fails, and freeing it goes to the top of the weekend list. If there are bars, there has to be a release you can work from inside without a key or a tool. No release, no bars; it’s that simple. Then clear the hallway to three feet wide and keep it that way. \[quietly\] An exit you haven’t opened this year is an exit you’re hoping works.



Now, number two, and here’s that closed door again.



Number two. Smoke alarms out in the hallway, and nowhere else.



A hallway alarm is good. In a house where doors stay open, it might even be enough. \[thoughtful\] But you just closed every bedroom door at number four, and the same slab of wood that holds smoke out also holds sound out. A closed door can soften an alarm’s scream into something a deep sleeper rolls straight through. It cuts the other way too. If the fire starts inside a bedroom — a heater, a charger, a lamp — the hallway alarm is sitting on the wrong side of that door, waiting on smoke that’s taking its time. \[matter-of-fact\] The fix is placement plus connection. One alarm inside every bedroom, one in the hallway outside the sleeping rooms, at least one on every level of the house. Then make them a team. Interconnected alarms mean that when one smells trouble, every one of them sounds at once, and the fire in the basement wakes the kid on the second floor. The wireless kind pair with a button press and hang on two screws — an evening’s work, no electrician, no excuses left.



And that brings us to the top of the list. The thing that isn’t there.



Number one. No working smoke alarm at all.



\[slower\] Not a hazard. An absence. And it takes the top spot on one plain fact from the people who keep this country’s fire numbers: three out of five home fire deaths happen in homes with no smoke alarms, or alarms that don’t work. Remember the head start we named at the beginning? While you sleep, a fire takes it — growing, smoking, filling rooms — and your nose is no help, because smell is one of the first senses sleep switches off. A working alarm is the only thing in your house built to take that head start back. And not working wears disguises: the battery borrowed for a toy and never returned, the unit pulled down after burnt toast and never put back, the quiet gray veteran on the ceiling that’s simply past its time. Alarms retire at ten years, and the date on the back doesn’t negotiate. \[emphatic\] So tonight, before bed, press and hold the test button on every alarm until it screams at you. Replace any battery you can’t vouch for. And if an alarm is past ten, or you can’t prove it ever worked, a sealed ten-year alarm runs twenty to thirty-five dollars. One trip, one screwdriver, ten years of a sentry that never sleeps.



Before you go push those buttons, here’s the truth that ties all ten together. When a fire does start in a modern home — modern furniture, modern foam, modern plastics — the time between the alarm’s first scream and a hallway you can no longer breathe in can be just a couple of minutes. \[slower\] Two minutes is not enough time to figure anything out. It is plenty of time to do something you’ve already decided. So decide it tonight: two ways out of every room, a meeting spot everyone can name, the mailbox, the neighbor’s porch, and one walk-through with every person in the house, kids and older folks included, practicing it low and slow once while it’s boring. \[quietly\] That way, if an alarm ever earns its keep at three in the morning, nobody in your house has to think. They just have to go.

Number ten is out in the yard. The inflatable snowman. The lights along the gutter. The plastic reindeer that leans a little further every year. A bright yard isn’t the danger. The cord feeding it is.



Most of us grab whatever extension cord is in the junk drawer, and most of those were built for a lamp in a dry living room. Outside, that cord sits in snow, takes the rain, freezes stiff overnight, and thaws again by afternoon. The jacket cracks. Water finds the copper. And a wet connection lying on the lawn can heat up, or arc, which is electricity jumping a gap it shouldn’t, throwing off sparks and heat as it goes.



So here’s your part. Read the cord. An outdoor-rated cord says so, right on the jacket or the tag, and you’ll usually see the letter W in the rating printed on it. \[matter-of-fact\] If yours doesn’t say it’s made for outdoor use, it isn’t. A proper outdoor cord runs about fifteen or twenty dollars, which makes it the cheapest piece of the whole display. Plug it into an outdoor outlet with ground fault protection, the kind with the test and reset buttons, which shuts the power off the moment current starts leaking somewhere it shouldn’t. Keep the plug connections up off the ground and out of the puddles.



And hang onto one small idea, because it comes back inside with us. A plain outlet timer. \[slightly amused\] About ten dollars, and the snowman goes dark at midnight whether anybody remembers or not.



Number nine is one door closer, in the attached garage. This one is for anybody up north with an engine block heater, and anybody who keeps a battery tender on the mower, the motorcycle, or the old truck through the winter. A block heater is a heating element that keeps the engine warm enough to start on a bitter morning. A battery tender is a small charger that keeps a battery topped off while it sits. Both are useful, and both are built to run for hours.



\[thoughtful\] The trouble is where they run. A garage is full of fuel. Gas in the can, oil on the shelf, paint thinner, cardboard, the rags you meant to throw out in the spring. And an attached garage shares a wall, and usually a door, with the rooms where your family sleeps. I’ve watched a block heater cord spend a whole season lying where a tire rolls over it every morning. That’s how the copper inside gives up, one strand at a time, until what’s left is carrying the full load and running hot.



The fix is mostly habit. Use the cord the heater came with, or a heavy outdoor-rated one, never a thin indoor cord. Route it where nothing drives over it. Put the block heater on a timer so it comes on a few hours before you leave instead of all night long. Give the battery tender a spot on bare concrete, well away from the gas can, and use a tender built to back off on its own once the battery’s full. If any of these plugs feels hot, or the face of the plug looks brown or melted, retire it. \[emphatic\] And keep the door between the garage and the house shut. It’s there for a reason.

Number eight is somewhere most people never look. Heat tape. If you’ve got pipes in a crawlspace, an unheated basement, or along an outside wall that froze once and you swore never again, there may be heat tape wrapped around them. It’s an electric cable that warms the pipe just enough to keep the water inside from freezing. A listed product, put on the way its instructions say, does that job well.



\[matter-of-fact\] Old or damaged heat tape is another story. It’s a known ignition source. It gets wrapped over itself, which most instructions forbid, so the heat doubles up in one spot. It gets buried under pipe insulation it was never meant to sit beneath. It gets nicked, chewed, or simply old, and the jacket cracks. \[quietly\] And it lives down in the dark next to wood framing, cobwebs, and dust, where nobody would notice it running hot.

Check the plug, and the little indicator light if it has one. If you can’t tell how old it is, or it looks like it came with the house, replace it with a listed product and install it exactly as directed. Number seven may be humming right beside the pipes. The dehumidifier. Or upstairs, the humidifier in the kid’s room. Winter air turns dry upstairs and damp downstairs, so plenty of homes run both. Neither one is a problem by itself. The trouble is the cord in between.



These machines run for hours, often all night, every night, and the outlet is never where you want the machine. So out comes an extension cord, usually the thin brown or white one that came free with a lamp. A dehumidifier pulls real power for a long stretch. Push that through light-duty wire and the cord warms up, because thin wire fights the current and turns that fight into heat. The plug ends warm up most of all. \[thoughtful\] Now add water. A humidifier makes mist on purpose, and a dehumidifier collects it by the bucket. A warm connection on a damp floor is a bad pairing.



This one’s nearly free. Move the machine to where the outlet is, and plug it straight into the wall. \[slightly amused\] If it has to sit somewhere else, the owner’s manual will tell you what kind of cord it allows, and plenty of them say none at all. Keep the plug where spilled water can’t reach it. Empty and clean the thing on schedule. And if a plug is ever warm when you pull it, that’s not the weather. That plug is warning you.



Number six is up the stairs, on the kitchen counter, the bathroom shelf, or the end table. The candle warmer. The wax melter. A lot of folks switched to these because they felt safer than an open flame, and in one way they are. No match, no wick, no flame. But a warmer is still a heater. Its whole job is to make something hot and keep it hot, and what it keeps hot is wax, which is fuel.



\[warm\] I understand why they get left on. They’re quiet, they smell good, and they don’t look like they’re doing anything. That’s exactly what makes them easy to forget at eleven at night. Number four is standing right beside it. The Christmas tree. \[slower\] Listen closely here, because this is the fastest fire on the whole list. A fresh, well-watered tree is hard to light. Number three is a few steps away, on the couch or the recliner. The heating pad. For a lot of people it’s the first thing out of the closet when the weather turns. Number two is on the bed itself. The electric blanket. The heated mattress pad. These are made for nighttime, and a good one in good shape, used the way the tag says, is fine. The danger is age, and whatever ends up on top.



\[thoughtful\] Inside that blanket is a long run of heating wire. Every time it’s folded, tucked, crammed into a closet, or slept on along the same crease, that wire flexes in the same spots.

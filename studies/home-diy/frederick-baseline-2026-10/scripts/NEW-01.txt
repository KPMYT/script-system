Firefighters don't unplug everything before bed, and you don't have to either. The fridge, the alarm clock, and the router blinking in the hallway were built to run through the night with nobody watching. But there's a short list of things they won't leave plugged in once the lights go out, and the line they draw comes down to heat, and whether anybody's awake to notice it. I've got ten of them, in the order you'd pass them on the way to bed, from the kitchen to your pillow. And number one isn't an appliance at all.



I'm Frederick Barnes. If you're the one who keeps an eye on things, whether that's a house full of kids, a parent's place you check on, or a house you just bought along with whatever wiring came with it, this is for you. Nearly every fix here takes a few minutes and your own two hands, and when one belongs to a licensed electrician, I'll say so.



Number ten. The toaster on your kitchen counter.



We start in the kitchen, because that's where the whole idea is easiest to see. Your refrigerator and freezer were designed to run day and night on their own. A toaster was designed for a couple of red-hot minutes with you standing right next to it. After that, it sits plugged in all night, and so do the toaster oven and the coffee maker with the warming plate. With the plug in, a sticking switch or a worn wire inside has power to work with, and the crumbs in the bottom tray are about as dry as fuel gets. I pulled the tray out of my own toaster once and found enough crumbs to feed every bird on the block.



So before bed, unplug the countertop crowd, anything on the counter whose whole job is making heat. When you take the trash out, empty that crumb tray too. If the lever sticks, if one side stays dark while the other glows, or you smell hot dust when it's supposed to be off, retire it. Next stop is the living room, where a cord moved in for one weekend and never left.



Number nine. The extension cord running under the living room rug.



It usually starts as something temporary. The lamp by the reading chair needed power, the outlet was on the wrong wall, and a cord under the rug looked neat and kept anybody from tripping. Then nobody moved it back.



And here's the part that matters. Any cord carrying power runs a little warm, and normally that warmth drifts off into the room. A rug on top holds it in. That's the enemy behind much of tonight's list, trapped heat, warmth with nowhere to go except into whatever's wrapped around it. On top of that, every time somebody walks across that spot, the cord gets pressed and flexed, and over months the insulation, the rubbery jacket keeping the wires apart, can crack where you'll never see it.



The Consumer Product Safety Commission has told people for years to keep cords out from under rugs and furniture, and this is why. So tonight, pull it out and run it along the wall where you can see it. If the jacket's cracked, flattened, or warm, throw that cord away. And if you need power in that spot every day, that cord has become part of your house's wiring without anybody deciding it should. A licensed electrician can put an outlet where you actually use it.



That's two down, and number one is still ahead, the one you can check tonight with nothing but the palm of your hand.

Number eight. The power strip plugged into another power strip.



I've knelt behind my own TV stand with a flashlight and found a power strip wearing a gray sweater of dust. One strip running a TV, a lamp, and a charger is doing the job it was built for. The trouble starts when it runs out of spots and somebody plugs a second strip into the first, maybe a third. Electricians call that daisy-chaining.



Stay with me on this one. Everything on that chain pulls its power through one cord and one wall outlet, and the more power you push through a connection, the hotter it runs. The little reset switch on the strip trips on a big overload. It won't notice a worn contact running hot for hours behind the couch. And a space heater, a microwave, or a hair dryer pulls a heavy load all by itself, so those belong straight in the wall, never on a strip.



A fire like this tends to come together from three pieces, a worn part, a habit that leans on it too hard, and a warning sign nobody caught. The strip runs warm and the dust starts to smell like hot plastic, exactly the kind of warning a person would catch, if anybody were awake to catch it. Tonight, unplug that second strip and spread things across more than one outlet. Pull the strip out where you can see it, out of the dust. If a strip feels warm, the plastic's gone yellow or brown, or plugs sit loose in it, replace it with one that carries a testing lab's mark, like UL. Number seven is in the laundry room, and it's the machine people love to start on their way to bed.



Number seven. The dryer you start on your way to bed.



It's easy to see how this happens. The wash finishes late, nobody wants wet towels sitting overnight, so in they go, you hit start, and you head upstairs. The National Fire Protection Association asks you not to, and their rule is plain. Don't run the dryer when you're asleep or out of the house.



A dryer pushes hot air through your clothes and out a duct to the outside, leaving lint as it goes. Lint is dry, fine fuzz, the kind of thing campers carry in their pockets to get a fire going. When it builds up in the screen, or the duct behind the dryer gets crushed against the wall, the hot air can't get out fast enough, and the heat builds right where the lint collects.



So run it while you're up and around, even if the towels wait until morning. Clean the lint screen before or after every load, and now and then pull the dryer out to check that the vent hose isn't crushed or kinked. If loads keep taking longer to dry, the top of the machine runs hotter than it used to, or you smell something scorched, the air isn't getting out. Clean the screen yourself, and if the vent's clogged somewhere inside the wall, call somebody who cleans dryer vents for a living. Number six is by the front door or out in the garage, and it's the newest thing on this list.



Number six. The e-bike or scooter battery charging overnight.



Electric bikes and scooters are everywhere now, and the well-made ones come with a solid battery and a charger built for it. What worries fire departments is the charging, especially through the night while the whole family's asleep. The New York City Fire Department has been warning about these batteries about as loudly as anything they warn about.



A lithium battery is a pack of small cells packed tight together. If one cell gets damaged, from a hard drop, a cheap charger, or water getting inside, it can start heating itself up, and that heat spreads to the cell beside it, then the next. Once that gets going it moves fast, from a hiss and white smoke to a jet of flame, and it's stubborn to put out. Now think about where these usually charge. The front hall, right by the door. The garage, next to the door into the house. That's your way out.



Their advice is simple. Charge it while you're home and awake, and unplug it once it's full. Use the charger it came with or one the maker approves, never a bargain replacement off the internet, and keep the bike and battery clear of the door you'd need to get out. Look for a certification mark from a testing lab like UL on the bike and its charger, and use the same thinking for the kids' hoverboard and the power tool batteries in the garage. If a battery's swollen, dented, leaking, smells strange, or gets hot while it charges, stop using it. Ask your local trash or recycling service how to get rid of it, because it doesn't go in the regular garbage. Number five is in the bathroom, sitting on the counter right where you left it this morning.



Number five. The curling iron or flat iron on the bathroom counter.



I know how this one goes. You're running behind, you use it, set it down, and head out the door, and it's still plugged in when you get home that night. Newer ones often shut themselves off after a while, and that helps. The catch is that you can't always tell which kind you've got, and the older one in the back of the drawer may not shut itself off at all.



These tools get hot enough to scorch a towel, and where do they tend to get set down? Right on a towel, next to a paper cup and a can of hairspray. A hot barrel lying against cloth has nowhere to send its heat except into the cloth, and it keeps at it for as long as it's plugged in. So unplug it when you're done, every time, and let it cool on a heat-safe mat or the bare counter, away from anything that burns. And wait until it's cold before you wrap the cord around it, because heat cracks the cord right where it meets the handle. If the cord's cracked or taped, or the light flickers when you bend it near the handle, that iron's finished, so throw it out. From there it's into the bedroom, and number four is the one you were probably expecting a lot sooner.



Number four. The space heater in the bedroom.



If you figured this one would sit at the very top, you're close, and it's the one fire departments bring up every winter. A heater in good shape, on a hard floor with room around it, is a reasonable way to take the chill off a room. Where it goes wrong is the space around it and the hours it runs. The National Fire Protection Association's rule is to keep anything that can burn at least three feet away, and to turn the heater off when you leave the room or go to bed.



Think about what a bedroom does at night. The comforter slides off when you roll over. The curtain drifts in a draft. A kid kicks a stuffed animal onto the floor. Any of that can happen while you're asleep, and a heater doesn't need an open flame to start trouble. Its coils or its face get hot enough that fabric resting against it will scorch, then smolder, then catch.



So warm the room before bedtime, then switch the heater off and reach for an extra blanket, the plain kind, because the electric kind is next. Plug the heater straight into the wall, never into a power strip or an extension cord. Look for one with a tip-over switch, which shuts it off if it gets knocked over. If the plug or outlet feels hot, the cord's gone stiff or discolored, or it smells like burning dust every time it runs, stop using it. And on your next visit to your parents' house, check where their heater sits. It's easy for a heater to creep toward the recliner or the bed a few inches at a time without anybody noticing.



Number three. The electric blanket, or the heated pad on top of the mattress.



A newer blanket that shuts itself off, lying flat and in good shape, is a comfortable way through a cold night. What concerns me is the way these get used, and the old one that's been in the linen closet so long nobody remembers buying it.



Thin heating wires run back and forth inside that blanket. When it gets folded, bunched, or tucked tight under the mattress night after night, those wires bend in the same spots, and heat piles up wherever the fabric is doubled over. Lay a sleeping dog or a stack of quilts on top, and that heat has even less room to escape.



So use it to warm the bed, then switch it off before you fall asleep, or at least use the timer if yours has one. Lay it flat, don't tuck it in, don't pile things on it, and don't run a heated pad and a heated blanket together. Once a season, spread it out in good light and look for dark or scorched spots, and check the cord where it meets the control. Scorching, a cracked cord, or a control that heats up means it's time to retire it. And check the one in your parents' linen closet, since it may be the oldest in the family.



Two left. Number two is at the pillow itself, yours or your teenager's, and number one is the thing every other item on tonight's list has to plug into.



Number two. The phone or tablet charging under a pillow.



Walk into a teenager's room after lights out, and there's a decent chance the phone's under the pillow, charger and all. Grown-ups do it too, and I understand why. It's close, it's handy, and that's where the alarm lives.



A phone charging on a nightstand, on a hard surface, with a good charger, is about as ordinary as it gets. The pillow changes that. Charging makes heat, in the battery and in the little brick at the wall, and a phone is designed to let that warmth drift off into the air. Bury it under a pillow or a comforter, and there's that trapped heat again, only now it's inside the one thing in the house made to hold warmth in, right under somebody who's asleep.



The New York City Fire Department tells people never to charge a device on a bed or under a pillow. And the charger matters. Use the one that came in the box or a certified replacement, and if you've got a no-name charger from a checkout counter with no safety listing anywhere on it, I'd toss it.



Tonight, move the charger to the nightstand or the dresser. If the cord won't reach, get a longer cord instead of a closer pillow. If the brick is too warm to hold comfortably, or the cord's frayed where it bends, that charger's done. And check the tablet and the handheld game while you're at it, because they end up under the covers just as often.



Number one. Anything plugged into an outlet that's warm, loose, or scorched.



It's the outlet itself, the thing everything on this list plugs into, and the one behind your nightstand is the place to start. An outlet in good shape grips a plug firmly and stays cool. The one to worry about is the worn one. Inside are little metal contacts that squeeze the prongs of the plug, and after years of plugs going in and out, they lose their grip. Less metal touching means the electricity squeezes through a smaller spot, and that spot gets hot. It won't trip a breaker, because nothing's overloaded. It just heats up and cools down, night after night, and each round leaves the connection a little worse.



An outlet like that gives you warnings. A lamp that flickers when you brush the cord. A plug that sags halfway out of the wall. A cover plate gone brown around the slots, a faint buzz, or a smell like hot plastic near the couch. Any one of those is easy to catch when you're standing in the room. The trouble is that the lamp, the nightlight, or the phone charger stays in that worn outlet all night, while the only person who'd notice is asleep.



Here's your job for tonight. Take a flashlight, walk the house, and at every outlet that keeps something plugged in overnight, lay your palm flat on the cover plate. It should feel about the same as the wall around it. Look for dark marks, listen for a buzz or a crackle, and give each plug a gentle wiggle. If one's warm, scorched, buzzing, or won't hold a plug snug, unplug everything from it and leave it empty. Don't take the cover off and go poking around. The problem lives behind that plate, so leave the fixing to a licensed electrician, and make that call tomorrow. Do the same walk at a parent's place, or in an older house you just bought.



Then take the whole list with you. Unplug the toaster and the curling iron. Pull the cord out from under the rug and break up that power strip chain. Run the dryer before bed, charge the bike battery while you're awake, and shut the space heater off when you turn in. Switch the blanket off, move the phone to the nightstand, and put your palm on those outlets.

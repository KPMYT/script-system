Picture two houses on the same street. They went up the same year, with the same furnace in the basement and the same tired wiring behind the same beige outlets. Walk into either one and you couldn't tell them apart. There's the same couch, the same pile of mail on the counter, the same kid's bike leaning against the wall in the garage.



The difference is where things live. In the first house, the dryer lint gets pulled every load, the space heater sits out in open floor, and the bedroom doors close at night. The second house has all the same stuff, but it ended up wherever it happened to land. On an ordinary night, nobody notices. On the wrong night, that's the whole story.



Ask anyone who fights fires for a living how they set up their own homes, and you won't hear about gadgets or a closet full of expensive gear. You'll hear about placement. What sits next to what, what runs while everybody's asleep, and where the fuel is.



I'm Frederick Barnes. This one's for anyone responsible for a house full of people who trust them, and by the end you'll have fifteen ways to set up your home the firefighter's way, nearly all of them free and all of them doable tonight.



Fire crews have a plain name for the thing we're organizing around. They call it the fuel load, and it just means everything in a room that can burn. You can't get rid of it, because you live in it. The couch, the curtains, the clothes, the paper. What you can decide is where it sits in relation to heat, and that one idea runs through every item on this list.



We'll start where the house works hardest, the laundry room, and finish at the bedroom door. The one waiting at number one costs nothing and takes about a second, and it does more for your family at three in the morning than anything else I'll show you. Hold onto that. We'll get there.



Number fifteen. The dryer.



A dryer tumbling a load of towels is about as ordinary as a house gets, and nearly every time, nothing happens. The trouble is lint. Every load leaves some behind, and lint may be the best fuel a house makes all on its own. It's light and dry, and it collects right up against a heating element and a hot duct.



The screen catches a lot of it, but not all of it. What slips past the screen settles in the duct behind the machine. A clogged duct holds the heat in, so the dryer runs longer and hotter, and all that lint sits in there getting baked, load after load.



So in a house set up the firefighter's way, the lint screen gets cleared every single load. Not every few loads, every one. Then, once in a while, pull the dryer away from the wall and look at the hose running out the back. If it's the thin foil kind that crumples in your hand, swap it for a rigid or semi-rigid metal duct, and keep the run as short and straight as the room allows. After that, go outside while the dryer's running and find the vent on the side of the house. The flap should be open, and you should feel warm air pushing out against your hand.



I've pulled a dryer away from a wall and found a gray carpet of lint on the floor behind it, the kind nobody sees until somebody goes looking.



Your tell is clothes that suddenly take two cycles to dry, or a machine that's hot on top when it's done. That's a blocked duct asking for attention. Clean it out yourself, or have a duct cleaning service do the long runs. And keep that dryer in mind, because it comes back near the top of the list.



Number fourteen is right next door, in the closet or the corner of the basement where the furnace and the water heater live.



Those two machines make heat, and often a flame, all day long. And in a lot of homes, the space around them turns into free storage. You'll find boxes of holiday decorations, the mop bucket, a stack of old paint cans, the recycling that never made it out to the curb.



The furnace is fine. The cardboard leaning against it is what worries me.



People who deal with fire think in circles. Picture a circle about three feet out around anything that makes heat. That means the furnace, the water heater, a wood stove, the space heater in the bedroom. Inside that circle, nothing that burns. Go stand in the doorway of that utility space tonight, draw the circle in your head, and look at what's inside it.



There's a second half to this one. Anything that gives off fumes, like gasoline, paint thinner, or some aerosol cans, never shares a room with a gas appliance that has a pilot light or a burner. Those vapors are heavier than air. They drift low along the floor, and a pilot light sits right down there near the floor waiting for them. Move the gas and the thinner out to a garage shelf well away from anything with a flame, or to a detached shed.



And the propane cylinder for the grill doesn't belong in any of those places. Where it actually lives is coming up, and so is the door at number one, the item I most want you to hear.

Number thirteen is in the living room, and it's under your feet.



In a firefighter's house, you can see every cord from end to end. None of them run under rugs, none of them squeeze through a doorway where the door closes on them, and none of them are pinned behind the couch with a furniture leg sitting on top.



I know why the rug happens. A cord across the floor looks messy, somebody trips on it, so it slides under the runner and the room looks tidy again. The problem is everything the rug hides. Every time someone walks across that spot, the cord gets pressed and bent. Over months, the insulation wears thin, and the wire underneath can start to heat right where it's damaged. The rug on top traps that heat and keeps you from ever seeing it. Heat that can't escape soaks into whatever is touching it, and here, that's a rug.



Behind the couch works the same way. The weight of the furniture can pull a plug halfway out of the outlet, and a plug that's halfway in makes a poor connection. A poor connection runs warm, and it runs a little warmer every time something switches on.



The organizing move is simple. If a cord has to cross a walkway to reach a lamp, move the lamp or rearrange the room so it doesn't. Pull the couch a few inches away from the wall so nothing gets crushed. And while you're back there in the dust, look at the plug. If it wobbles in the outlet, sags out of it, or the faceplate feels warm under your palm, stop there. You've done your part by noticing it. What's behind the plate is for a licensed electrician.



Number twelve is in the same room, probably down on the floor beside the TV.



The power strip. Plenty of them are perfectly good. The trouble is what we ask them to carry. You've got a strip plugged into another strip, holding a phone charger, a lamp, the TV, a game console, and in winter a space heater, all feeding off one wall outlet that never agreed to any of it.



A power strip doesn't make more electricity. It just gives you more places to plug into the same circuit. Everything you add pulls from the same supply, and the more you pull, the harder that wiring works and the warmer it runs.



So here's how this gets organized. One strip per outlet, and never a strip plugged into another strip. That's called daisy-chaining, and it's one of the first things a fire inspector will point at in any house. Anything built to make heat goes straight into the wall, never into a strip and never into an extension cord. That covers the space heater, the toaster, the microwave, the coffee maker, and the hair dryer. Those appliances pull hard, and they'll overwhelm a strip that's perfectly happy running a lamp and a phone.



Then there's the extension cord. An extension cord is for a job that ends, like the leaf blower on a Saturday or the lights on the porch in December. If one has been powering the same lamp behind the bookshelf for years, it's been drafted into the house wiring without anybody asking it. When a room keeps needing cords like that, it's telling you it needs another outlet, and adding one is an electrician's work.



Number eleven is for anything with a battery, and this one is sitting on a nightstand or tucked into a couch cushion in a lot of homes right now.



Phones, tablets, laptops, the kids' handheld games, and more and more, e-bikes and scooters parked by the door. Nearly all of it runs on lithium batteries, and nearly all of it is fine. The trouble is a damaged battery, a cheap charger that doesn't match the device, or a battery charging somewhere its heat has no way out.



When one of these batteries fails, it doesn't just quietly smoke. It can go fast and very hot, and it's stubborn to put out.



So give your house one charging spot. Pick a hard, clear surface, like a stretch of kitchen counter or a shelf, where nothing soft touches the device and where you'd see it if something went wrong. Not under a pillow, not on the bed, not wedged between couch cushions. Use the charger that came with the device, or a replacement from a maker you trust that carries a safety certification mark. For the e-bikes and scooters, charge them where someone's awake to keep an eye on them, never overnight while the house sleeps, and never parked in the hallway or beside the door you'd need to get out.



Your tells here are a battery that's started to swell, a phone that's too hot to hold while it charges, or a charger that gives off the smell of hot plastic. Any one of those, and that thing is done. Unplug it and replace it.



Number ten takes us into the kitchen, and it's the surface right in front of you.



The stovetop. In a lot of kitchens it's quietly become a shelf. The paper towel roll stands beside it, oven mitts hang off the handle, a dish towel drapes over the oven door, and somebody sets the pizza box down on the back burners because the counter was full.



Cooking is where a great many house fires begin, and it rarely starts with the food. It starts with something that burns sitting a few inches from a burner, and then a knob gets bumped by a hip, or a pot boils over, or the wrong burner gets turned on.



The firefighter's kitchen keeps the cooktop bare when it's not in use, and keeps a clear zone around it when it is. Towels and mitts live in a drawer or on a hook away from the stove. The paper towel holder moves over to the far side of the sink. And nothing gets stored inside the oven, because sooner or later somebody preheats it with a stack of plastic containers still inside.



Then there's what you can't see. Once in a while, pull the stove out and look behind it. Grease drips down the sides and collects back there for years, right beside the heat. Wipe it out and push the stove back.



Number nine is two things that belong near the stove, and one of them belongs closer than you'd think.



The first is a lid. A plain lid that fits your biggest frying pan, sitting on the counter within arm's reach any time you're cooking with oil. If grease catches fire in a pan, you never reach for water. Water hits hot grease, flashes into steam, and throws burning oil across the kitchen. You turn off the burner, slide the lid over the pan, and leave it there until the pan is completely cool. Don't carry the pan anywhere. That's the fire triangle doing the work for you. Fire needs heat, fuel, and oxygen, and the lid takes the oxygen away.



The second is a fire extinguisher, and where it hangs matters. A lot of kitchens mount it right over the stove, which means reaching through the fire to get to it. In a firefighter's kitchen it lives near the way out of the room, so when you go to grab it, you're already headed toward the exit. Get a multipurpose one rated for the kinds of fires you find in a house, and glance at the gauge now and then to make sure the needle is sitting in the green.



A word on using it. An extinguisher is for a small fire you caught early, with a clear way out behind you. If the fire is bigger than a wastebasket, if it's climbing the wall, or if the room is filling with smoke, you don't fight it. Get everyone out and call for help from outside.



Number eight is out in the garage, and the garage is where the fuel load gets heavy.



There's gas for the mower, paint, thinner, and stain, a stack of flattened boxes, maybe the old couch nobody's hauled away yet. And there are rags, the ones used to wipe on a deck stain or oil a cutting board.



Those rags are the ones I want you to find tonight. Certain oils, linseed oil and plenty of oil-based stains, give off heat as they dry. Crumple those rags into a pile or toss them in a trash can, and that heat has nowhere to go. It builds, and a pile of oily rags can catch fire all by itself, with no spark and nobody home.



The firefighter's way is to lay those rags out flat, outdoors, and let them dry completely. Or drop them into a metal can with a tight lid, fill it with water, and keep it there until you can dispose of them properly.



Gasoline lives in a proper fuel container, capped tight, on a shelf away from the water heater if yours sits out there. Keep only what you'll use this season.



Then there's the door between the garage and the house. Keep it shut, and don't prop it open with the recycling bin for convenience. That door is there to slow smoke and fire coming in from the garage, and it can only do that when it's closed.



Number seven only matters about half the year, and that half is almost here.



Fireplace and wood stove ash. Ash looks finished. You scoop it out the next morning, it's gray and cool on top, and it feels safe. Underneath, buried coals can stay hot for days. Dump them into a paper bag, or into the garbage can against the side of the garage, and they'll find the first thing that burns.



In a firefighter's house, ash has one home, and that's a metal bucket with a metal lid. The bucket goes outside, on concrete or bare dirt, a good ten feet from the house and clear of the deck and the woodpile. It stays there until the ash is cold all the way down, and even then it gets soaked with water before it goes out with the trash.



The woodpile itself gets stacked away from the house, never against the siding. And before that first fire of the season, have the chimney swept and inspected. What builds up inside a chimney is fuel you'll never see from the living room, and cleaning it out is a job for a chimney sweep.



Number six moves back inside, to the dinner table and the nightstand.



Candles. I'm not going to tell you to throw them out. A candle on the table while the family is sitting around it is fine. What goes wrong with candles tends to be the same few things. Someone leaves the room, someone falls asleep, a curtain drifts toward the flame, or the cat swishes past.



So a candle gets about a foot of clear space all around it, a sturdy holder that won't tip, and it goes out every time the last person leaves the room. Bedrooms get no candles at all.



Then comes the swap that matters most. When the power goes out, a lot of households light candles to get through the evening, and that puts open flame all over a dark house full of tired people. The organizing move is a flashlight in every bedroom, in the nightstand drawer, where you could find it with your eyes closed. Check the batteries twice a year, when the clocks change.



Hang onto that flashlight. It's coming back when we talk about getting out.



Number five is out back, and it's where the propane finally goes.



The grill. Plenty of people grill straight through the fall, so this one isn't done for the year. A grill belongs well out from the house, away from the siding, clear of the deck railings, and never tucked under the eaves or a low branch. Never use it in the garage or under a covered porch. Clean out the grease tray regularly, because that tray is a little pan of fuel sitting right under a big flame.



Now, the propane cylinder I told you had no business indoors. It lives outside, standing upright, and that goes for the spare too. It stays out of the house, out of the garage, and out of the basement. When you're done cooking, close the valve on the tank. At the start of the season, brush a little soapy water on the hose and the connections and turn the gas on. If you see bubbles growing, you've got a leak. Shut the tank off, don't light it, and replace the part before you cook on it.



Number four is overhead, and it's the one thing on this list that's supposed to make noise.



Smoke alarms. A house set up the firefighter's way has one inside every bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, and at least one on every level, basement included. If your alarms can be connected so that when one sounds they all sound, that's worth doing. An alarm going off in the basement doesn't help much when you're asleep upstairs, and it helps even less once you start doing number one.



The organizing part is putting them on the calendar. Press the test button on each one once a month. Then take one down and look at the back, where you'll find a date. Smoke alarms retire at ten years, even when they still beep on the test. Write the date on each one in marker when you put it up, so nobody has to guess later.



If anything in your house burns fuel, like a gas furnace, a gas water heater, or an attached garage, you want carbon monoxide alarms too, near where people sleep.



And if an alarm chirps once every so often, it's telling you the battery is low or the alarm has reached the end of its service. Replace what it's asking for. Never just pull the battery to make the chirping stop.



Number three is about getting out, and here's where the flashlight earns its drawer.



Every room where somebody sleeps needs two ways out, usually the door and a window. This is where a house can get organized against you without anybody noticing. The dresser ends up in front of the bedroom window because it's the only wall it fits on. The window has been painted shut since before you bought the place. The hallway slowly turns into storage, with laundry baskets and a box of books, so your first way out becomes an obstacle course in the dark.



Tonight, go into each bedroom and open the window all the way. Make sure nothing heavy stands in front of it. On an upper floor, keep an escape ladder stored near that window. Then clear the hallway so it's a straight shot from every bedroom door to the stairs.



Put a plan together, and pick one spot outside where everybody meets, like the mailbox or a neighbor's tree. Walk the kids through it in daylight, then once after dark. If an aging parent lives with you or on their own, walk it with them on your next visit, because a route that's easy for you might not be easy for them.



That flashlight in the nightstand is your light for this. Smoke swallows a hallway fast. Stay low, where the air is clearer, and follow the path you already cleared.



Number two is more habit than place, and it takes us back to the dryer in the laundry room.



Nothing that makes heat runs while nobody's watching. The dryer and the dishwasher don't run after bedtime or while the house is empty. The curling iron and the flat iron get unplugged the moment you're done, and they cool on a heat-proof mat instead of the bathroom counter next to a towel. The space heater goes off when you leave the room and every time you go to sleep. And you don't walk away from a pan on the stove. If the doorbell rings, the burner goes off before you answer it.



I understand how it happens. You start a load before bed so it'll be done in the morning. You get warm under the blanket with the heater humming and drift off. Nobody decides to leave something running all night. The day just ends before the machine does.



So the firefighter's house has a last walk before the lights go out. Check the stove knobs, check that the dryer is quiet and the heater is off, check that the candles are out and the chargers are off the bed. It takes a couple of minutes, and it ends at the one thing I've been saving.



Number one. The one I asked you to hold onto at the very start.



Close the bedroom doors when you go to sleep.



That's the whole thing. It costs nothing, and it takes a second.



Here's why it sits at the top. A lot of what fills a modern home is synthetic, and it burns hot and fast. Once a fire gets going, smoke and heat move through an open house far quicker than you'd expect. A closed door puts a barrier between the people sleeping in that room and all of it. Fire researchers who've tested this found the room behind a closed door stays far cooler and clearer than the room down the hall with its door left open. They've been telling families for years to close before you doze.



If your kids want the door open, give them a nightlight in the room and use a monitor if you need to hear them. This is also why number four matters so much. With the door shut, you want an alarm inside that room, connected to the others.



And if you ever wake to an alarm, put the back of your hand against the door and the knob before you open it. If either one is hot, leave it shut and use your second way out, the window you cleared tonight.

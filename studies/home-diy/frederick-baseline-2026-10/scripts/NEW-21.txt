The stretch between the first whiff of hot plastic and your hand on the front door is longer than it used to be, and the fire uses every bit of it.

Number ten. The space heater at your feet.



A space heater by the recliner is a reasonable thing to own, and a newer one that shuts itself off when it tips is a decent piece of equipment. \[matter-of-fact\] The trouble is the inches. The three feet of clear floor it needs on every side gets eaten a little at a time, by the blanket you pulled off the back of the chair, by the end of the curtain, by the newspaper that slid off the side table. Heat that can’t get away doesn’t just sit there. It soaks into whatever’s closest, and a blanket that’s been taking that heat for two hours is a lot closer to catching than it looks. That’s the enemy for most of tonight, trapped heat, and I’ll point at it every time it shows up. Same rule by the bed, where a heater on the floor and a comforter hanging over the edge are a few inches from being one object. \[emphatic\] Here’s what to do tonight. Before you leave the chair, the heater goes off and the plug comes out, and it never runs while you sleep. Three feet clear in every direction, and if the cord or the plug ever feels warm, it’s past its service, and it goes in the trash, not the closet.



Number nine. The bathroom heater.



I know why it’s there. \[slightly amused\] A cold bathroom floor at six in the morning is its own kind of miserable, and a little plug-in heater on the tile fixes that in ten minutes. The problem is everything else in that room. Water on the floor, a towel hanging a foot above the grille, a bath mat that migrates, and a cord lying across tile that’s still damp from the shower. Hot coils and water don’t mix, and a towel that drifts down onto a running heater while you’re in the next room has nobody watching it. Small things get forgiven, and a heater the size of a toaster gets forgiven most of all. The fix is free. It runs while you’re standing in the room and never otherwise, and it gets unplugged, not just switched off, the moment you walk out. If you want warmth in there overnight, that’s a job for the furnace, not a cord.



Number eight. The nightlight.



\[warm\] A nightlight is a good idea after sixty, and I’d rather you had one than not. Most of them are fine. The ones that worry me are the older style with a real bulb that gets hot, plugged into a low outlet behind a curtain, under a hanging towel, or tucked beside the bed where the dust ruffle brushes it every time somebody sits down. Fabric and a hot bulb, pressed together for eight hours, every night, for a year. \[quietly\] Trapped heat again, just smaller and more patient. Tonight, put your hand near each one after it’s been on an hour. Warm is normal. Hot means it’s done. And give every one of them a clear few inches of air, nothing draped over it, nothing leaning against it. If it’s a cheap one from a bin and it feels hot, swap it for an LED. They run cool, and that’s the whole point.

Number seven. The power strip behind the bed.



\[slightly amused\] Your power strip was never built to be furniture. It was built for a desk, and then one day it got pushed behind the headboard, and over the next five years it picked up a lamp, a clock, two chargers, a heating pad, and a fan, along with the dust that collects anywhere nobody vacuums. Dust is fuel, and a strip that’s a little loose on one plug runs a little warmer every time the lamp comes on, and the bedding that hangs down on top of it holds that warmth in like a lid. Firefighters have a habit of looking behind the headboard first, and there’s a reason. \[matter-of-fact\] Tonight, pull it out and look at it in good light. Any brown around a socket, any plug that wobbles, any smell of hot plastic, and it’s done. Replace it with one that has a breaker and a rating printed on the back, and then give it less to carry. The heating pad and the blanket and the heater never go on a strip. Those plug straight into the wall or they don’t plug in at all. And keep the strip up off the floor, out from under the bedding, somewhere you’d notice if it started looking tired.



Number six. The charger under the pillow.



The charger that came in the box with the phone is fine. The gas station replacement is another story, and where it ends up is the bigger one. \[thoughtful\] People keep the phone close at night after sixty for good reasons, the pharmacy might call, the kids might call, and if something goes wrong in the house, that phone is the way out. So it goes under the pillow, or down in the sheets, with the little brick still warm against the cotton. Trapped heat, one more time. A charger that’s doing its job gives off a little warmth and gets rid of it into the air. Put it under a pillow and there’s no air to get rid of it into, and the warmth piles up hour after hour against the one thing in the room your face is resting on. This one doesn’t cost a dime. Charger plugs into the wall, phone sits on the nightstand, cord runs across open air, not under anything. Close enough to grab in the dark, which I’ll come back to, and far enough that nothing’s lying on it. If a charger ever feels hot rather than warm, or the cord’s gone stiff or frayed at the end, retire it.



Number five. The electric blanket that’s older than your grandkids.



An electric blanket is a comfort, and a newer one with an automatic shut-off is a fair thing to own. The wires inside a blanket are thin, and they get folded in the same spot every single night, the same crease at the foot of the bed, the same bunch where it gets tucked under. \[slower\] Ten years of that and a wire inside gets tired at one point, and a tired wire runs hot right there, in a spot you can’t see, under a layer of fabric that holds the heat in. Any blanket over about ten years old is past its service, and I’d say the same even if it looks fine, because the wear is inside. Tonight, pull yours out and run your hands slowly over the whole thing with it switched off. Any lump, any thin spot, any place that’s been warmer than the rest, any scorch mark, any crease that won’t flatten, and it’s done. \[warm\] Thank it for its service and let it go. And a blanket that stays never runs while you sleep, never gets folded or tucked tight while it’s on, and never, ever gets another blanket piled on top of it.



Number four. The heating pad you fell asleep on.



\[sighs\] Nobody sets out to sleep on it. You lie down on your side with the pad on your hip for twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes turns into the whole night. I know why. It feels good, and you’re tired. The danger is in two places. The pad is under your weight, so the heat has nowhere to go but into you and into the mattress, and the controller is wherever it fell, pressed between the mattress and the wall. Older pads with no automatic shut-off will run until somebody turns them off, and if that somebody is asleep, they run all night, every night, until a wire gives up. So here’s the rule, and it’s a hard one. \[emphatic\] Auto shut-off only. If the pad you own doesn’t turn itself off after an hour or two, it’s retired tonight, not next month. And no pad, even a good one, ever gets slept on. Warm up before bed, switch it off, pull the plug, and set it on the floor, not in the bed. That’s it. The whole fix is unplugging it before your eyes close.



Number three. The lift chair, and the bed that moves.



A lift chair or an adjustable bed is one of the better things to have in the house after sixty, and the motor in it is nothing to be afraid of. \[matter-of-fact\] What I want you to look at is the cord, because the cord has a hard life. Every time the chair rises or the bed tilts, the frame moves, and the cord lies somewhere in the path of that frame. Usually it’s pinched between the base and the carpet, or run under a caster, or crushed against the wall when the back goes down. Same spot, every time, twice a day, for years. A crushed cord dies slowly. It frays a little inside the jacket, the copper gets thinner at one point, and that one point runs warmer each time the motor pulls current through it, with the carpet right there underneath. Tonight, get a flashlight and look at the full length of the cord with the chair or bed in both positions, up and down. Any flat spot, any kink, any place where the jacket’s cracked or shiny from rubbing, and it needs replacing, and that’s usually a call to the company, not a splice job on the kitchen table. Then route the cord so nothing moving can ever touch it, and plug it straight into the wall. Spotting it is on you. Fixing it isn’t.



Number two. The scooter charging in the hallway.



Now we’re on the way out, which is the point. A mobility scooter or a power chair runs on a big battery, and more of them are lithium every year. \[quietly\] When a lithium battery lets go, it doesn’t give you a warning you can act on. It doesn’t smolder, it vents, and it throws heat and flame out fast enough that you don’t get to walk over and unplug it. And where does it charge? In the hallway, because that’s where it parks. By the front door, because that’s where it’s easiest to roll out. \[slower\] Which means the one fire in this list that nobody can put out is sitting in the one place in the house you need clear. Tonight, two moves. Charge it while you’re awake and in the house, and unplug the charger before bed. And if it has to sit by the door, then it sits off to the side, with the path from the bedroom to the door and the door itself clear of it. If the battery ever swells, or gets hot while charging, or the charger’s light does something it never used to do, stop charging it and call the dealer. That’s not a tomorrow problem.



Number one. The oxygen concentrator.



\[thoughtful\] I told you at the top that people trust this one completely, and they should, because a doctor sent it home with them, and it’s doing exactly the job it was sent to do. It stays on. I’m not telling you otherwise, and nothing about tonight changes that. What changes is where it sits and what sits near it. A concentrator takes the air in the room and makes it richer in oxygen, and oxygen is the third side of the fire triangle, heat, fuel, and air. On its own, it has never started a fire. Feeding one is a different matter. A room that’s been running a concentrator all night has more oxygen in it than a room that hasn’t, and it gets into the fabric, the hair, the chair cushion, the bedding. A tiny flame that would’ve gone out on its own in another room burns hotter and faster in this one, and the people who’ve seen it will tell you it’s a different kind of fast. \[emphatic\] So the rules are the strictest on the list. The concentrator plugs directly into the wall, never a power strip, never an extension cord, because this is one of the few things in the house that really does have to run all night and it deserves a solid connection. It sits well away from anything that makes heat, the space heater from number ten, the lamp, the radiator, the stove if it’s near the kitchen. That means no candles in the room, and no cigarettes in the room or anywhere in the house while oxygen is running. Keep the filters clean and the tubing out from under the furniture so it isn’t getting kinked, and if the unit ever smells hot or sounds different, that’s a call to the supplier, not something to tinker with.

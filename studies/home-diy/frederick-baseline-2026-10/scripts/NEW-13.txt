Sign number seven. The flicker you’ve been blaming on the bulb.



\[matter-of-fact\] One lamp that stutters when you switch it on is usually just a bulb at the end of its service, or a bulb that isn’t seated right. Give it a quarter turn, swap it out, and if the problem leaves with the bulb, that was the whole story. The flicker that belongs on tonight’s list behaves differently. It shows up in a whole fixture, or across half a room. The kitchen lights sag for a beat when the refrigerator hums to life. The bedroom lamp dims every time the air conditioner kicks on. Or lights stutter on their own, in more than one room, with nobody touching a switch.



That pattern says the problem isn’t in the bulb. It’s behind the wall, where the circuit is struggling. Sometimes the circuit is simply carrying more than it was built to carry, so every big appliance that wakes up steals light from everything else. And sometimes it’s our enemy making his first appearance. A loose connection is a joint where the wire and its screw have let go of each other, just slightly. The electricity still gets across, but it has to fight for it, and the fight makes heat, right there inside the wall where nothing can cool it and nobody can see it.



So tonight, walk the house with the big appliances running and watch the lights. One bulb acting up, change the bulb. A pattern, the same rooms, the same moment, the same appliance, write down which lights and when, because that’s exactly the map an electrician needs. \[emphatic\] Flickering that spans rooms is never a bulb problem, and chasing it behind the wall is their work, not yours.



Your eyes have done their part for now. Sign six belongs to the back of your hand.



Sign number six. Heat where no heat belongs.



The ordinary cases first. A dimmer switch runs a little warm because that’s how dimmers are built, and a phone charger gets mildly warm while it’s charging. Neither of those should worry you. A standard light switch, though, or an everyday outlet, should feel like the wall around it. Room temperature. Nothing more.



\[slower\] So as you move through the house tonight, lay the back of your hand flat against the switch plates and outlet covers. Not a quick tap. Let it rest there a moment. I’ve put my palm on a plate that carried warmth like a coffee mug on a coaster, while the lamp plugged into it sat switched off. That warmth is the loose connection again. Somewhere behind that cover, current is squeezing through a joint that’s lost its grip, and the heat soaks into the plastic, the metal box, and the framing lumber behind it. Wood that gets gently baked for months doesn’t need much persuading later.



If a plate feels warm, unplug whatever’s on that outlet and come back in an hour. Still warm with nothing running means the problem lives on the inside, and the inside of a wall is a licensed electrician’s country. Your job ends at the cover plate. And keep a thumb on that warmth, so to speak, because at sign two I’ll take you to the one door in your house where this same heat likes to show its face early, and almost nobody looks.

Sign number five. A wall with something to say.



Your house is full of honest noise. The refrigerator hums, the furnace fan breathes, and a cheap charger can whine so faintly you need young ears to catch it. That’s machinery, and machinery is allowed to sound like machinery. A wall is not. A healthy outlet or switch has nothing inside it that moves, so it has nothing inside it that should make sound. A wall that buzzes is electricity jumping a gap it was never meant to jump, a spark happening over and over, so fast it blurs into a hum. Our loose connection again, grown wide enough to sing.



Tonight’s check costs five quiet minutes. \[quietly\] Turn off the television, let the house settle, and bring your ear close to the switches and outlets you use most, flipping each switch once as you listen. Any pop, any crackle, any steady buzz from inside the wall, and that switch or outlet goes out of service tonight while you write down where it lives. A dimmer humming faintly at half brightness can be ordinary. A buzzing anything else has earned the phone call. And sound coming from the electrical panel itself skips the list entirely and goes straight to a licensed electrician, because arcing near the place where all the power enters the house is nobody’s homework.



Which brings us to your nose, and the smell I promised you. Sign four.



Sign number four. Fish, in a house where nobody’s cooking fish.



This is the sign that saves houses, mostly because it’s strange enough to remember. The plastics and coatings inside your electrical system, the insulation on the wires, the guts of your outlets and switches, are built to shrug off heat for decades. When a bad connection finally runs hot enough to start cooking them, the fumes they give off carry a sharp, oily odor that most noses read as fish. Sometimes it leans toward hot plastic, sometimes it has a sweetish chemical edge, but fish with no fish in the kitchen is the classic. And because it’s chemistry instead of dinner, it stays local. Strongest near one outlet, one switch, one stretch of wall, and it often comes and goes with whatever runs on that circuit.



\[matter-of-fact\] Rule out the ordinary first, the dishwasher, the trash, last night’s pan. Then follow your nose along the walls, slow and low, close to the outlets. If the smell sharpens near anything electrical, and warm counts double here, unplug what’s on it and leave it alone. I’ll be straight with you about the escalation, too. \[emphatic\] A strong electrical burning smell you cannot trace is the one entry on tonight’s list where tonight means right now. Kill the breaker to that area if you know which one it is, make the call, and let a professional find what your nose already found. That smell means material has already begun breaking down. It is the last polite warning before smoke.



Nose, done. Sign three wants your eyes back, but from six inches away this time.



Sign number three. The outlet that’s started leaving evidence.



Get down to eye level with the outlets that work hardest, the one behind the coffee maker, the one feeding the television wall, the bedroom one buried in chargers. You’re looking for color where color doesn’t belong. A brown or amber shadow creeping out from the slots. A gray smudge across the cover. Prongs that come out of the wall darkened, or a cord end with a faint melted sheen, like it sat near a stove it never touched. Paint and age can stain an old plate, that’s fair, but age spreads its discoloration evenly. Scorch gathers at the slots, right where the current lives.



\[thoughtful\] Marks like that are a diary. They mean heat has already visited, probably more than once, and the loose connection signs its work in exactly this handwriting. While you’re down there, run the grip test. Push a plug in and let go. A healthy outlet holds it like a firm handshake. If the plug sags, tips, or slides out under the weight of its own cord, the springs inside are worn, and a loose grip at the face is a loose connection you can feel with two fingers. Weak contact makes heat, heat tires the springs further, and the outlet fails slowly while looking perfectly fine from the couch.



The response is simple. Anything wearing scorch retires from service tonight, and an outlet that can’t hold a plug never feeds a heater, an air conditioner, or an iron again. Swapping an outlet is a small, quick job for an electrician, and your notes on which ones are the half of the job you’ve already finished.

Sign number two. The breaker your family has learned to live with.



Find your electrical panel, the gray or beige box in the garage, the basement, or a hallway closet, and open the door. \[matter-of-fact\] The door, you may open. The cover behind it, the one held on with screws, stays shut forever as far as you and I are concerned, because everything behind that cover is live, and it belongs to a licensed electrician and no one else.



A breaker that trips once in a great while, say the morning the space heater, the kettle, and the microwave all asked at once, is the system doing its job. A trip is a guard slamming a door because too much was coming through it. The sign we care about is the breaker that trips every week. The same breaker, the same rooms going dark, and a household response worn down to muscle memory. In plenty of homes, flipping that one breaker back has become part of the evening routine, as automatic as the porch light, and nobody has ever once asked the breaker what it keeps objecting to. Every reset feeds the same overload back into the same wires, and insulation that gets cooked a little every week does not heal between rounds.



And I owe you the warm plate from sign six. Heat born at a bad connection travels, and the panel is where every circuit in the house comes home, so trouble often shows its face here early. With the inner cover untouched, pass the back of your hand near the breakers. One breaker clearly warmer than its neighbors, any darkening or melt around a breaker, any buzz from inside the box, and the call happens this week, not this year. \[thoughtful\] Hear the harder truth while the door’s open, too. A breaker is a last line, not a promise. A connection can run hot for months without ever pulling enough current to trip anything, which is exactly why your palm, your ears, and your nose walked the house tonight before you ever reached this door.



Write down which breaker trips and what goes dark when it does, close the door gently, and come with me, because sign number one was never in the panel at all.



Sign number one. The extension cord you can’t put a date on.



I told you it makes no flicker, no buzz, and no smell. The test for it is one honest question. Somewhere in your house there’s an extension cord or a power strip, and the question is simply, when did it go in? If the answer comes easily, last month, for the holiday lights, for the shop vac, good. That’s what extension cords are for, jobs with an end. If the honest answer is that it came with the room, that it’s been feeding the television wall or the bedside chargers or the garage freezer since before you can remember, you’ve found the hazard that outranks everything else tonight, because it manufactures the others. \[slightly amused\] An extension cord is built to be a guest. In an awful lot of houses, one of them has quietly become a resident.



Think about what that cord has been doing. It’s thinner than the wiring in your walls. It was sized for short visits in open air, and now it runs under a rug where its heat has nowhere to go, or through a doorway where the door has been biting the same inch of it for years, or behind a couch in a drift of dust, or into a power strip that feeds a second power strip. And every plug seated in every socket along that chain is one more joint, one more place for our enemy to move in and start loosening.



So tonight, follow every cord in the house to both of its ends. Anything under a rug or pinched in a door comes out now, not Saturday. Space heaters, window air conditioners, anything that makes heat or moves real power, plugs straight into the wall or it doesn’t run. And the cord nobody can date gets retired, because a cord that has turned permanent is a request for another outlet, written in the only language the house has, and adding outlets is an electrician’s trade, not a weekend hobby.

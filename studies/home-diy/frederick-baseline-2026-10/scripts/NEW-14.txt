Nothing humming out in the garage. It isn’t fear of their own wiring. It’s that they know exactly where that glow can lead, and they’d rather not follow it there.

And the hour matters more than most people realize. Fewer fires start at night than in the daytime, because we’re awake for the daytime ones and we catch them early. \[matter-of-fact\] But of the people who die in home fires, roughly half die during the sleeping hours. A fire at three in the morning gets the one thing no daytime fire gets — a head start on a houseful of closed eyes.



So we’ll count them down, fifteen to one. And the thing sitting at number one — ten years ago, most houses didn’t own a single one. It has climbed to the top of this list the hard way, and we’ll get there.



\[short pause\] Number fifteen. The holiday lights. A modern set, certified and healthy, lit for the evening — nobody loses sleep over that. The worry is the old box from the attic: the strand with a crushed section, insulation gone stiff and cracked, stapled across a gutter and soaking up a week of weather. And the inflatables — inside every one of them is a little electric blower, and it runs every second that snowman stands, wet or dry. The fix is free. The lights go dark when you do, and a cheap plug-in timer will do the remembering for you. Any strand with cracked or bare spots has decorated its last December. \[slightly amused\] They’re seasonal help. Don’t hand them the night shift.



Number fourteen glows softly in the living room. The aquarium heater. Perfectly good gear — as long as it’s underwater, because the water is the entire safety system. The heater makes heat, and the water carries it off. But tanks evaporate. The waterline creeps down, and one day the element is cooking air and bare glass instead. \[thoughtful\] And meet the enemy that will follow us through this whole list: trapped heat. Trapped heat doesn’t sit still — it goes looking for a neighbor. A plastic hood. A curtain hanging too close. So keep the water topped well above the element, mount the heater low and fully submerged, and unplug it every time you drain the tank. If it has ever run dry, or you can see a scorch shadow on the tube, its tour is over. Swap it before the next refill.



Number thirteen waits out in the garage, on the workbench. The power-tool battery charger. Honest tool, honest work — but picture where it spends the night. A lithium pack latched into its charger, on wood, with sawdust drifted around it and a can of something flammable on the next shelf, behind a closed door while the whole house sleeps. And the packs that worry people most aren’t the ones that came with the drill. They’re the bargain look-alikes, built loose and cheap on the inside. So charge your packs while you’re home and awake, on concrete or metal with the bench swept clear, and pull them off the moment the light turns green. \[quietly\] Don’t file this one away yet, either — lithium climbs back to the very top of this list.

Number twelve. The flat iron, and its cousin the curling iron. Ten minutes at the mirror — no argument from anyone. The trouble starts when it leaves your hand: a tool running hotter than a kitchen oven, parked on a counter between a hand towel, a roll of paper, and a can of hairspray, on a morning that got busy. Plenty of older irons have no shut-off at all, and the ones that do take their time. \[matter-of-fact\] So the rule is simple and free. The second it leaves your hand, it leaves the wall. Set it on something that can’t burn, let it cool before it’s put away, and treat any iron that’s still plugged in as hot — because it is.



Number eleven hums in the window. The air conditioner — or its winter cousin, the dehumidifier — running on an extension cord. The machine is fine. The cord is the problem. A compressor starting up is one of the hungriest moments in the house, and a household extension cord is too thin for that job. Electricity forced through wire that skinny turns into heat, right at the plug — which usually lives behind a curtain. If that plug is ever warm under your palm, you’ve caught it early. \[emphatic\] These machines go into the wall, full stop. And if the wall is too far away, the room has outgrown its outlets — that’s a phone call to a licensed electrician, not a longer cord.



Number ten hides in a fist. The vape charger. Inside it sits the same chemistry we met in the garage — a lithium cell — except this one gets charged on a nightstand, on a bargain cable, inches from a pillow, all night long. Small battery, same temper. So keep it in the family and keep it in daylight: the charger it came with, charging while you’re awake, on something hard, and off the plug once it’s full. And a battery that swells, rattles, or gives off a faint sweet chemical smell is done — out of the device, and out of the house.



Number nine is probably within reach of your bed right now. The laptop, charging on the mattress or a couch cushion. Here’s the design problem: the vents that keep it alive are on the bottom, and a duvet seals them the way a hand seals a mouth. The battery is working, the charger is working, and all of that warmth — there’s our enemy again, trapped heat — has nowhere to go but down into the foam and fabric. The cure costs you exactly one new habit. Hard surfaces only: the desk, a tray, the nightstand. \[slightly amused\] The pillow is for your head. And if the underside is ever too hot to rest your palm on, it has been smothered too long.



Number eight. The heating pad — and it earns its spot for one habit alone: falling asleep on it. A pad is built for twenty or thirty minutes of company while you’re awake, not an eight-hour shift under a sleeping body, where your own weight presses its heat into the mattress and holds it there. If yours is old enough to have no automatic shut-off, that fact alone retires it. Use it sitting up, use it lying flat — never under you, never through the night — and when it smells faintly scorched or the cord shows cracks, its working life is over.



Number seven lies on top of the bed. The electric blanket — \[warm\] and I know exactly why people love them. I’m not here to take warm feet away from anyone. Two things turn one dangerous. The first is age: the heating wires flex every time the blanket folds, and a blanket creased in the same spot every night is quietly breaking in that spot. The second is tucking — fold it under the mattress, or pile a comforter or a sleeping dog on top, and its heat is trapped against its own wiring. So run it flat with nothing on it, use it to warm the bed and then switch it off, and let the ten-year-old one go. A decade of winters is a full career.



Number six is under your feet. The extension cord under the rug — and this one is a double. First, the heat a working cord sheds can’t escape through carpet; it builds into the fibers instead. Second, every footstep grinds the insulation down, and the rug hides the wear — damage you’d spot in a week on an open floor. Tonight’s fix is free: pull it out and run it along the wall. \[thoughtful\] But hear the bigger message. A cord that has held the same spot for a year isn’t temporary anymore. It’s wiring, doing a wall’s work, and that room needs a real outlet — which is a licensed electrician’s job, not a weekend’s. Your job tonight was lifting the rug.



Number five, you can picture before I name it. The frayed charger cable — the one wrapped in tape, kinked at the plug, the one that only works when you hold it at the right angle. \[slower\] That angle is a confession. Inside the cord run dozens of fine copper strands, and most of them have already snapped; the current that used to share the load now squeezes through the few survivors, and wire carrying more than its share runs hot, right where the damage lives. A cable you have to coax has told you everything. Retire it tonight — the taped ones, the kinked ones, the ones that only charge on a good day.



Number four is the one most people never see coming, because it looks brand new. The cheap replacement charger — the no-name block from the bargain bin. Turn over the charger that came with your phone and you’ll find small letters pressed into the plastic: U L, or E T L, or C S A. Each mark means an independent safety lab took that design apart and tried its best to make it fail. A charger’s whole job is taming the full force of the wall outlet down to the trickle a phone can drink, and certified ones do it with proper spacing, real insulation, and a fuse standing guard. The knock-offs skip exactly those parts, because those are the costly parts. \[matter-of-fact\] So tonight, flip every charger in the house. No mark, no nightstand. A certified one runs about fifteen to thirty dollars, and it is the cheapest guard you will ever post at your bedside.



Number three, nearly every house gets wrong. The power strip. One under the desk carrying a lamp, a router, and a phone is doing exactly what it was built for. But a strip is a waiter, not a kitchen — it ferries power, it doesn’t make more of it. Load it with a heater, a microwave, a toaster oven, anything whose whole job is heat, and you’ve ordered far more than it can carry. I’ve laid a palm on strips that had no business being that warm. And plugging one strip into another adds no outlets at all — only a second weak link, and twice the length for trouble to hide in. So the big appliances go in the wall. One strip per outlet, a certified strip with its own breaker and a switch — about fifteen to twenty-five dollars — and then use your hand: a strip that feels warm is a strip that’s overloaded. \[emphatic\] And remember that switch. It matters again in a minute.



Number two, you knew was coming. The space heater. The heater itself mostly keeps its promises — the danger is everything within three feet of it, and everyone asleep around it. \[emphatic\] Two rules, no exceptions. It plugs straight into the wall, never a strip and never an extension cord, because this is the hungriest appliance you own, and we just covered what a strip does under that kind of load. And it runs only while you’re awake and in the room. Three feet of clear floor on every side — no drifting curtain, no bedspread hanging off the edge, no laundry waiting nearby. If yours doesn’t shut off when it tips over, its replacement should. A space heater is a campfire with a cord, and nobody sleeps beside an unwatched campfire.



And number one. The e-bike battery — the scooter, the hoverboard, every one of the big lithium packs that moved into our hallways these past few years. Ten years ago, this list wouldn’t have known what to do with it. Today, in the biggest city in this country, the people who investigate fatal fires have spent the last few years writing the same cause on report after report: one of these packs, charging. \[slower\] Here’s why it owns the top spot. When one cell in a damaged or badly charged pack fails, it doesn’t smolder politely — it erupts, and every cell it touches erupts with it, in seconds, with smoke you can’t breathe and a fire that relights after you’re sure it’s out. And where do we charge them? The hallway. Beside the front door. The exact path you’d be feeling for in the dark. \[short pause\] So number one gets the full set of rules.

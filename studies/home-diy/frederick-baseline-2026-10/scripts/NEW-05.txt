The hurricane isn't the thing most likely to burn your house down. The week after it is.



Picture it. The storm came through two nights ago. The wind is gone, the yard is a mess of branches, and the power still isn't back. It's close to midnight. There's a candle on the kitchen counter because nobody could find the good flashlight. There's a generator droning somewhere near the back door, and an orange extension cord pinched under that door, running inside to keep the fridge cold. Everybody's worn out. Everybody's finally asleep.



Most of what you did to get ready was right. The water, the canned food, the batteries, the plywood on the windows. Good. That's what gets you through the storm. It's the few things people do after the storm that I want to talk about tonight, because that's where the fires come from.



I'm Frederick Barnes. This one's for anybody responsible for a house in a hurricane's path, whether it's your own or a parent's a few hours up the road. By the end, you'll know what to set up before the storm, what to do the first night the power's out, and what to watch for the morning it comes back.



Here's what I wish every homeowner understood before a hurricane season ever starts.



A storm doesn't just knock things over. It takes away the everyday things that were quietly keeping you safe. The lights. The stove. The fridge. The phone charger. And the minute those are gone, you go looking for something to fill in. A candle fills in for the light. A generator fills in for the power company. A grill fills in for the stove. An extension cord fills in for the wall.



Call those the stand-ins. And the stand-ins are where storm fires start.



None of them is a villain. A generator is a good machine. Candles lit houses for a few thousand years. The trouble is when you're running them. You're tired. You're sleeping at odd hours. You're cooking in the dark, maybe with water in places water never belongs. And the last few seasons made one thing plain. Hurricane country is bigger than the coast. When Helene came through, it flooded mountain towns that never thought of themselves as storm towns at all. So this isn't only for people with a beach down the street.



Think of a house fire as the last link in a chain. One link is a piece of equipment that's failing, or being used for a job it wasn't built for. One link is a habit. One link is a warning sign nobody acted on. On an ordinary week, it can take months for those three to line up. The week after a hurricane, they can line up in a single night.



So let's start with the one thing that's supposed to catch it when all of that goes wrong. Your smoke alarms.



A lot of homes have hardwired alarms. The kind that are wired into the ceiling and all go off together when one of them smells smoke. People trust those more than the little battery kind, and on a normal night, that trust is fair.



But stay with me here. A hardwired alarm runs on house power. When the power goes out, the only thing keeping it awake is a small backup battery tucked inside. And plenty of people have never once twisted one off the ceiling to look.



So think about the timing. The week you're burning candles and running a generator is the exact week your alarms may be living on a battery nobody's checked in years.



Before the season, press the test button on every alarm in the house. Twist the hardwired ones off their bases and find the backup battery. If it's missing, or you can't remember when it went in, replace it. If an alarm is getting close to ten years on the ceiling, it's done its work. Replace the whole unit. And if you don't have a carbon monoxide alarm that runs on batteries, or has a battery backup, put one on this week's list. A generator makes carbon monoxide. You can't see it, you can't smell it, and an alarm is the only thing in the house that will notice it for you.

Three days out. The sky still looks fine, and this is the best time you'll get, because every decision here gets made in daylight, dry and calm, instead of at eleven at night in the rain.



First, the light plan. One flashlight for every person in the house, kept where they sleep. A battery lantern for the kitchen and one for the bathroom. Spare batteries in one place everybody knows. I know why people reach for candles. They're already in the drawer, and they feel cozy when the house goes dark. But a candle should be the last thing you use, not the plan. Most outage candle fires don't start because somebody chose a candle. They start because nobody chose anything else.



Second, charging. Before a storm, every phone, tablet, and power bank in the house gets plugged in at once. And then the power banks end up on the bed, on the couch, under a pillow while somebody scrolls in the dark. A power bank is a small brick of stored energy. Most of them are fine. The problem is the heat. A battery charging on a mattress has nowhere to shed that heat, so it soaks into the bedding around it. Charge on a hard surface. The kitchen table, the counter, a tile floor. If a power bank ever feels hot, looks swollen, or gives off a sweet chemical smell, it's done. Set it outside, away from the house, and replace it.



Third, the generator spot. Walk outside and pick it now. Somewhere well away from the house, a good twenty feet, with the exhaust pointed away from every door, window, and vent. Not in the garage, even with the big door rolled up. Not in the carport. Not under a porch roof, and not beside the bedroom window you'll crack open when the house gets hot. Carbon monoxide drifts in through openings, and a garage attached to your house is one big opening. Fire marshals repeat this every single season, and every season somebody tries the garage anyway because it's raining. So decide the spot while it's dry. When the rain comes, the decision's already made.



Fourth, the fuel. Store gasoline in containers made for gasoline, outside the house, ideally in a detached shed. Not in the garage next to the water heater or the furnace. Those have flames or hot parts inside them, and gasoline gives off vapor you can't see that creeps along the floor looking for exactly that. Buy what you need, store it right, and keep it away from anything with a pilot light.



Fifth, the cords that run from the generator. This is where the stand-ins stack on top of each other. People grab whatever cord is in the garage, usually a thin indoor one, and run it from the generator to the fridge. Here's the trouble. A cord that's too thin for its load gets hot, and it stays hot for as long as the fridge runs, which is all night. Use a heavy outdoor-rated cord built for the load you're putting on it. The packaging will tell you. Then route it so nothing crushes it. Not under a closed door, and not pinched in a window. A door shut on a cord mashes the insulation in the same spot every time someone walks through, and you'll never see the damage because it's hidden in the jamb. If a generator cord feels warm anywhere along its length, it's telling you it's undersized. Swap it.



Sixth, and this is the big one people get wrong. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet to power the whole house. There are cords sold with a plug on both ends that make this look easy. It's called backfeeding. It pushes power into your house wiring the wrong way. It can send current back out onto the lines where utility crews are working, and it can start a fire inside your walls the moment the real power comes back and meets the generator's power in the same wires. If you want your generator to run the house, the right way is a transfer switch or an interlock, installed by a licensed electrician. That's an electrician's job, and it's one you want finished in June, not the day the storm gets a name.



Seventh, the panel. Find your breaker panel before the storm. Hang a flashlight on a hook right beside it. Learn which switch is the main, the big one that shuts off the whole house. If you're in a spot where water could come inside, and you're standing on dry ground with dry hands, shut the main off before the water gets there. But if water has already reached the floor around the panel, don't go near it. Leave it off, stay out of the water, and call the power company or an electrician.



And last for this stretch. If you're in a flood zone and you own anything with a big lithium battery, an electric car, a golf cart, an e-bike, a scooter, move it to higher ground before the storm if you can. Hold onto that one. It comes back later, and it matters more than people think.



The first night the power goes out.



Start with what was running when it went dark. The stove burner somebody forgot. The iron. The coffee maker. The space heater in the back bedroom. When the power fails, the switches on all of those stay right where they were. So when the power comes back, maybe at three in the morning while everyone's asleep, every one of them turns itself back on. Including the burner with a dish towel folded on top of it. Walk the house the night the power goes out and turn everything off. Unplug the TV, the computer, the microwave. Then leave one lamp switched on, just one, so you'll know the second power returns.



Now, candles. If you end up using one, and plenty of people will, give it a real holder on a flat hard surface, a good foot clear of anything that can catch. Curtains, paper towels, a stack of mail, a kid's reach, a cat's tail. And storm houses get drafty. A curtain that hangs still on a normal night will drift toward a flame when the wind finds a gap. No candles in a room where somebody's going to fall asleep. Blow it out whenever you leave the room, even for a minute. The candle doesn't get tired. You do. And the fix is the flashlight you already bought three days ago.



Next, the generator, running. It goes to the spot you picked, and it stays there. When it runs low on gas, shut it off and let it cool before you refuel. Gasoline splashed on a hot engine, or on a hot muffler, can light before you've finished pouring. So turn it off, give it real time, and refuel by flashlight, never by candle or lighter. Any firefighter who's worked a storm will tell you refueling is when generators bite.



Then, cooking. The grill, the camp stove, the charcoal. All of them stay outside. Not in the kitchen, not in the garage, not on a screened porch with the door shut. Those are fire risks and carbon monoxide risks at the same time, and a small closed room makes both of them worse.



And inside, watch what you plug into the generator cord. People run one cord in, then a power strip, then another power strip off the first, then the fridge, a fan, a lamp, a phone charger, and a coffee maker all hanging off that one line. That's a lot of weight on something thin. Plug the fridge straight into a heavy cord. Keep the rest to the few things you actually need tonight.



The morning the power comes back.



Here's the moment I promised you. The lights come on, the fridge hums, somebody cheers. And if water got into your house during the storm, that's the minute the danger starts.



The storm is over, but electricity doesn't know that. When the current comes back, it runs into whatever the water left behind. Wet outlets. Mud inside the dryer. Salt and grit on the connections in your panel. Corrosion doesn't wash off when the water goes down. A connection that's wet or corroded pushes back against the current, and pushing back makes heat. It can arc. And it can do all of that inside a wall, where you won't see it until it's already burning.



So here's the rule. If floodwater reached your outlets, your panel, or any of your appliances, keep the main off, and have a licensed electrician look the house over before the power goes back on. In a lot of places, the power company won't reconnect a flooded house until it's been inspected anyway. Anything that sat in floodwater, the furnace, the water heater, the washer, the dryer, the outlets along the bottom of the wall, gets checked or replaced before you use it. That feels slow when you want your house back. It's the difference between cleaning up after a flood and cleaning up after a fire.



Now, back to the batteries. Here's the one that catches people off guard. After the big storms on the Gulf Coast these last few years, fire officials in Florida kept warning about electric cars, golf carts, scooters, even power tool batteries that had sat in saltwater. Salt gets inside the battery pack and starts eating at it. And the fire doesn't always come right away. It can come days later, or weeks later, while the car sits in a garage attached to a house full of people. Lithium batteries don't give a lot of notice.



If anything with a big lithium battery sat in floodwater, especially saltwater, get it out of the garage and away from the house, well clear of anything else that burns. Don't charge it. Don't try to start it to see if it works. Call the dealer or the manufacturer and let them handle it from there. The same goes, on a smaller scale, for the power tool batteries that were sitting on the garage floor when the water came in.



Even if your house stayed dry, do a walk-through the day the power returns. Grab a flashlight and go room by room. Use your nose first. Hot plastic, anything smelling scorched. Then your ears. A buzz or a crackle from an outlet or a switch plate. Then your palm, flat on the faceplates. A faceplate should feel like the wall around it. If it's warm, turn that circuit off at the panel and call an electrician. Then plug things back in one at a time, not all at once, so if something trips, you know exactly which one did it. If the same breaker trips again after you reset it, stop resetting it. A breaker that keeps tripping is doing its job, and the job is telling you something's wrong behind that wall. And if you smell gas, don't touch a single switch. Walk out of the house and call from outside.



One last thing, and it isn't about your house at all. If you've got a parent living on their own anywhere a storm could reach, call them this week. Walk the house with them over the phone. Have them press the test button on the smoke alarms while you listen for the beep. Ask where the flashlights are, and where the generator's going to sit, and make sure they know never to plug it into the wall. Ten minutes on the phone before the season starts covers more of that house than any visit after the storm ever could.

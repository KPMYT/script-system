## How to read this

- **Fetched** means the page was opened directly and the wording read from it (most official pages were downloaded and searched as text: EPA, ENERGY STAR, IRS, NFPA, energy.gov PDFs and articles, CPSC PDFs, AGA, PG&E, SoCalGas, Carrier, InterNACHI, OSHA, Kidde, First Alert). Quotes from these are word-for-word.
- **Fetched (tool summary)** means the page was read through a fetch tool that returns a summary. Words inside quotation marks are the page's own; anything outside quotes is the tool's paraphrase. CPSC's HTML pages and CDC's page could only be read this way (both block plain downloads).
- **Via search** means the wording came from the search engine's extract, because the page blocked reading (energy.gov's HTML pages return 404 to readers, ESFI returns 403). Probably right, but not read off the page. Don't quote these on camera without a re-check.
- **Can cite** means the source's own figure or instruction is quoted exactly here, from a fetched page, so the script can say "according to the CPSC" (or whoever). Everything else is **in words**: say it plainly, without naming the source.
- Official sources come first (CPSC, NFPA, CDC, EPA, DOE, ENERGY STAR, IRS, OSHA). Where no official source covers a fact (lifespans, furnace watts, condensate volume), two independent trade or maker sources are used, and the line is "in words".
- "Year" is the page's own "last updated" or publication date where it shows one; otherwise "seen 2026-10-27".
- **Searches used: 22 of 35.** Everything on the list was researched; three small gaps are marked "not found" in the summary.

## 1. Carbon monoxide alarms: where they go

**Say:** "One on every level of the house, and one outside each bedroom area. If you can, get the kind that are linked, so when one goes off they all go off." **Can cite** (CPSC, NFPA).

**Sources**
- CPSC, "Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers", https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center/Carbon-Monoxide-Questions-and-Answers: "Install battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas." "Avoid locations that are near heating vents or that can be covered by furniture or draperies." "CPSC does not recommend installing CO alarms in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances." (no date shown; seen 2026-10-27; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- NFPA, "Carbon Monoxide safety", https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/home-fire-safety/carbon-monoxide: "CO alarms should be installed in a central location outside each sleeping area and on every level of the home and in other locations where required by applicable laws, codes or standards. For the best protection, interconnect all CO alarms throughout the home. When one sounds, they all sound." Also "Follow the manufacturer's instructions for placement and mounting height." "Choose a CO alarm that has the label of a recognized testing laboratory." (page updated 2025-01-17; fetched)
- CPSC news release, "Keep Warm and Safe This Winter: Tips for Using Generators, Furnaces and Space Heaters", https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2026/Keep-Warm-and-Safe-This-Winter-Tips-for-Using-Generators-Furnaces-and-Space-Heaters: CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; battery or battery backup; interconnected best; test monthly; "Replace batteries at least once a year" or use alarms with sealed 10-year batteries (2026-01-23; fetched (tool summary), mostly paraphrase)
- CPSC winter safety sheet NSN-10, https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/NSN-10-012025rv032025_WinterFireCO_Safety.pdf: "Install working CO and smoke alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas." (rev. 03/2025; fetched)
- CDC, "Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning Basics", https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html: "Install battery-operated or battery back-up CO detectors near every sleeping area in your home." "Check CO detector batteries when you change the time on your clocks each spring and fall." (page last reviewed 2026-10-26; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- EPA, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Protect Your Family and Yourself Factsheet", https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/carbon-monoxide-poisoning-protect-your-family-and-yourself-factsheet: "Place them in a central location on every level of the home's livable space (including the basement) and at least 15 feet away from any fuel-burning appliance." "Test alarms once a month." (updated 2026-09-16; fetched)

**Agreement:** agree. Small differences: CPSC and NFPA say "outside sleeping areas"; CDC says "near every sleeping area"; EPA adds "at least 15 feet away from any fuel-burning appliance" and CPSC says not in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances. Test monthly (NFPA, EPA, CPSC).

## 2. Carbon monoxide alarms: how long they last, and the end-of-life chirp

**Say:** "Carbon monoxide alarms wear out. Most are good for about 5 to 10 years, depending on the model, and the date is printed on the back. When one reaches the end, it starts chirping on a set rhythm, and a new battery won't stop it. That chirp means replace the whole alarm." **In words** for the 5-10 years (maker figures). "Replace them according to the manufacturer's instructions" **can cite** (NFPA).

**Sources**
- NFPA (as section 1): "Test CO alarms at least once a month; replace them according to the manufacturer's instructions." "If the audible trouble signal sounds, check for low batteries. If the battery is low, replace it. If it still sounds, call the fire department." (2025; fetched)
- CPSC Q&A (as section 1): "Alarms have a recommended replacement age, which can be obtained from the product literature or from the manufacturer." No number of years. (fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- Kidde, "When Should I Replace My Carbon Monoxide Alarm?", https://www.kidde.com/support/carbon-monoxide-alarms/replacement: "Kidde carbon monoxide alarms have a proven life of seven to ten years, depending on the model type." "In 2009, Underwriters Laboratories (UL) began requiring an end-of-life warning to alert homeowners when their carbon monoxide alarm has reached the end of its useful life." "End-of-Life Warning: The alarm will sound every 30 seconds." "An end-of-life or sensor error will not respond to a battery change." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)
- First Alert, "Carbon Monoxide Detector Beeping", https://www.firstalert.com/blogs/safety-corner/what-does-the-beep-mean: "Most carbon monoxide alarms last between 5 and 7 years." "5 Beeps Every Minute: End of Life." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)
- UL Solutions, "Carbon Monoxide Alarm Considerations for Code Authorities", https://www.ul.com/thecodeauthority/knowledge/carbon-monoxide-alarm-codes: "Newer CO alarms also produce an end-of-life signal that indicates when the device has reached the end of its useful life and should be replaced." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)

**Agreement:** agree on 5-10 years (First Alert 5-7, Kidde 7-10). The chirp rhythm differs by brand (Kidde every 30 seconds; First Alert 5 beeps a minute), so don't give one rhythm as universal. The brief's "most last 5-10 years" is right.

## 3. What to do when a CO alarm sounds (complete, in order)

**Say (in order):**
1. "Never ignore it, and don't go looking for the source."
2. "Get everyone out into fresh air right away. Outside."
3. "Count heads. Make sure everyone, every person in the house, is out."
4. "From outside, call 911 or the fire department."
5. "Stay out until the firefighters say it's safe to go back in."
6. "If they find the furnace or another appliance is the cause, don't run it again until a trained tech has fixed it."
7. "If anyone has a headache, feels dizzy or sick, get them medical help."

**Can cite** (CPSC for 1-6, in CPSC's order; NFPA for 2-5; EPA for 7).

**Sources**
- CPSC Q&A (as section 1): "Never ignore an alarming CO alarm! It is warning you of a potentially deadly hazard." "do not try to find the source of the CO". Then, in order: "Immediately move outside to fresh air." "Call your emergency services, fire department, or 911." "After calling 911, do a head count to check that all persons are accounted for." "DO NOT reenter the premises until the emergency services responders have given you permission." "If the source of the CO is determined to be a malfunctioning appliance, DO NOT operate that appliance until it has been properly serviced by trained personnel." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- NFPA (as section 1): "If the CO alarm sounds, immediately move to a fresh air location outdoors or by an open window or door. Make sure everyone inside the home is accounted for. Call for help from a fresh air location and stay there until emergency personnel." [sic: the sentence ends there on the live page] Also "Call your local fire department's non-emergency number to find out what number to call if the CO alarm sounds." (2025; fetched)
- CPSC, Carbon Monoxide Information Center, https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center: for suspected poisoning, "get outside to fresh air immediately, and then call 911." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- EPA CO factsheet (as section 1): "If you experience symptoms that you think could be from CO poisoning: Get to fresh air immediately. Seek medical attention." (2026; fetched)

**Agreement:** agree on the order: out first, count heads, call from outside, stay out. Two small differences:
- NFPA allows "by an open window or door"; CPSC says "move outside". For the script, use "outside" (the stricter one).
- CPSC puts the head count after the 911 call; NFPA puts it before. Either way works: "get out, count heads, call".

**Conflict to leave out:** an older U.S. Fire Administration and NAHB factsheet (seen only via search, in a Scribd copy) says that if no one feels ill, "Silence the alarm", turn off appliances, open doors and windows and call a professional. CPSC and NFPA today say to get out first in every case. Don't use the old "silence it and air out the house" steps.

**The chirp is not the alarm:** a single chirp is a low battery or end of life (section 2); the full alarm is a loud repeating pattern. NFPA: if the trouble signal continues after a new battery, "call the fire department".

## 4. Carbon monoxide deaths a year (US)

**Say:** "Every year, more than 400 Americans die from carbon monoxide poisoning that has nothing to do with a fire, more than 100,000 end up in the emergency room, and more than 14,000 are hospitalized." **Can cite** (CDC).

**Sources**
- CDC, "Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning Basics", https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html: "Each year, more than 400 Americans die from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires, more than 100,000 visit an emergency department, and more than 14,000 are hospitalized." (page last reviewed 2026-10-26; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- NFPA (as section 1): "A 2023 study by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control found unintentional non–fire-related CO poisoning between 2005 and 2018 to be annually responsible for approximately 101,847 emergency department (ED) visits (48.3 visits per 1 million), 14,365 hospitalizations (4.1 cases per 1 million), and at least 430 deaths." Also: "In 2023, the National Fire Incident Reporting System data included 82,245 CO incidents which required a local fire department response." (2025; fetched)
- CPSC Q&A: "On average, about 170 people in the United States die every year from CO produced by non-automotive consumer products." CPSC Information Center: more than 200 a year from non-fire CO linked to consumer products, more than 100 of them tied to portable generators (tool summary; links to CPSC's 2022 annual estimates report). CPSC 2026 release: about 750 deaths from generator CO, 2012-2022 (tool summary). (fetched (tool summary))
- EPA, "Preventing Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Information for Older Adults", https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-08/documents/pcmp_english_100-f-09-001.pdf: "about 500 deaths and 15,000 visits to emergency rooms annually. Older adults over 65 years of age are especially vulnerable" (2009-2013 document: **old**, don't use the number)

**Agreement:** agree. CPSC's smaller numbers (170, 200+) count only consumer products and leave out cars, so they aren't a conflict. Use CDC's "more than 400".

## 5. Carbon monoxide symptoms

**Say:** "It feels like the flu: headache, dizziness, weakness, an upset stomach, throwing up, confusion. But no fever. Here's how you tell it's not the flu: more than one person in the house gets sick at the same time, the dog or cat seems sick too, you feel better when you leave the house, or it starts when the furnace comes on." **Can cite** (CDC for the list; EPA for the warning signs).

**Sources**
- CDC (as section 4): "The most common symptoms of CO poisoning are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion." Symptoms often described as "flu-like". "Infants, the elderly, and people with chronic heart disease, anemia, or breathing problems" are more likely to get sick. "Everyone is at risk for CO poisoning." (2026; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- CPSC Q&A: "The initial symptoms of low to moderate CO poisoning are similar to the flu (but without the fever)." Headache, fatigue, shortness of breath, nausea, dizziness; at high levels mental confusion, vomiting, loss of muscular coordination, loss of consciousness, death. (fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- EPA CO factsheet (2026): "Flu-like symptoms (without fever)", headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, shortness of breath, confusion. (fetched)
- EPA, older adults CO leaflet (as section 4): "Symptoms could be the result of CO poisoning when: You feel better when you are away from your home. More than one person in the home gets sick at the same time (it usually takes several days for the flu to pass from person to person). Family members who are most affected spend the most time in the home. Symptoms occur or get worse shortly after turning on a fuel-burning device or running a vehicle in an attached garage. Indoor pets also appear ill, exhibiting symptoms such as drowsiness and lethargy (human flu viruses are not transmitted to pets)." (2009-2013 document, still on epa.gov; fetched)
- NFPA (as section 1): "CO poisoning can be confused with flu symptoms, food poisoning and other illnesses." (2025; fetched)

**Agreement:** agree. The "everyone at once, pets, better away from home" warning signs come from the older EPA leaflet (still hosted by EPA). They're good guidance, but the leaflet is old, so say them without "according to the EPA".

## 6. A gas leak: the full steps (plumbing guide row, re-checked)

**Say (unchanged from the plumbing guide):** "Get everyone out now, and if you're outside, move away, upwind. Don't touch light switches, appliances, doorbells, phones or flashlights, don't light anything, and don't start a car. Once you're well away, call 911 and the gas company, and don't assume someone else called. Don't go back in until they say it's safe, and let the gas company turn the gas back on and relight the pilots. Some people can't smell the gas, and the smell can fade." **In words** (utility and industry sources, no federal page).

**Re-checked live today:**
- American Gas Association, "Using Natural Gas Safely", https://www.aga.org/natural-gas/safety/using-natural-gas-safely/: "Leave the building immediately and take others with you. If you are outside when you smell the gas, leave the area immediately." "Avoid causing a spark, which might cause the gas to explode: Do not light a match or smoke. Do not turn appliances or lights on or off. Do not use a flashlight. Do not start a car. Do not use a telephone". "Find a phone away from the area and call 911 or your natural gas utility. You can report leaks anonymously. Always call to report the problem. Do not assume someone else will do it." "Follow directions from utility employees or emergency responders who are on site." (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)
- PG&E, "Gas safety", https://www.pge.com/en/outages-and-safety/safety/gas-safety.html: "Alert everyone nearby and leave the area immediately to an upwind location." "Do not use anything that could be a source of ignition, including cell phones, flashlights, light switches, matches or vehicles, until you are a safe distance away." "Call 9-1-1 for emergency assistance and then call PG&E". On restoring service: "Gas Service Representatives will visit customer homes and businesses to restore service and relight pilot lights." Also signs: "hissing, whistling or roaring sounds", "dead or dying vegetation in an otherwise moist area". (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)
- SoCalGas safety insert, https://www.socalgas.com/documents/billing/submetered_safety.pdf: "IMMEDIATELY EVACUATE THE AREA." "CALL 911 promptly, from a safe location, if there is damage". "CALL SOCALGAS ... from a safe location." "DO NOT turn electrical devices or light switches on or off". "Some persons may not be able to smell the odor because they have a diminished sense of smell, olfactory fatigue ... a temporary loss of smell", and conditions "can cause odor fade". (seen 2026-10-27; fetched)
- National Grid ("doorbells"): not re-read; its page moved. Kept from `studies/plumbing/price-research-2026-10/B-verify.md` (via search there).

**Agreement:** confirmed. Every step in the plumbing row is still on the live pages. Still not found in any source: "open the windows" and "shut it off at the meter yourself". Leave both out.

**HVAC note:** a furnace is a gas appliance, so the same steps apply if a viewer smells gas near the furnace. The Carrier furnace manual (section 17) also says "Never test for gas leaks with an open flame."

## 7. Generators and cars: distance, garages, backfeeding

### 7a. How far from the house

**Say:** "A generator runs outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away. The CDC says more than 20 feet from any window, door or vent." **Can cite** (CPSC 2026 release, CDC).

**Sources**
- CPSC 2026 release (as section 1): "Use portable generators outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust pointed away from any nearby building." Never inside a home, basement, shed or garage. Never refuel a running generator. (2026-01-23; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- CPSC winter sheet NSN-10 (as section 1): "Use portable generators outside only, at least 20 feet from home, and direct exhaust away from home and any other buildings." "Never use a generator inside a home, basement, shed or garage, even with the door(s) open." (rev. 03/2025; fetched)
- CPSC Information Center: "Use generators outside only, at least 20 feet away from homes with exhaust facing away." (fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- CDC (as section 4): "Operate your generator outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents to avoid CO poisoning." "Never use a generator inside your home or garage, even if doors and windows are open." (2026; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- EPA CO factsheet (2026): "Never use a portable fuel-fired generator inside or within 20 feet of a building or structure." (fetched)
- CPSC Safety Alert, "Portable Generator Hazards", Publication 5123/102021, https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5123_SafetyAlert_PortableGenerators_102021.pdf: "Locate the unit outdoors and far from doors, windows, and vents that could allow CO to come indoors and point the exhaust away from any nearby buildings." No number in this 2021 alert. (2021; fetched)

**Agreement:** agree on 20 feet. The wording differs: CPSC says 20 feet "from the home"; CDC says "more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents".

**Flag on the briefs:** the car-in-an-outage brief says to park the car "at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents (CPSC's distance for generators)". That distance is for **generators**. No official source gives a distance for an idling car. Say it in words ("well away from the house, tailpipe pointing away") and don't credit 20 feet to CPSC for a car.

### 7b. Never in a garage, even with the door open

**Say:** "Never run a generator or a car in the garage, not even with the big door open." **Can cite** (CDC, NFPA, CPSC, EPA).

**Sources**
- CDC: "Never run your car or truck inside a garage that is attached to a house, even with the garage door open." "Always open the door to a detached garage to let in fresh air when you run a car or truck inside." (2026; fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- NFPA (as section 1): "If you need to warm a vehicle, remove it from the garage immediately after starting it. Do not run a vehicle or other fueled engine or motor indoors, even if garage doors are open. Make sure the exhaust pipe of a running vehicle is not covered with snow." (2025; fetched)
- EPA CO factsheet: "NEVER RUN A VEHICLE INSIDE A GARAGE! Carbon monoxide can build up to deadly levels, even with the garage door open." (2026; fetched)
- CPSC Q&A: "Even with open doors and windows, these spaces can trap CO and allow it to quickly build to lethal levels." (fetched (tool summary), quoted)
- CPSC 2021 alert: "NEVER use a generator inside homes, garages, crawlspaces, sheds, or similar areas, even when using fans or opening doors and windows for ventilation. Deadly levels of carbon monoxide can quickly build up in these areas and can linger for hours, even after the generator has shut off." (fetched)

**Agreement:** agree.

### 7c. Never backfeed a panel (why: line workers)

**Say:** "Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or the dryer outlet to power the house. Electricians call that backfeeding. The power can run back out onto the utility lines and electrocute a line worker fixing them, or a neighbor on the same transformer. The safe way is a transfer switch or an interlock kit, put in by an electrician." **Can cite** (CPSC).

**Sources**

## Part 1. New section "Emotion, all the way through" (after "The opening")

The operator (2026-10-21): "simpler and stronger on emotions... using strong words and targetting the exact pain points of this audience like reference scripts do. more work on emotion triggering throughout."

The hits run on household pain plus money: the bill, the thing that came back, the damage nobody saw, the bad night, the pride of doing it yourself. Ours named the pain once and then explained. Every item makes the viewer feel something, in plain, strong words.

1. **Put the viewer in it.** Say "you" and open each item on what they see, hear or smell in their own house, not on how the thing works.
   - Did it: "You finish cooking up some bacon on a Saturday morning" (Roger, 453x).
   - Ours: "Inside every tank water heater, a long metal rod hangs down from the top."
2. **Aim at one real pain per item,** the ones this audience lives with:
   - the surprise bill
   - the fix that came back
   - damage you can't see
   - the bad night (water on the floor, no heat, the holiday rate)
   - being sold something you didn't need
   - making it worse
   - the old house wearing out
   - the family's safety
   - the pride of doing it yourself
3. **Strong, plain words.** Physical verbs and pictures over explanations: eating, rotting, packed solid, splitting, soaking, slime, sludge, "from the inside out", "while you sleep". Simple words, short sentences: no clever metaphors (Voice rule stays: at most one picture-phrase per item).
4. **Name the belief, then turn it.** Say what most people think or do, then "but", then the truth.
   - Did it: "most people think they are actually helping their pipes" (Roger, 453x).
   - At least one turn in every item. Ours used "but" once per 1,000 words; Silver Cymbal 20.
5. **Ask the viewer.** A question they can answer about their own house, answered straight away, about every two items.
   - Did it: "Problem solved, right? Not even close." (Roger, 453x).
   - Ours asked none.
6. **Use disgust where the topic has it.** Plumbing's unfair advantage, used once on items about grease, gunk, hair, roots or what grows in a tank.
   - Did it: "black, slimy, musty-smelling ring" (TheCheapFix).
   - Ours barely used it (0.3 disgust words per 1,000, against 2.4 in Roger's hit and 18.8 in TheCheapFix's).
7. **End every item on a win.** A short line of relief, pride or the contrast (the item's one price can sit here): "That's a $5 part doing a $300 visit's job." Ours ended 11 of 15 items on an instruction; TheCheapFix closes most on a payoff.
8. **Keep the title's feeling alive.** Say the title's idea again in new words through the video ("quietly", "costing you"); ours never said "mistake" or "quietly" once after the opening.
9. **Bring the villain back.** At least twice after the opening, as a price or a silence, never a motive: "That's a $150 visit for a ten-minute job." Ours said it once and dropped it.
10. **Let Roger speak.** One short "I" opinion or verdict in most items ("I'd leave that jug on the shelf"), and once per video a line of insistence where it matters ("I need you to hear me on this one"). Ours had no verdict from item 10 to 15.
11. **Finish on relief, not danger.** The last item before the end CTA closes on reassurance or something to do tonight, even when it's a safety item.

**Never, still:** invented facts or events, guaranteed savings, the trade's motives, teases, the mocked hype ("horror movie", "brutal tests"), jokes.

## Part 2. The opening: simpler and stronger (replaces nothing; adds to "The opening")

The first line hits the viewer's own pain in strong, plain words; it doesn't have to be the worst case.

- **Today's opening:** "That toilet pulled up off the floor, its drain packed solid with tree roots, didn't get that way overnight. A camera down the sewer line would've found those roots years earlier for a couple hundred bucks, and once they've taken over the line, you're into the thousands."
- **Under the new rule (example of the shape, not wording to reuse):** "That slow flush you keep ignoring? It can end with a crew digging up your front yard. The expensive stuff in an old house always starts out quiet: a toilet that runs at night, a drip under the sink nobody sees, a rubber hose behind the washer that's older than you think. Fifteen of them, and almost every one takes a flashlight and five minutes to catch."
- One number at most in the opening; the prices belong to the items.

## Part 3. The item shape for lists (adds to "The video types")

The hits give every item the same order, so viewers can follow and retell it (the top comment on Roger's 453x hit, 1.5K likes, is a viewer listing all five):

1. the scene in the viewer's house
2. what's really happening in the pipe (one or two lines)
3. what it costs, once
4. the turn ("So what do you do instead?")
5. the fix, with its steps cut to what the viewer needs
6. the win

**Length per item:** about 100-130 words (45 seconds to a minute) in a list of 10 or more; longer items only in short lists. Ours ran about 187 words each, mostly extra steps.

**Bridges:** never more than two items in a row without a forward line or a price line. Ours went from item 7 to 12 without one.

## Part 4. The CTA template (Roger and Mark; replaces the mid-video line in "CTAs" and the profiles' "Style and length")

HANDOFF (locked, 2026-10-20): Roger's and Mark's CTAs "go back to the old, much shorter structure their hits used". The pack shortened that to "20-60 words after the first real payoff", and the structure was lost. Roger's hits used the same shape in three videos (7:14 in the 364x hit, about 37% of the way in). Only the order transfers: its opening words ("if you're finding this…") are a banned stock line.

**Where:** after the second or third item (about a third of the way in), at the end of an item's win, never in the middle of a forward line.

**The shape, in about 60-80 words:**
1. an aside signal ("By the way", "Quick thing", rotated)
2. tied to the help just given ("if that one just saved you a service call")
3. "this is a small part of it": the book has the rest, with the pages when the topic's in the book
4. one benefit
5. where to get it: the link in the description and the QR code
6. a return line into the next item

**Example of the shape (not wording to reuse):** "Quick thing before number four. If that toilet check just saved you a service call, that's one of a hundred fixes in my book, One Hundred Old-House Fixes That Save You Thousands. The sewer warning signs from number one are on pages 56 and 57. It's at the link in the description, or use the QR code on your screen. Now, the hose on your outside faucet."

The end CTA stays a one-line reminder, then the comment line (P5).

## Part 5. The six fixes from the judge's report (already put to the operator)

1. **Number cap:** at most one dollar figure per item; in a list, figures on no more than about half the items (the rest say the cost in words); the opening doesn't repeat item one's prices. The brief decides what things cost; the rule decides how many numbers are said.
2. **Straight quotes:** the script straightens every quote and apostrophe before the final check, and the audit's scanner line comes from a run on the delivered text, or says "checked by hand".
3. **Copy check:** `plumbing-scripts/SKILL.md` joins the copy-guard files, so the script can't lift the pack's own examples.
4. **Gas leak:** any script that mentions a gas leak uses every step in the price guide's section 9.
5. **Trade terms:** a short list explained in plain words the first time: psi, trap, P-trap, jetting, relining, anode rod, cleanout, PRV, angle stop, sillcock.
6. **"Gets backwards" promises** name the belief they flip, in one line.

Fix #1: Find and Test Your Main Shut-Off Valve
If I could only teach you one thing in this entire book, it would be
this. When a pipe bursts—and eventually, one will—you don’t have

## Page 13 (printed page 13)

time to Google “how to turn off water.” You don’t have time to call
your spouse. You don’t have time to panic. You have seconds.
Maybe a minute. And in that minute, hundreds of gallons of water
can flood your home, destroying drywall, hardwood floors,
furniture, and electronics.
Your main shut-off valve is usually located where the water line
enters your house. If you have a basement, check the front
foundation wall, usually near the water meter. If you’re on a slab
foundation, check near the water heater or in the garage. If you live
in a warm climate like Florida or Arizona, it might be outside in a
covered box near the street.
Once you find it, you need to know what type you have. There are
two common types:
Gate Valve: This has a round wheel handle, like a ship’s wheel. You
turn it clockwise to close. The problem? Gate valves are notorious
for seizing up after years of disuse. The internal gate corrodes and
gets stuck. If you try to force it, the stem can snap off, and now you
have no way to shut off your water at all.
Ball Valve: This has a straight lever handle. When the lever is
parallel to the pipe, the water is on. When it’s perpendicular
(forming a “T” with the pipe), the water is off. Ball valves are vastly
superior—they operate with a quarter turn and almost never seize.

Fix #2: Build Your $50 Emergency Toolkit
You don’t need a $500 tool chest to fix 90% of plumbing problems.
Contractors want you to think you need specialized, expensive
gear. You don’t. Here is everything you need, and you can get it all
for under $50 at any hardware store:
1. Two pairs of Channellock pliers (slip-joint pliers): You need

fitting with the other. This is the single most important
plumbing tool you will own. Get the 10-inch size.
2. A multi-bit screwdriver: For removing faucet handles, access
panels, and cover plates. Get one with Phillips, flathead, and
hex bits.
3. An adjustable wrench (Crescent wrench): For hex nuts on
supply lines and compression fittings. The 8-inch size handles
most residential work.
4. A basin wrench: This odd-looking tool is specifically designed
to reach up behind sinks where normal tools physically cannot
fit. You’ll need it exactly once—when replacing a faucet—and
you’ll be incredibly grateful you have it.
5. Teflon tape (pink, high-density) and pipe dope: The dynamic
duo of leak prevention. Buy a roll of pink tape and a small tube
of Teflon-based pipe joint compound. Together, they cost
about $8 and will last you years.
6. A bucket and old towels: Because plumbing is wet. Always
have a bucket under whatever you’re working on. Always.

Fix #3: Identify Your Pipes
Before you buy a single replacement part, you need to know what
kind of pipes you’re working with. Different materials require
different tools, different fittings, and different techniques. Here is
your quick identification guide:
Copper: The gold standard of residential plumbing. It’s rigid,
metallic, and has a distinctive reddish-brown color (or green if it’s
old and oxidized). Copper lasts 50–70 years and is joined by
soldering (also called “sweating”) or by using push-to-connect
fittings. If your home was built between 1960 and 2000, you
probably have copper supply lines.
PEX (Cross-Linked Polyethylene): The modern miracle of
plumbing. PEX is flexible plastic tubing, usually color-coded red for
hot water and blue for cold water (white PEX is used for either). It’s
incredibly easy to work with—you can bend it around corners
without fittings, and it connects using crimp rings, expansion
fittings, or push-to-connect fittings. If your home was built or
repiped after 2005, you likely have PEX.
PVC and CPVC: Rigid white (PVC) or cream-colored (CPVC) plastic
pipes. PVC is used exclusively for drain lines and cold water. CPVC
can handle hot water and is sometimes used for supply lines. Both
are joined using a messy, smelly purple primer and solvent cement
(glue). Once glued, the joint is permanent.
Galvanized Steel: Found in homes built before 1980. It’s silver or
gray metal that threads together at every joint. Here is the brutal
truth about galvanized steel: it rusts from the inside out. The pipes
look fine on the outside, but inside, they are slowly closing up with
rust and scale, reducing your water pressure to a trickle. If you have

## Page 19 (printed page 19)

galvanized supply lines, start saving for a whole-house repipe to
PEX or copper. It’s not a matter of if they’ll fail—it’s when.
Cast Iron: Heavy, dark gray or black pipes used for drain lines in
older homes. Cast iron is incredibly durable but eventually rusts
through at the joints. If you see orange stains or flaking on your
cast iron drain pipes, they are nearing the end of their life.
CALL A PRO

If you discover lead pipes (dull gray, soft enough to scratch with a
key) or polybutylene pipes (gray, flexible plastic with copper crimp
rings, common in homes built 1978–1995), call a licensed plumber
immediately. Lead pipes are a serious health hazard. Polybutylene
(often called “Poly-B” or “PB”) is notorious for sudden, catastrophic
failures and should be replaced.

Fix #4: The “Two-Try” Rule
Here is a secret that will save your sanity and your wallet: if you try
to fix a leak twice and it still leaks, stop. Walk away. Have a cup of
coffee. Come back in 30 minutes with fresh eyes.
Plumbing requires a delicate touch. Overtightening a plastic fitting
will crack it. Cross-threading a metal fitting will strip the threads
and ruin it permanently. Forcing a stuck valve will snap the stem. If
you are frustrated, angry, or rushing, you will break something.
And when you break something in plumbing, you turn a $10 repair
into a $500 emergency call at 10 PM on a Saturday night.
The Two-Try Rule is simple: attempt the fix. If it doesn’t work,
disassemble everything, inspect each component, and try again
with fresh tape, fresh dope, and a calm mind. If it still doesn’t work
after the second attempt, the problem is not what you think it is.
Step back, reassess, or call a professional. There is no shame in
knowing your limits.
MARK’S PRO TIP

The number one cause of DIY plumbing disasters is overtightening.
Plastic fittings (like the ones under your sink) only need to be
hand-tight plus a quarter turn with pliers. That’s it. If you’re
cranking on it with all your strength, you are about to crack
something.

Fix #5: Know When to Call a Pro
I want you to DIY as much as possible. That’s the whole point of
this book. But I also want you to stay alive and keep your house

intact. There are certain jobs that are simply too dangerous, too
complex, or too legally restricted for a homeowner to tackle. Here

Always call a licensed professional for:
• Gas lines: Never, ever mess with natural gas or propane. A gas
leak is invisible, odorless (the smell is added artificially), and

the claim.

house belongs to the water company (up to the meter) or to you
(from the meter to the house). Either way, it’s buried
underground and requires specialized equipment to repair.

• The repair involves soldering (sweating) copper pipes near
wood framing. A torch in a wall cavity can start a fire that
smolders for hours before igniting.
• You need to cut into a wall or floor and are not sure what’s

Most modern faucets don’t use rubber washers anymore. They use
a plastic or brass cartridge inside the handle that controls water
flow and temperature. When the faucet drips, the cartridge’s
internal seals have worn out. The fix is simple: pull the old
cartridge out and drop a new one in.
1. Turn off the water at the shut-off valves under the sink. Open
the faucet to release any remaining pressure.
2. Remove the handle. Look for a small Allen set-screw hidden
under a decorative cap on the front or side of the handle. Pop
the cap off with a flathead screwdriver, then loosen the set-screw with an Allen wrench. The handle will pull straight off.
3. Remove the retaining nut (or clip) holding the cartridge in
place. This is usually a large brass or plastic nut that unscrews
with Channellock pliers.
4. Pull the old cartridge straight up and out. It may require some
wiggling. If it’s really stuck, you can buy a cartridge puller tool
for $15, but usually a firm, steady pull does the trick.
5. Take the old cartridge to the hardware store. Match it exactly.
Cartridges are brand-specific—a Moen cartridge won’t fit a
Delta faucet. The replacement will cost $15–$25.
6. Drop the new cartridge in, reassemble in reverse order, and
turn the water back on. The drip is gone.

Fix #7: Cure Low Water Pressure at One Sink
If the water pressure is fine at every other fixture in the house but
terrible at one particular sink, the problem is almost certainly a
clogged aerator. The aerator is the small screen at the very tip of
the faucet spout. Its job is to mix air into the water stream to create
a smooth, splash-free flow. Over time, grit, calcium, and mineral
deposits clog the tiny holes in the screen, strangling the flow to a
trickle.

unscrew by hand (counter-clockwise). If it’s stuck, wrap a rag
around it to protect the finish and use pliers.
2. Inspect the screen. You will likely find it packed with white,
chalky mineral deposits and tiny bits of grit.
3. Soak the aerator in white vinegar for 30 minutes. The acid in
the vinegar dissolves calcium and lime. Scrub it with an old
toothbrush to remove any remaining debris.
4. Screw it back on. Instant full pressure.
If the aerator is completely destroyed, buy a new one for $3–$5.
Bring the old one to the store to match the thread size.

Fix #11: The Phantom Flusher (Replacing the
Flapper)
If you hear your toilet randomly refilling itself in the middle of the
night—a ghostly whoosh of water every 20 minutes or so—you
have what plumbers call a “phantom flush.” Water is slowly leaking
from the tank into the bowl through a worn-out flapper. The fill
valve detects the dropping water level and kicks on to refill the
tank. This cycle repeats endlessly, wasting hundreds of gallons of
water per month and adding $50–$100 to your annual water bill.
The flapper is a round rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that
lifts when you push the flush lever and drops back down to seal the

## Page 34 (printed page 34)

tank after flushing. Over time, the rubber warps, cracks, or
develops mineral buildup, preventing it from sealing completely.
1. Turn off the water at the shut-off valve behind the toilet (turn
the oval handle clockwise).
2. Flush the toilet to empty the tank.
3. Unhook the flapper chain from the flush lever arm (the metal
rod connected to the handle).
4. Pull the old flapper off the two pegs on either side of the flush
valve tower at the bottom of the tank. It usually just snaps off.
5. Snap the new $5 flapper onto the same pegs and reattach the
chain to the flush lever. Leave about half an inch of slack in the
chain—too tight and the flapper won’t close fully; too loose
and it won’t lift high enough to flush.
6. Turn the water back on and let the tank fill. Listen. Silence.
The phantom is gone.
MARK’S PRO TIP

Here’s a trick to confirm it’s the flapper before you buy one: put 5
drops of food coloring in the tank water. Wait 15 minutes without
flushing. If the colored water appears in the bowl, the flapper is
leaking. This test costs you nothing and takes 15 minutes.

Fix #12: The Toilet That Won’t Stop Running (Fill
Valve)
If the water runs constantly and you can see it spilling over the top
of the overflow tube in the center of the tank, your fill valve is

failing to shut off. The internal diaphragm or seal is worn out, and
the valve can no longer stop the flow of water when the tank is full.

on the left side of the tank), there is a small plastic adjustment
screw or a squeeze clip on the float rod. Turning the screw
clockwise (or sliding the clip down) lowers the float, which lowers
the water level. The water level should be about 1 inch below the
top of the overflow tube. If adjusting the float stops the running,
you’re done.

replace the entire fill valve. Buy a universal fill valve kit (like the

1. Turn off the water and flush the tank. Sponge out any
remaining water.
2. Disconnect the water supply line from the bottom of the tank

3. Unscrew the large plastic lock nut on the outside bottom of the

4. Pull the old fill valve straight up and out.
5. Adjust the height of the new fill valve to match your tank (it’s

6. Drop it in, tighten the lock nut from underneath (hand-tight

refill tube to the overflow pipe.
7. Turn the water on and adjust the float so the water level sits 1
inch below the overflow tube.

Fix #13: The Weak Flush
If your toilet flushes, but it lacks power—the water swirls lazily and
takes forever to go down—the problem usually isn’t a clog. The
problem is clogged rim jets.
Under the rim of the toilet bowl are dozens of small angled holes
where water enters the bowl during a flush. These holes create the
swirling vortex that powers the flush. Over time, calcium and hard
water minerals plug these holes, reducing the flow to a weak
dribble.
1. Turn off the water and flush the tank to empty it.
2. Pour a gallon of white vinegar down the overflow tube in the
center of the tank. This sends the vinegar directly into the rim
channel where the jets are.
3. Let it sit for 2–4 hours (or overnight for severe buildup). The
vinegar dissolves the calcium deposits.
4. Take a piece of stiff wire (like a straightened coat hanger) and
carefully poke it into every single hole under the rim to break
up any remaining deposits.
5. Turn the water back on and flush. The difference will be
dramatic—it will flush with authority.

Fix #14: The Rocking Toilet
If your toilet shifts, rocks, or wobbles when you sit down, you have
a ticking time bomb. Every time the toilet rocks, it flexes the wax
ring seal between the toilet base and the drain flange in the floor.
Eventually, that wax ring cracks, and raw sewage begins seeping
out underneath the toilet, silently rotting your subfloor. By the
time you notice the damage, you could be looking at thousands of
dollars in floor repairs.
Do not just caulk around the base of the toilet. I see this “fix”
constantly, and it makes me furious. Caulking hides the leak—it
doesn’t stop it. The water and sewage get trapped under the toilet
and rot the floor even faster because it can’t evaporate.
1. Check the two closet bolts at the base of the toilet (one on each
side, usually hidden under plastic caps). If they are loose,
tighten them gently, alternating sides (a quarter turn on the
left, then a quarter turn on the right). Warning: Do not
overtighten. Porcelain is brittle. If you crank too hard, you will
crack the base of the toilet, turning a free fix into a $300 toilet
replacement.
2. If tightening the bolts doesn’t stop the rocking, the floor
underneath is uneven. You need to pull the toilet, replace the
wax ring ($5), and use plastic toilet shims to level the bowl on
the uneven floor before bolting it back down. Once it’s stable,
then you can caulk around the base for aesthetics.

Fix #16: The Right Way to Plunge a Sink
Most people plunge incorrectly. They push down hard and pull up
fast, thinking the downward force is what clears the clog. That’s
wrong. A plunger works on suction, not pressure. The upward pull
is what dislodges the blockage by creating a vacuum that yanks the
clog loose.
1. If you have a double-bowl kitchen sink, you must plug the
other drain with a wet rag or a rubber stopper. If you don’t, the
pressure will just shoot out the other side and you’ll
accomplish nothing.
2. Fill the clogged sink with 2–3 inches of water. The water
creates a seal around the plunger cup, which is essential for
generating suction.
3. Place the plunger over the drain and push down gently to
force the air out of the bell. You want the cup completely full of
water, not air.
4. Now pull up sharply. This is the power stroke. Repeat 10–15
times with sharp, forceful upward pulls.
5. Remove the plunger. If the water drains, you’ve cleared it. Run
hot water for 2 minutes to flush any remaining debris.

(with the extra rubber lip) is designed for toilets—the flange fits
into the toilet drain opening. Using the wrong plunger is like using
a Phillips screwdriver on a flathead screw. It technically works, but
poorly.

Fix #17: Clearing the P-Trap (The 5-Minute Fix)
If plunging doesn’t clear the clog, the blockage is almost certainly
sitting in the P-trap—the curved pipe directly under the sink that
looks like the letter “P” lying on its side. The P-trap holds a small
amount of water at all times to block sewer gases from coming up

## Page 44 (printed page 44)

through the drain. It also catches debris, hair, grease, and anything
else that goes down the sink.
1. Place a bucket directly under the P-trap. This is non-negotiable. When you remove the trap, everything inside it—
water, sludge, and whatever is causing the clog—will fall out.
2. Loosen the two slip nuts on either end of the curved pipe
section. These are the large plastic or chrome nuts that
connect the trap to the tailpiece (the pipe coming down from
the sink) and the wall pipe. You can usually loosen them by
hand. If they’re stuck, use Channellock pliers.
3. Pull the curved trap section straight down. Water and
disgusting black sludge will fall into the bucket. This is
normal.
4. Clean the trap thoroughly. Take it outside or to another sink
and use a bottle brush, a wire hanger, or a strong stream of
water to flush out all the gunk.
5. Inspect the slip joint washers (the beveled rubber or plastic
rings inside the nuts). If they are cracked, crushed, or hard,
replace them. They cost 50 cents each.
6. Reassemble the trap. Hand-tighten the slip nuts, then give
each one a quarter-turn with pliers. Do not overtighten plastic
slip nuts—they will crack.

Fix #21: Identifying an Illegal S-Trap
Look under your sink right now. Follow the pipe from the drain
down into the curved trap. Does the pipe go down, curve up, and
then immediately curve straight back down through the floor? If
so, that is an S-trap. The shape of the pipe literally looks like the
letter “S.”
A proper P-trap, by contrast, goes down, curves up, and then exits
horizontally through the wall. The horizontal exit is the critical
difference.
Why is the S-trap illegal? Because of physics. When a large volume
of water rushes through that “S” shape (like when you drain a full
sink), it creates a siphon—the same principle that makes a garden
hose siphon water out of a pool. The rushing water literally sucks
all the standing water out of the trap. Without water sitting in the

## Page 51 (printed page 51)

trap, there is absolutely nothing stopping toxic, flammable sewer
gases from floating straight up the pipe and into your kitchen or
bathroom.
Sewer gas contains methane (explosive), hydrogen sulfide (toxic,
smells like rotten eggs), and other harmful compounds. In high
concentrations, it can cause headaches, nausea, and in extreme
cases, unconsciousness. If you smell rotten eggs near a sink with
an S-trap, the trap has been siphoned dry.

Fix #22: The Quick Fix for an Evaporated Trap
Even a proper P-trap can lose its water seal if the fixture isn’t used
for a long time. Guest bathrooms, basement floor drains, and
utility sinks that sit unused for weeks or months will eventually
have their trap water evaporate, allowing sewer gas to enter the
home.
The fix is absurdly simple: run the water for 30 seconds. That’s it.
This refills the trap and restores the water seal.
For floor drains that you rarely use, pour a bucket of water down
them once a month. To make the water last even longer between
visits, pour a tablespoon of mineral oil down the drain after the
water. The oil floats on top of the water in the trap and dramatically
slows evaporation. This trick alone can keep a trap sealed for 6
months or more.

Fix #23: Converting an S-Trap to a P-Trap
The permanent fix for an S-trap is to convert it to a proper P-trap.
A P-trap goes down, curves up, and then exits horizontally into the

## Page 52 (printed page 52)

wall. This horizontal run breaks the siphon effect because air
enters the pipe at the wall connection, preventing the vacuum that
sucks the water out.
If your drain pipe currently goes through the floor, you have two
options:
Option 1: Reroute through the wall. If there is a wall directly
behind the sink, you can cut into the wall, install a sanitary tee on
the vertical drain pipe inside the wall, and run a horizontal pipe
from the P-trap into the tee. This is the gold-standard fix but
requires opening the wall and some basic drain pipe work.
Option 2: Add a vent (see Fix #24). If rerouting through the wall
isn’t practical, you can keep the floor connection but add an Air
Admittance Valve to prevent the siphon.

Fix #24: Installing an Air Admittance Valve
(AAV)
If you are converting an S-trap but don’t have a vent pipe in the
wall (or can’t easily add one), you must install an AAV, often called a
Studor vent after the most popular brand.
An AAV is a one-way mechanical valve. When water drains, it
opens to let air into the pipe (preventing the siphon effect). When
the water stops flowing, gravity and an internal spring snap it shut,
keeping sewer gas from escaping into your home. It’s an elegant,
simple solution.

## Page 53 (printed page 53)

To install one:
1. Install a P-trap under the sink (the curved section that holds
water).
2. After the P-trap, install a vertical pipe extension (a short piece
of PVC going straight up).
3. Thread or glue the AAV onto the top of this vertical extension.
The AAV must be installed above the horizontal drain line and
as high up under the cabinet as possible—at least 4 inches
above the trap weir (the highest point of water in the trap).
4. The drain pipe then continues from the P-trap down through
the floor to the main drain.

Fix #25: Fixing a Gurgling Drain
If your sink gurgles like a percolating coffee pot when you drain
the bathtub or flush a toilet, your plumbing system is gasping for
air. The vent pipe—the PVC pipe that goes from your drain system
up through the roof—is likely clogged.

## Page 54 (printed page 54)

Vent pipes allow air to enter the drain system so water can flow
freely (the same principle as the air hole in a gas can). When the
vent is blocked, draining water creates a vacuum that sucks air
through the nearest trap, causing the gurgling sound. If the
vacuum is strong enough, it can siphon the water right out of the
trap, just like an S-trap.
Common vent blockages include:
• Leaves and debris that fell into the open pipe on the roof
• Bird or wasp nests built inside the pipe
• Snow and ice blocking the opening in winter
• A tennis ball or toy that a child threw onto the roof (I’ve seen
this more than once)
If you are comfortable on a ladder and your roof is safely accessible,
go up and shine a flashlight down the PVC pipe sticking out of the
shingles. Clear any visible debris. If the blockage is deeper, you can
run a plumber’s snake down the vent pipe from the roof to push it
through.

Fix #26: The Emergency Pipe Patch
It’s 2 AM on a Saturday. You hear dripping in the basement. You go

coupling. But they will stop the water and prevent further damage

Fix #27: The Right Way to Use Teflon Tape
Teflon tape (technically called PTFE thread seal tape) is the most
commonly used—and most commonly misused—product in
plumbing. I see homeowners wrap it like they are mummifying a
pharaoh, piling on 10 or 15 layers. More is not better. Too much
tape actually prevents the fitting from threading on properly,
which causes leaks.
Here is the correct technique:
1. Hold the pipe in your left hand, with the threaded end
pointing to the right (toward you).
2. Apply the tape clockwise—the same direction the fitting will
screw on. This is critical. If you wrap it counter-clockwise
(backward), the tape will bunch up and unravel when you
screw the fitting on, defeating the entire purpose.
3. Wrap it exactly 3 times. Three layers. No more, no less. Pull
the tape tight as you wrap so it sinks into the threads and
conforms to their shape.
4. Tear or cut the tape and press the end flat against the threads
with your thumb.

## Page 61 (printed page 61)

5. Screw the fitting on by hand until it’s snug, then tighten with
a wrench. One to two full turns past hand-tight is usually
sufficient.

Fix #28: White vs. Yellow vs. Pink Tape
Stop using the cheap white tape for everything. There are three
colors of Teflon tape, and they exist for very specific reasons:
White Tape (Standard Density): The thin, cheap stuff you find in
the bargain bin. It’s fine for basic water connections on small-diameter pipes (3/8 inch and under), like supply lines to a faucet.
But it tears easily, stretches thin, and provides a mediocre seal on
larger fittings.

## Page 62 (printed page 62)

Pink Tape (High Density): This is what the professionals use. It’s
thicker, stronger, and provides a significantly better seal than
white tape. It costs about $1 more per roll. Use pink tape for all
water line connections. Once you try it, you’ll never go back to
white.
Yellow Tape (Gas-Rated): This tape is specifically formulated for
gas lines. It is denser and thicker than white tape and is rated to
seal against natural gas and propane. Never use white or pink tape
on a gas line. The gas will permeate through standard tape. Always
use yellow tape (and pipe dope) on any gas connection.
CALL A PRO

While I’ve explained the difference in tape colors for your
knowledge, I want to be crystal clear: do not work on gas lines
yourself. Gas work requires a licensed plumber, a pressure test, and
in most jurisdictions, a permit. A gas leak can be fatal. Leave it to
the pros.

Fix #29: The Magic of Pipe Dope (Belt and
Suspenders)
Here is something most homeowners don’t know: Teflon tape does
not actually seal the threads. It lubricates them. The tape reduces
friction, allowing you to tighten the fitting further, which is what
creates the seal (metal-to-metal contact in the tapered threads).
The tape fills minor imperfections in the threads, but it is not a
sealant by itself.

## Page 63 (printed page 63)

For a truly bulletproof, never-going-to-leak joint, use the “belt and
suspenders” method:
1. Wrap the threads with 3 layers of pink Teflon tape (clockwise).
2. Then brush a thin, even layer of Teflon-based pipe joint
compound (pipe dope) over the tape.
3. Screw the fitting together and tighten.
The tape lubricates and fills the threads. The dope provides an
additional chemical seal that hardens slightly over time. Together,
they create a joint that will not leak. Period. This is how
professional plumbers assemble every threaded connection, and
it’s how you should too.
A tube of pipe dope costs $5 and lasts for years. It is the single best
investment in leak prevention you can make.

Fix #30: Tightening Slip Joints Under the Sink
If the plastic drain pipes under your sink are dripping at the
connections (the large nuts), do not reach for the glue or the Teflon
tape. Those are slip joints—they are specifically designed to be
hand-tightened and taken apart for cleaning. Teflon tape does not
belong on slip joint connections.
If tightening the nut by hand doesn’t stop the drip, the problem is
the internal washer:
1. Unscrew the leaking slip nut completely.
2. Inside, you will find a beveled (angled) rubber or plastic
washer. Over time, these washers get crushed flat, cracked, or
brittle from age and cleaning chemicals.

## Page 64 (printed page 64)

3. Buy a new slip joint washer at the hardware store. They cost
50 cents and come in standard sizes (1-1/4 inch for bathroom
sinks, 1-1/2 inch for kitchen sinks).
4. Install the new washer with the beveled (angled) edge
pointing toward the fitting you are pushing it into. This is
critical—if the bevel faces the wrong way, it won’t seal.
5. Hand-tighten the nut, then give it a quarter-turn with pliers.

Fix #31: When to Use Push-to-Connect Fittings
SharkBites are genuinely incredible in specific situations. Here is

Fix #32: The Hidden Danger of SharkBites
Here is the brutal truth, and this is my professional opinion after 35
years: I do not trust a rubber O-ring inside a wall for 30 years.
SharkBites are approved by code to be installed behind drywall, in
walls, in ceilings—anywhere. And technically, they are rated for the
life of the plumbing system. But the seal depends entirely on a
small rubber O-ring. Rubber degrades over time. It dries out. It
shrinks. It hardens. Water chemistry, chlorine, and temperature
fluctuations all accelerate this process.

## Page 67 (printed page 67)

A soldered copper joint is metal fused to metal. It will last 70 years.
A crimped PEX connection uses a permanent metal ring
compressed onto the pipe. It will last 50 years. A SharkBite depends
on a rubber ring that might last 25 years. Maybe.
My rule: Use SharkBites in exposed, accessible locations
(basements, crawlspaces, under sinks, in utility rooms) where you
can see them and catch a failure before it causes damage. If you are
closing up a wall, learn to solder copper or use crimped PEX
connections. The extra 30 minutes of work is worth decades of
peace of mind.

Fix #33: The Perfect SharkBite Installation
Ninety percent of SharkBite failures are due to user error, not
product defects. The fitting is only as good as the preparation. Here
is how to install one correctly:
1. Cut the pipe perfectly square. Use a pipe cutter (for copper) or
a PEX cutter (for PEX). A jagged, angled cut from a hacksaw
will not seat properly in the fitting and may slice the internal
O-ring.
2. Deburr the pipe. After cutting, use a deburring tool (or even
sandpaper) to smooth the outside and inside edges of the cut.
A sharp burr on the pipe will tear the O-ring as it slides past,
causing a slow leak that may not appear for weeks.
3. Mark the insertion depth. Each SharkBite fitting has a specific
insertion depth (usually about 1 inch, but check the chart on
the packaging). Use a permanent marker to draw a line on the
pipe at the correct depth from the end.
4. Push the pipe into the fitting until your mark is flush with the
edge of the fitting. If you don’t push it all the way in, the
stainless steel teeth won’t fully engage, and the fitting can
blow off under pressure. Push firmly and steadily until you feel
it “click.”

and check for moisture after 5 minutes.

Fix #34: Removing a Push-to-Connect Fitting
Push-to-connect fittings are designed to be permanent, but they
can be removed and reused. You need a disconnect clip—a cheap,
horseshoe-shaped plastic tool that costs about $2.
1. Turn off the water and drain the line.
2. Snap the disconnect clip onto the pipe, right up against the
fitting’s release collar (the metal ring where the pipe enters the
fitting).
3. Push the clip firmly against the release collar. This
compresses the collar inward, retracting the stainless steel
teeth that grip the pipe.
4. While holding the collar in with the clip, pull the pipe straight
out of the fitting.
The fitting can be reused, but inspect the O-ring inside. If it’s
nicked, scored, or deformed, replace the fitting. A damaged O-ring
will leak.

Fix #36: Flushing the Sediment
When water is heated, dissolved minerals (calcium, magnesium,
lime) separate from the water and fall to the bottom of the tank as
sediment. Over time, this sediment builds up into a thick, rock-hard layer that buries the lower heating element (electric) or sits
directly on the burner plate (gas). The heater has to work twice as
hard to heat water through this insulating layer of rock, driving up

## Page 76 (printed page 76)

your energy bills and eventually burning out the element or
warping the burner.
Flushing the tank once a year removes this sediment and keeps
your heater running efficiently:
1. Turn off the power. For electric heaters, turn off the breaker.
For gas heaters, turn the gas valve to “Pilot” (not “Off”—you
don’t want to have to relight the pilot).
2. Turn off the cold water supply valve at the top of the tank.
3. Attach a standard garden hose to the drain valve at the
bottom of the tank. Run the other end of the hose outside, to a
floor drain, or into a large bucket.
4. Open a hot water faucet somewhere in the house (any sink or
tub). This lets air into the system so the tank can drain.
5. Open the drain valve. Let the tank empty completely. The
water will be hot—be careful. You may see chunks of white or
tan sediment coming out with the water. This is normal and
exactly what you want to remove.
6. Once empty, turn the cold water supply on and off in short
bursts (10 seconds on, 10 seconds off) three or four times. This
stirs up any remaining sediment at the bottom and flushes it
out through the hose.
7. Close the drain valve, remove the hose, close the hot water
faucet you opened, and turn the cold water supply fully on. Let
the tank refill completely (you’ll know it’s full when water
flows steadily from the open faucet).
8. Turn the power back on. For gas, turn the valve back to your
desired temperature setting.

Fix #37: Testing the T&P Valve
The Temperature and Pressure (T&P) Relief Valve is the single most
important safety device on your water heater. If the thermostat
fails and the water temperature or pressure rises to dangerous
levels, the T&P valve opens automatically to release water and
prevent the tank from exploding. And I do mean exploding—a
failed water heater with no functioning T&P valve can launch
through a roof like a rocket. This is not an exaggeration; it has
been documented and tested.
Test the T&P valve once a year:
1. Place a bucket under the discharge pipe (the pipe running
from the T&P valve down toward the floor).
2. Lift the metal lever on the T&P valve (located on the side or
top of the tank). Water should shoot out forcefully through the
discharge pipe into the bucket.
3. Release the lever. It should snap shut immediately, and the
water should stop.
If the valve doesn’t release water when you lift the lever, or if it
won’t stop dripping after you release it, replace the valve

## Page 78 (printed page 78)

immediately. A new T&P valve costs $15 and threads into the tank
with a wrench. It is a 10-minute job that could save your life.

Fix #38: The Secret to a 20-Year Tank (The
Anode Rod)
Inside your water heater tank is a long metal rod called the
sacrificial anode rod. It is typically made of magnesium or
aluminum and is threaded into the top of the tank. Its sole purpose
is to attract corrosive elements in the water so they attack the rod
instead of the steel tank lining. The rod literally sacrifices itself to
protect the tank—hence the name.
Once the anode rod is completely consumed (eaten away by
corrosion), the water starts attacking the steel tank itself. Rust
forms on the inside walls, weakens the steel, and eventually eats
through, causing an irreparable leak. At that point, the entire
heater must be replaced.
Check the anode rod every 3 years:
1. Turn off the power and cold water supply.
2. Locate the anode rod. It’s under a hex-head plug on top of the
tank (sometimes hidden under a plastic cap).
3. Use a 1-1/16” socket on a breaker bar (a long-handled ratchet
for extra leverage) to unscrew the rod. It will be tight—you may
need someone to hold the tank steady.
4. Pull the rod out. If it looks like a chewed-up wire—thin, pitted,
and corroded to less than half its original diameter—it’s spent.
Replace it.

## Page 79 (printed page 79)

5. A new anode rod costs $25–$40 and adds 5–7 years to the life
of your tank. Thread it in, tighten it, and you’re done.

If your hot water smells like rotten eggs, the anode rod is reacting
with bacteria in the water. Replace the magnesium rod with an
aluminum/zinc anode rod. The zinc kills the bacteria and
eliminates the smell. This is a $30 fix that solves a problem most
plumbers will charge $200 to “diagnose.”

Fix #39: Adjusting the Temperature
Most water heaters are set to 140°F from the factory. This is too
hot. Water at 140°F can cause a third-degree burn in just 5 seconds.
It also wastes energy and accelerates sediment buildup and tank
corrosion.
The Department of Energy recommends 120°F for most
households. This is hot enough for comfortable showers and
effective dishwashing, while being significantly safer and more
energy-efficient.
For gas heaters: Turn the dial on the gas control valve (the box on
the front of the tank near the bottom) to the setting marked “Hot”
or “A” (which is typically 120°F). Some valves have actual
temperature markings.
For electric heaters:
1. Turn off the breaker to the heater.
2. Remove the two metal access panels on the side of the tank
(upper and lower), secured by screws.
3. Pull back the insulation to reveal the thermostat.
4. Use a flathead screwdriver to turn both the upper and lower
thermostats down to 120°F.
5. Replace the insulation and panels, and turn the breaker back
on.
Lowering the temperature from 140°F to 120°F can save you $36–
$60 per year on your energy bill, depending on your usage and fuel
type.

Fix #40: Silencing the Popping Noise
If your water heater sounds like a popcorn machine when it’s
heating, that is the sound of water trapped under a thick layer of
hardened sediment. The burner or element heats the sediment
layer, and pockets of water underneath boil, creating steam
bubbles that burst through the crust with a loud pop or rumble.
The first step is to flush the tank thoroughly (Fix #36). If the
sediment hasn’t calcified into a solid block, flushing will remove it
and silence the noise.
If flushing doesn’t work (meaning the sediment is too hardite to
flush out), try this: remove the anode rod from the top of the tank
and pour a gallon of white vinegar into the tank through the anode
rod opening. Let it sit for 24 hours. The vinegar will dissolve the
calcium. Then flush the tank again.
If the vinegar treatment doesn’t work either, the sediment has
essentially turned to concrete inside the tank. At this point, the
tank is nearing the end of its useful life. Start budgeting for a
replacement.

Fix #41: Relighting a Gas Pilot
If you have no hot water and a gas water heater, the first thing to
check is the pilot light. Look through the small glass window at the
bottom front of the tank. If you don’t see a small blue flame, the

## Page 85 (printed page 85)

pilot has gone out. This can happen from a strong draft, a brief
interruption in gas supply, or simply age.
1. Turn the gas control knob to “Pilot.”
2. Press and hold the pilot knob down. This manually opens the
gas valve to the pilot burner.
3. While holding the knob down, click the piezo igniter button
(the small button or spark generator next to the gas valve)
repeatedly until you see the pilot flame light through the
window.
4. Keep holding the pilot knob down for a full 60 seconds. This
is critical—you are heating the thermocouple, which is the
safety sensor that tells the gas valve a flame is present. If you
release too early, the thermocouple won’t be hot enough and
the gas valve will shut down.
5. Release the knob. If the pilot stays lit, turn the gas control
knob to your desired temperature setting (“Hot” or 120°F). The
main burner should ignite within a few minutes.
If the pilot won’t light at all, make sure the gas supply valve (on the
gas line leading to the heater) is open. If it’s open and you still can’t
get a flame, the piezo igniter may be faulty—try using a long-reach
barbecue lighter to ignite the pilot manually through the access
opening at the bottom of the tank.

Fix #42: Replacing a Bad Thermocouple
If you follow Fix #41 and the pilot lights but goes out the instant
you release the knob, the thermocouple is dead.

## Page 86 (printed page 86)

The thermocouple is a thin copper probe that sits directly in the
pilot flame. When heated, it generates a tiny electrical current
(millivolts) that signals the gas valve: “There is a flame present, it is
safe to release gas.” If the thermocouple fails, the gas valve receives
no signal and shuts down completely as a safety precaution.
1. Turn off the gas at the gas control valve (turn to “Off”).
2. Locate the thermocouple. It’s the thin copper tube running
from the pilot burner assembly up to the gas control valve.
3. Unscrew the thermocouple from the gas valve (it threads in
with a small nut) and detach it from the pilot assembly
bracket.
4. Take it to the hardware store and buy a universal
thermocouple ($10–$15). They come in standard lengths.
5. Thread the new thermocouple into the gas valve (hand-tight
plus a quarter turn with a wrench—do not overtighten) and
position the probe tip so it sits directly in the path of the pilot
flame.
6. Relight the pilot following Fix #41. Hold the knob for a full 60
seconds. Release. The pilot should stay lit.

Plumber’s charge: $150–$250
Your cost: $15 (universal thermocouple)
Your savings: $135–$235

Fix #43: Resetting an Electric Heater
If you have an electric water heater and suddenly lose all hot water,
the high-limit safety switch (also called the ECO—Energy Cut-Off)
may have tripped. This switch is designed to shut off power to the
heater if the water temperature exceeds a safe limit (usually 170°F),
preventing the tank from overheating.
1. Turn off the breaker to the water heater at your electrical
panel.
2. Remove the upper access panel on the side of the tank
(usually held by two screws).
3. Pull back the insulation to reveal the upper thermostat.
4. Look for a small red button on the thermostat. This is the
reset button.
5. Press it. If it clicks, the switch was tripped. This means the
water got too hot, and the safety shut it down.
6. Replace the insulation and panel, and turn the breaker back
on.
If the reset button trips again within a day or two, you have a
failing thermostat that is not regulating temperature properly.
Replace the thermostat ($20 part, 20-minute job) before it causes a
more serious problem.

Fix #44: Replacing a Heating Element
Electric water heaters have two heating elements—an upper
element and a lower element. They work in sequence: the upper

## Page 88 (printed page 88)

element heats the top of the tank first (so you get hot water
quickly), then the lower element takes over to heat the rest.
If your water is lukewarm but never truly hot, the upper element
is likely burned out. If you get a few minutes of hot water that
quickly turns cold, the lower element is dead (the upper element
heats a small amount at the top, but the rest of the tank is cold).
1. Turn off the breaker to the water heater.
2. Drain the tank below the level of the element you are replacing
(follow the draining steps in Fix #36). You don’t need to drain
the entire tank if you’re only replacing the lower element—just
drain it below that level.
3. Remove the access panel and pull back the insulation.
4. Disconnect the two wires from the element terminals. Take a
photo first so you remember which wire goes where.
5. Use a heating element wrench (a large, inexpensive specialty
socket available at any hardware store for $5) to unscrew the
old element from the tank.
6. Screw in the new element ($15–$25), reconnect the wires,
replace the insulation and panel.
7. Refill the tank completely before turning the breaker back on.
CALL A PRO

CRITICAL WARNING: Never turn the power on while the tank is
empty or partially filled. The heating element will instantly
overheat and burn out (called “dry firing”), destroying your brand-new $20 element in seconds. Always verify the tank is completely
full (water flows steadily from an open hot faucet) before restoring
power.

Fix #45: Repair vs. Replace—The Decision Guide
How do you know when to stop fixing your water heater and just
buy a new one? Here is the decision framework I use after 35 years:
Repair it if:
• The tank is under 8 years old.
• The problem is electrical (heating elements, thermostats,
high-limit switch).
• The problem is a gas component (thermocouple, gas valve,
pilot assembly).
• The leak is coming from a fitting, valve, or pipe connection—
not the tank itself.
Replace it if:
• The tank is over 12 years old (check the serial number on the
label—most manufacturers encode the date in the first four
characters).
• Water is leaking from the actual steel tank body (not from a
valve or pipe). Once the steel rusts through, it cannot be
repaired. The tank is done.
• You are spending more than $300 per year on repairs.
• The tank is undersized for your household (you consistently
run out of hot water).

Fix #46: Winterizing Your Hose Bibs
Every single spring, I make thousands of dollars replacing burst
pipes because homeowners forgot to disconnect their garden

## Page 93 (printed page 93)

hoses before winter. This is the easiest, most preventable plumbing
disaster in existence, and it happens to millions of homes every
year.
Here is what happens: you leave a garden hose attached to the
outdoor faucet (hose bib) over winter. Even if you turn the faucet
off, water remains trapped in the pipe between the shut-off point
and the hose connection. When temperatures drop below freezing,
that trapped water expands as it turns to ice. Ice expands with
enormous force—enough to split a copper pipe like a zipper. The
crack happens silently, inside the wall. You won’t know it happened
until spring, when you turn the hose on and water floods your
basement or wall cavity.
Before the first freeze every year:
1. Disconnect all garden hoses from every outdoor faucet. Drain
the hoses and store them.
2. If you have a separate shut-off valve for the outdoor faucet
(usually in the basement near where the pipe goes through the
wall), turn it off.
3. Open the outdoor faucet to drain any remaining water from
the pipe.
4. If you have frost-free hose bibs (the long-stem type where the
shut-off point is actually inside the wall, in the heated space),
you still need to disconnect the hose. The frost-free design
only works if water can drain out of the pipe after you close the
valve. A connected hose traps the water and defeats the entire
purpose.
This takes 10 seconds per faucet and prevents a $500–$2,000
repair. Do it every fall without exception.

Fix #47: Silencing Banging Pipes (Water
Hammer)
If your pipes bang loudly against the wall or floor joists when the
washing machine stops filling, when you shut off a faucet quickly,
or when the dishwasher valve closes, you have “water hammer.”
This is a hydraulic shockwave caused by the sudden stoppage of
fast-moving water. The momentum of the water has nowhere to
go, so it slams against the pipe walls and fittings like a battering
ram.
Over time, water hammer will loosen pipe straps, break solder
joints, damage fittings, and cause leaks. It needs to be addressed.
The fix is a water hammer arrestor—a small sealed cylinder with
an internal air bladder or piston that acts as a shock absorber.
When the water slams to a stop, the arrestor compresses and
absorbs the energy, eliminating the bang.
For washing machines (the most common source), you can buy
screw-on arrestors that attach directly to the hot and cold washing
machine valves—no pipe cutting required. They cost $15–$25 each
and install in 2 minutes. For other locations, arrestors are available
with push-to-connect or threaded connections that install on the
supply line near the offending fixture.

Fix #48: Upgrading Supply Lines
Look under your sinks and behind your toilets right now. What do
the water supply lines (the flexible tubes connecting the shut-off
valve to the fixture) look like?
If they are made of stiff gray plastic, ribbed white plastic, or plain

virtually indestructible under normal residential water pressure.

5 minutes (unscrew the old one, screw on the new one). Replace
every supply line in your house on a Saturday afternoon for under

Fix #49: The Plumber’s Putty Secret
When installing a new sink drain (the chrome or brushed nickel
pop-up assembly), the instructions will tell you to use the included
rubber gasket under the drain flange inside the sink basin. Here is
my advice: throw that rubber gasket in the trash.
Rubber gaskets in the sink basin almost always leak eventually.
They compress unevenly, they don’t conform perfectly to the sink
surface (especially on textured or porcelain sinks), and they dry out
and shrink over time.
Instead, use plumber’s putty. It looks and feels like gray Play-Doh.
Roll a snake of putty about the thickness of a pencil and wrap it in a
complete ring under the rim of the drain flange. Press the flange

## Page 99 (printed page 99)

into the sink hole and tighten the retaining nut from underneath.
The putty will squeeze out around the edges, creating a perfect,
waterproof, custom-molded seal that conforms exactly to the
shape of your sink. Wipe away the excess putty with your finger.
Plumber’s putty costs $4 for a container that will last you a decade.
It never hardens completely (it stays slightly pliable), which means
it maintains its seal even as the sink expands and contracts with
temperature changes. This is how every professional plumber
installs a sink drain.
MARK’S PRO TIP

Exception: Do NOT use plumber’s putty on marble, granite, or
natural stone sinks. The oils in the putty can stain porous stone
permanently. For stone sinks, use clear silicone caulk instead.

Fix #50: The Final Inspection
This is the most important habit you will develop as a DIY plumber,
and it is the one thing that separates a professional-quality repair
from an amateur one.
Whenever you finish a plumbing repair—any repair, no matter how
small—do not just turn the water on, glance at it for two seconds,
and walk away. That is how slow leaks go undetected for weeks,
silently causing damage behind cabinets and inside walls.
Here is the professional inspection method:
1. Turn the water on slowly. Watch the joint you just worked on
as pressure builds.

## Page 100 (printed page 100)

2. Wipe the joint completely dry with a paper towel or clean rag.
Remove every drop of residual water.
3. Run the fixture for a full 5 minutes. Not 30 seconds. Five
minutes. Some leaks only appear after the fitting has been
under sustained pressure and has thermally expanded.
4. Take a piece of dry toilet paper (or a dry paper towel) and wipe
it slowly around every connection, joint, and fitting you
touched. Drag it along the bottom of pipes where gravity
would pull a drip.
5. Inspect the toilet paper. If it shows even a single drop of
moisture—even a tiny damp spot—the joint is leaking. Fix it
now.
This toilet paper test is more sensitive than your eyes. A
microscopic weep that you would never see visually will show up
immediately as a wet spot on dry paper. It takes 30 seconds and
catches leaks that would otherwise go unnoticed for months.

checks it three times. And then he checks it again in the
morning.”

APPENDIX C

Glossary of Plumbing Terms
Every term you need to know, in plain English

AAV (Air Admittance Valve): A one-way mechanical valve that lets
air into a drain system to prevent siphoning, without allowing
sewer gas to escape. Also called a Studor vent.
Aerator: The small screen assembly at the tip of a faucet spout that

Commonly clogs with mineral deposits.
Anode Rod: A sacrificial metal rod (magnesium or aluminum)
inside a water heater tank that corrodes so the tank doesn’t. Should
be checked every 3 years.
Ball Valve: A shut-off valve with a lever handle that operates with a
quarter turn. Superior to gate valves for reliability.
Cartridge: The internal mechanism inside a modern faucet handle
that controls water flow and temperature. Replaces the old rubber
washer design.
Closet Bolts: The two bolts that secure a toilet to the floor flange.
Also called Johnny bolts or T-bolts.
CPVC: Chlorinated Polyvinyl Chloride. A cream-colored rigid
plastic pipe that can handle hot water, unlike standard PVC.
Flapper: The rubber seal at the bottom of a toilet tank that lifts
during a flush to release water into the bowl, then drops back to
reseal the tank.

## Page 109 (printed page 109)

Gate Valve: A shut-off valve with a round wheel handle. Prone to
seizing after years of disuse. Being replaced by ball valves in
modern plumbing.
P-Trap: The curved pipe under a sink (shaped like the letter P on
its side) that holds water to block sewer gases from entering the
home.
Packing Nut: The hexagonal nut on a valve stem that compresses
rubber packing to prevent water from leaking around the stem.
PEX: Cross-Linked Polyethylene. Flexible plastic tubing used for
water supply lines. Color-coded red (hot), blue (cold), and white
(either).
Pipe Dope: A paste (pipe joint compound) applied to threaded pipe
connections to ensure a leak-proof seal. Used in combination with
Teflon tape.
Plumber’s Putty: A soft, pliable sealing compound used under sink
drain flanges and other fixtures. Creates a waterproof seal that
remains slightly flexible.
PVC: Polyvinyl Chloride. White rigid plastic pipe used for drain
lines and cold water applications. Joined with primer and solvent
cement.
S-Trap: An illegal, outdated drain configuration where the pipe
goes down, up, and straight back down through the floor. Creates a
siphon that removes the water seal, allowing sewer gas into the
home.
SharkBite: A popular brand of push-to-connect plumbing fittings
that require no soldering, glue, or special tools. Uses internal teeth
and an O-ring to grip and seal pipes.

Slip Joint: A drain pipe connection that uses a compression nut and
beveled washer, designed to be hand-tightened and easily
disassembled for cleaning.
T&P Valve: Temperature and Pressure Relief Valve. A critical safety

pressure exceeds safe limits.
Teflon Tape: PTFE thread seal tape wrapped around pipe threads

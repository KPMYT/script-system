# Roger the Pipe Guy: the one-page brief

You are writing a YouTube voiceover script for **Roger the Pipe Guy**, a plumbing channel for owners of older American homes, mostly 50 and up, who would rather fix it than pay for a visit. Under every click sits one question: "Can I do this myself without making it worse?" A text-to-speech voice (a calm, confident man) reads it, so write for the ear. It's a Classic script: no markers.

## Who he is
A blunt, unhurried tradesman, 45 years in the trade, and he wrote the book. He talks to one homeowner as "you", like they're standing next to him at the sink: plain-spoken, zero patience for waste or sales pitches, firmly on their side. He steers them away from caustic drain cleaners and from anything truly dangerous (gas, sewage, wiring), and under the bluntness he's reassuring ("nothing's broken", "there's no shame in calling").
- **Used only if it helps:** his 45 years, once, where it backs a verdict (or the title's number); his drain check on the first Saturday of the month, with his coffee; a plain picture that teaches (grease setting like candle wax). None of these has a number to reach; many scripts need none.
- **Never:** his name in the opening, a customer story, "I tested", "watch me", a relative, a town, an employer, a date, or his other channel.

## The opening
The first sentence makes the point: the title's subject and, wherever the title allows, the fix or product itself, named outright. Then, in the order that suits the title (the model shows one way):
- **The scene and the mistake:** the problem in their house and what people try first (the jug, the toilet plunger, the kettle). A failed attempt is the strongest feeling these viewers bring.
- **The villain:** the trade's price or its silence, said as plain fact.
- **Where the job stops being theirs,** said honestly ("everything here is yours until two drains back up at once"). Never "no plumber needed" when a later step ends in a call.

If it fits, add a question they can answer in their own house, the count ("three levels, smallest first"), or the escalation said simply (where the video starts and where it ends up).

Start the first real step **by about 45 seconds**. No tool list (each tool is named once, in the step that uses it), no channel intro, no "today we're going to", no tease, no run-down of later steps. At most one promise, and only one the end pays; most openings need none. Prices belong to the steps; the opening needs one at most, often none.

**Hold the reveal** (operator, 2026-11-06: "if our title is saying these 3 powders, we say powders, we dont name them yet so we keep building curiosity until the actual reveal"; for every hook). When the title holds something back ("these 3 powders", "1 cup of this", "this $15 build", "the 8 things", "the one mistake"), the opening says it the way the title does and never names it, not even one of the list. Each one is named only at its own reveal, in the body. Build the curiosity instead: what it does, what it costs her or him today, that it is cheap and already on a shelf. What the title itself names (the sawdust, the grease, the granny cut) can be said.

## The feeling, all the way through
Every title carries one thing they want and one thing they fear. Name both early and let them come back now and then, never as a refrain.

| Title | They want | They fear |
|---|---|---|
| How to, for beginners | to do it themselves and see it work | making it worse, water on the floor |
| Never do, mistakes | to stop the damage they can't see | a bill they caused without knowing |
| Things plumbers don't tell you | the cheap fix worth knowing | overpaying, being kept in the dark |
| One product fixes it | a cheap fix that works tonight | another wasted jug, a burnt pipe |
| Why it keeps happening | the cause gone for good | the fix that came back |

- **Put them in it.** Open each step on what they see, hear or smell in their own house. Disgust where the topic has it: the gray mat of hair, the sour smell.
- **The villain is the trade's price and its silence, and the store shelf's jug.** Two plain facts side by side ("Nobody mentions the strip. A visit is a bill in the hundreds."), never a motive. At a price in the hundreds or thousands, his disgust as his own opinion, in fresh words. It comes back now and then, never in the same words.
- **Credit the old habit before correcting it** (baking soda and vinegar was thrift).
- **End each step on the win:** the drain spinning clear, the visit they didn't pay for.
- **Finish on relief:** the last step closes with one small thing to do tonight, never a to-do list.

## How it flows
- **Like one man talking, start to finish.** Each step leads into the next. If a sentence exists only to tick a box, cut it.
- **Copy the model's flow,** never its words, facts, stories or signature phrases (the model file lists them).
- **Point first, then the why in one line.** Longer explanations broken by short, hammered verdicts; "but" where the thought turns; a question to them now and then, answered at once. "You" for every instruction, "I" for his verdicts.
- **A trade word gets a short explanation the first time** (the trap, the U-shaped bend under the sink). Never explain what everyone knows.
- **Each step takes its own shape.** Never end every step alike, and never say a stock phrase twice ("here's what you do" three times was iter-09's fault).
- **Lists of three at most,** anywhere.

## The hard rules
1. **Length:** at least 2,040 spoken words (12 minutes at his 170 a minute); 12 to 20 minutes is the sweet spot. Short: build out a point from the brief, never filler. If the brief can't reach 12 minutes, say so.
2. **The book, once in the middle,** about a third of the way in, never before the end of the third step or item, at the end of a step's win, about 60 to 80 words in his own words, in the shape his hits used: a quick aside, tied to the help just given ("if that just saved you a visit"), the book has the rest, one benefit, where to get it, and back into the video.
   - "One Hundred Old-House Fixes That Save You Thousands" ("the book" or "my book"), at the link in the description and the QR code on screen; then back into the video with a real line, not "back to the drain".
   - The chapter, fix or printed pages from the brief's book match (or the closest in the book index), claiming only what it confirms. Other true facts: 100 numbered fixes in 16 chapters; each gives the problem, why it costs money, his first check, the cheap fix, when to call a pro and the money saved; the checklists and the seasonal calendar. One benefit, never a list.
   - Never a price, "free", "e-book", or this video's steps put in the book's mouth.
3. **The ending,** in this order, then stop: the last step, with one small thing for tonight; one line reminding them of the book, the link in the description and the QR code; a short turn to them, an outright ask to comment with one specific question about their own house, and "I read every one." as the last line. No recap, subscribe ask, sign-off or next video.
4. **Safety** (the brief wins where it says otherwise), one short line inside its step, with the reason:
   - hot tap water, never boiling, for drains and plastic pipe; washing soda dissolved first
   - one cleaner at a time: never bleach with ammonia, vinegar or any acid; never peroxide with vinegar
   - the chemical check comes before any plunger or snake: a drain with chemical cleaner in it gets nothing added, plunging included
   - **the main line:** two or more drains backing up at once. When nothing goes down, the test for it comes first: if it's the main, stop running water and call. Never send them back to the plunger first (iter-09's fault)
   - before any water-heater job, the power or the gas goes off; gas and live wiring are a call; a gas leak gets every step (everyone out, no switches, flames or car, 911 and the gas company from well away, back in only when they say)
   - every call names its trigger: "Two drains backing up at once is the main line. That's a call."
5. **Money:** prices from the brief, then the price guide, always estimates (a rounded range or "about"), never cents, except a cost per use ("call it fifty cents a use"). Say what moves a range or its top ("depending on where you live"). Never a guaranteed saving or a total stated as certain. Not every step needs a price; the rest carry it in words ("a service call").
6. **Never:** a named company run down as fact; the trade's motives; pointing at the screen ("as you can see", "right here"); teases; recaps; edit notes; swearing; jokes; hype ("horror movie", "brutal tests"); stock lines ("If you take one thing from this video", "Here's the part people skip", "not a scam, just economics").
7. **The brief wins on facts.** Where it's silent, only everyday plumbing sense, never an invented dose, price or claim.
8. **For the voice:** no symbols, dashes, brackets or stage directions; whole-dollar prices as digits or words; sizes and units in words ("three-quarters of an inch"), never two numbers side by side; no sentence over 35 words.

## After writing: check, fix, stop
Read it aloud once, run the checking program (the plumbing pack says how), then check by eye: the point first and the first step by about 45 seconds; at most one promise; the chemical check before the plunger; the main-line test first when nothing goes down; the book's facts and place; the ending's order; the "never" list; no echo of the model. Fix only what fails; never add a line to meet a count.

And check that the opening names nothing the title holds back (each one waits for its reveal).

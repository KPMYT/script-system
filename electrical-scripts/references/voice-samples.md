# Voice samples: electrical

The voice anchor for every electrical script (core `voice-anchoring.md`). These are four stretches of real human narration from the model winners, copied verbatim from the labelled scripts in `references/models/`, with the label lines taken out and nothing else changed: caption slips, missing capitals and missing punctuation are kept as the captions have them. All four are US channels, and all four were picked as the closest the models come to Frederick Barnes: calm, plain and warm, a patient teacher who says "you" for the steps, gives the reason in one line and says the safety part plainly inside the step. Everyday Home Repairs' captions (samples 1 and 2) run without punctuation, so they read as one long line; Top Homeowner and Practical Engineering (samples 3 and 4) are punctuated. All four creators talk over their own hands or footage, so take their sentence shapes and leave the camera behind. Test every sentence of a draft against these. The copy guard applies: never 8 or more words in a row from this file.

**Borrow:** how the sentences are built. That means:
- **The viewer in the job, in the first line:** "you just want to swap out this outlet" (sample 1). The problem arrives as something the viewer notices, not as a topic.
- **"You" for every step and every place:** "you can use either use a non-contact voltage tester" (sample 2), "typically you'll find GFCIs in wet locations like kitchens or bathrooms or even outside" (sample 3).
- **Safety said plainly, as the first thing in the step:** "so first thing when removing this outlet one we want to make sure the power is cut off" (sample 2), and in the right order: the tester shown working on the live outlet, then the breaker, then the tester again.
- **The reason in one line:** "because all of that electricity is running through that body of water" (sample 3); "With nowhere for current to flow, the device could just sit there, indefinitely dangerous, until someone happened to touch it" (sample 4).
- **The "but" turn:** "But, critically, it might not create a short circuit." (sample 4).
- **Reassurance that the viewer did nothing wrong:** "now this is an older home it's been updated numerous times over the years so this is not uncommon" (sample 1).
- **The practical "keep in mind" that saves the viewer a worry:** "So, you don't have to worry about having both in place." (sample 3).
- **The stake once, in proportion, then straight back to what to do:** sample 3 names the electrocution risk in one picture (the hair dryer in the sink) and moves on to where the protection goes.

**Don't borrow:** the content, the facts or the numbers (every fact comes from the brief or the facts guide), and anything that needs a presenter or a camera. In these samples and the videos around them, that means:
- **Pointing at the picture:** "this is what we find" (sample 1), "now we can see that all lights are off" (sample 2), "you can find them in a receptacle format like this here" (sample 3). Our narrator never points at the screen; name the thing instead.
- **On-camera demos and the narrator's own hands:** "i'll mock up a piece of romex here which will give a better example" (sample 1), "and then i have two amber color lights on" (sample 2). In our scripts every step is the viewer's: "you" plug the tester in, "you" read the lights.
- **Links and mid-video asks:** "now both of these links will be down in the description" (sample 2). This channel has no mid-video CTA.
- **The creator's own channel history:** "through many many hundreds of comments through some of my videos" (sample 2). Say it as the typical case.
- **Thin safety:** sample 1's "confirming you don't have any power" is a six-word clause with no breaker and no tester. Ours always says breaker off, then prove it with a tester checked on a live outlet first.
- **Promises nobody can keep:** "so there's no chance or very very little chance of getting electrocuted" (sample 3). No guaranteed outcomes.
- **Codes read as labels:** "The NEMA 1-15 was the classic North American outlet" (sample 4). Say what the thing is; numbers and units are written as words for the voice.
- **Prices:** none in the four stretches, but the videos around them have them: "so this one would be about 50 cents" (E08 [5:03]) and "you're going to spend like two or three dollars" (E22 [8:42]). This channel says no prices at all.
- **Trade bashing and personal stories:** none in the four stretches, but the models around them have both: "this is one that i've seen electricians do because my house was wired in some places like this" (E24 [6:04]). Our narrator trusts electricians, and he never claims a house, a job or a customer.
- **UK wording:** none of the four stretches is UK, but the UK models are close in tone, and their words never transfer: "the consumer unit is the Hub of the home" (E27 [0:52]), "confident and more importantly competent" (E10 [0:41]). Ours says panel, breaker, outlet, ground, hot.
- **Slang and filler:** "man you notice" (sample 1), "a you know," (sample 3). Plain words, no slang.

## Sample 3: Top Homeowner, "AFCI vs GFCI: What's The Difference and Why You Should Care" (E22, 1:01-2:03, 105.75x normal)

So, the advantage this gives us is it helps protect against electrocution. And probably the most common example I can think of for an electrocution scenario would be one in the bathroom. Say you drop a hair dryer into a sink or into a bathtub. Well, that's going to create a you know, a very very high risk of electrocution because all of that electricity is running through that body of water. And a GFCI is designed to detect this and shut power off so there's no chance or very very little chance of getting electrocuted. So, because of this, typically you'll find GFCIs in wet locations like kitchens or bathrooms or even outside. GFCI protection can come in a couple different forms. One, you can find them in a receptacle format like this here, which is pretty typical. You'll see these in bathrooms a lot. And you can also find and install GFCI breakers, which will protect an entire circuit. And one of the things to keep in mind, too, is if you have a GFCI breaker, you don't have to have GFCI receptacles in a kitchen or a bathroom. The breaker will cover the protection for the entire circuit. So, you don't have to worry about having both in place.

## Sample 4: Practical Engineering, "Every US Electrical Outlet Explained" (E14, 3:05-4:27, 1.45x normal)

For example, NEMA 1 receptacles are rated for 125 volts and have just two poles, a hot and a neutral with no ground. The NEMA 1-15 was the classic North American outlet until the 1960s and you still see these in older buildings. Lots of devices made today can still use them, especially low voltage equipment like chargers and critically those without external metal parts. If an energized wire inside the device comes loose and contacts the case, there's still an insulating barrier protecting someone from being shocked. The reason NEMA 1 receptacles are mostly a thing of the past is what could happen when equipment didn't have that protection. If a device with a metal enclosure or exposed metal parts had an energized wire come loose, that metal would be energized, too. But, critically, it might not create a short circuit. With nowhere for current to flow, the device could just sit there, indefinitely dangerous, until someone happened to touch it, allowing current to flow through them to a lower potential. The ground wire we see in nearly all plugs and receptacles today fixes that specific hazard. Bonding exposed conductive elements and connecting them to ground make they somehow become energized, current will flow, a short circuit will form, and protective devices like breakers will activate.

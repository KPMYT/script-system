# Voice samples: electrical

The voice anchor for every electrical script (core `voice-anchoring.md`). These are three stretches of real human narration from three different US channels, each well above its channel's normal, copied verbatim from the labelled scripts in `references/models/`, with the label lines taken out and nothing else changed: caption slips, missing capitals and missing punctuation are kept as the captions have them. All three were picked as the closest the models come to Frederick Barnes: calm, plain and warm, a patient teacher who says "you" for the steps and gives the reason in one line. Everyday Home Repairs' and Silver Cymbal's captions (samples 1 and 2) run without punctuation, so they read as one long line; Top Homeowner (sample 3) is punctuated. All three creators talk over their own hands or footage, so take their sentence shapes and leave the camera behind. Test every sentence of a draft against these. The copy guard applies: never 8 or more words in a row from this file.

**Borrow:** how the sentences are built. That means:
- **The viewer in the job, in the first line:** "so you're jumping into what should be a quick little electrical update project" (sample 1) ("this outlet" in the next words is pointing; name it: "an outlet"). The problem arrives as something the viewer notices, not as a topic.
- **"You" for every step and every place:** "you want to remember that those silver screws will always be connected to the white wire" (sample 2), "typically you'll find GFCIs in wet locations like kitchens or bathrooms or even outside" (sample 3).
- **Something the viewer has looked at a hundred times, seen fresh:** "now when you look at an outlet you might never have noticed the two of the screws on one side are silver" (sample 2).
- **Safety said plainly, as the first thing in the step:** none of the three stretches has a full power-off step (sample 1's is a six-word clause; see Don't borrow). The fullest US sequence is E08 1:23-2:33 (the tester shown working on the live outlet, the breaker, the tester again), but it proves only the outlet's slots: ours also checks the tester on a known live outlet first and proves the wires dead before anything is touched (SKILL "Safety" 1).
- **The reason in one line:** "because all of that electricity is running through that body of water" (sample 3); "now it matters which direction that hook is facing because that screw tightens down screwing clockwise" (sample 2).
- **The "but" turn:** "but many people wire up outlets incorrectly and the outlet still seems to work fine but they've created a reverse polarity condition" (sample 2).
- **Reassurance that the viewer did nothing wrong:** "now this is an older home it's been updated numerous times over the years so this is not uncommon" (sample 1).
- **The practical "keep in mind" that saves the viewer a worry:** "So, you don't have to worry about having both in place." (sample 3).
- **The stake once, in proportion, then straight back to what to do:** sample 3 names the electrocution risk in one picture (the hair dryer in the sink) and moves on to where the protection goes.

**Don't borrow:** the content, the facts or the numbers (every fact comes from the brief or the facts guide), and anything that needs a presenter or a camera. In these samples and the videos around them, that means:
- **Pointing at the picture:** "this is what we find" (sample 1), "use a plug-in tester like this" (sample 2), "you can find them in a receptacle format like this here" (sample 3). Our narrator never points at the screen; name the thing instead.
- **On-camera demos and the narrator's own hands:** "i'll mock up a piece of romex here which will give a better example" (sample 1), "now you might think this looks great but i've done it completely wrong" (sample 2). In our scripts every step is the viewer's: "you" plug the tester in, "you" read the lights.
- **Demonstration promises:** "so let me show you a few different options" (sample 1). The scanner bans "let me show you"; ours says what the viewer will know.
- **Link cards and mid-video asks:** "i talked more about that in the video i've linked in the upper right hand corner" (sample 2). This channel has no mid-video CTA and no cards.
- **The creator's own channel history:** none in the three stretches, but the models around them lean on it: "through many many hundreds of comments through some of my videos" (E08 [1:41]). Say it as the typical case.
- **Thin safety:** sample 1's "confirming you don't have any power" is a six-word clause with no breaker and no tester, and sample 2 wires an outlet with no power-off line at all. Ours always says breaker off, then prove it with a tester checked on a live outlet first.
- **Promises nobody can keep:** "so there's no chance or very very little chance of getting electrocuted" (sample 3), and "always" said of wire colors in a house of any age ("those silver screws will always be connected to the white wire", sample 2). No guaranteed outcomes; old houses can have re-marked wires.
- **Codes read as labels:** none in the three stretches, but the explainer models have them: "The NEMA 1-15 was the classic North American outlet" (E14 [3:05]). Say what the thing is; numbers and units are written as words for the voice.
- **Prices:** none in the three stretches, but the videos around them have them: "so this one would be about 50 cents" (E08 [5:03]) and "you're going to spend like two or three dollars" (E22 [8:42]). This channel says no prices at all.
- **Trade bashing and personal stories:** none in the three stretches, but the models around them have both: "this is one that i've seen electricians do because my house was wired in some places like this" (E24 [6:04]). Our narrator trusts electricians, and he never claims a house, a job or a customer.
- **UK wording:** none of the three stretches is UK, but the UK models are close in tone, and their words never transfer: "the consumer unit is the Hub of the home" (E27 [0:52]), "confident and more importantly competent" (E10 [0:41]). Ours says panel, breaker, outlet, ground, hot.
- **Slang and filler:** "man you notice" (sample 1), "now we should be on easy street" (sample 2), "a you know," (sample 3). Plain words, no slang.

## Sample 1: Everyday Home Repairs, "How To Fix Short Wires In An Electrical Box" (E09, 0:00-1:25, 15.11x normal)

## Sample 2: Silver Cymbal, "BEWARE Of These 3 Common Wiring Mistakes On Outlets & Switches" (E26, 0:10-1:32, 55.74x normal)

the number one mistake people make when wiring up an outlet is they connect the wrong wire to the wrong terminal now when you look at an outlet you might never have noticed the two of the screws on one side are silver and the other two are brass or a kind of goldish color and this is the built-in guide in every us outlet to help you do the job correctly you want to remember that those silver screws will always be connected to the white wire which is also called your neutral wire and those brass or gold screws they're going to always be connected to the black wire which is also called your hot but many people wire up outlets incorrectly and the outlet still seems to work fine but they've created a reverse polarity condition i talked more about that in the video i've linked in the upper right hand corner but i recommend when you're done doing any outlet wiring then use a plug-in tester like this this will confirm that the polarity is correct and your ground wire has also been connected properly now we should be on easy street just strip your wire make a hook and then put it onto the screw on the outlet and tighten it down now you might think this looks great but i've done it completely wrong and this is the second most common mistake i've put the hook on backwards now it matters which direction that hook is facing because that screw tightens down screwing clockwise and you want the end of the hook to be facing to the right and now as i tighten that screw down it's actually going to pull the wire in a little bit tighter and that's going to give you a secure connection if i have that hook facing to the left as i screw it down it's actually going to force the wire out and that can make it slip off

## Sample 3: Top Homeowner, "AFCI vs GFCI: What's The Difference and Why You Should Care" (E22, 1:01-2:03, 105.75x normal)

So, the advantage this gives us is it helps protect against electrocution. And probably the most common example I can think of for an electrocution scenario would be one in the bathroom. Say you drop a hair dryer into a sink or into a bathtub. Well, that's going to create a you know, a very very high risk of electrocution because all of that electricity is running through that body of water. And a GFCI is designed to detect this and shut power off so there's no chance or very very little chance of getting electrocuted. So, because of this, typically you'll find GFCIs in wet locations like kitchens or bathrooms or even outside. GFCI protection can come in a couple different forms. One, you can find them in a receptacle format like this here, which is pretty typical. You'll see these in bathrooms a lot. And you can also find and install GFCI breakers, which will protect an entire circuit. And one of the things to keep in mind, too, is if you have a GFCI breaker, you don't have to have GFCI receptacles in a kitchen or a bathroom. The breaker will cover the protection for the entire circuit. So, you don't have to worry about having both in place.
